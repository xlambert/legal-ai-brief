Your AI Can't See the Yellow Highlighter

A new study finds document converters throw away the formatting lawyers use to flag what matters.

Walk through any law firm and look at the documents sitting on people's screens. You'll see yellow highlighting on the key terms. Red text on the language opposing counsel wants changed. Blue for the new stuff. A partner I worked with years ago had a whole color system for her deal checklists, and woe to the associate who used green for the wrong thing.

Now ask yourself what happens to all that color when an AI tool reads the document.

A paper from Yikai Zhao, Saurabh Pandey and Pradeep Kumar Misra, What the Author Wrote, What the Agent Reads: Silent Cue Loss in Enterprise Document Ingestion, answers that question, and the answer made me wince. Most of it disappears before the model ever sees the file.

What they tested

Before an AI agent can read a Word file or a PDF, some piece of software has to convert it into text. That conversion step is boring plumbing, so nobody looks at it very hard. The authors did.

They took 76 real policy documents and asked agents to find the requirements the author had singled out with formatting. They scored the answers against the formatting stored in the Word file itself, so there was no AI judge involved in the grading.

When the conversion step kept the formatting, the agents scored a micro F1 of 0.69. When they used docling, a popular open-source converter, the score fell to 0.15. Plain text dropped it to 0.075. They saw the same collapse with five different Claude models and with GPT-5.6, so this isn't one model having a bad day.

The really interesting part is which formatting mattered. Bold, italics and strikethrough barely moved the numbers. Highlight and font color carried almost all the loss. And color is the one cue that, according to the authors, no mainstream converter keeps and no parsing benchmark measures.

Why this one worries me

The authors surveyed 6,460 documents and found that about a third used color or highlighting on purpose, and about 10 percent used it to mark an obligation. I would bet law firm documents run higher than that. We highlight everything.

The worst part is how the failure looks. The agent doesn't come back and say, "I think I'm missing something." It guesses, and it guesses with confidence. The docling setup flagged requirements the author never marked at four times the rate of the authors' format-aware approach. So you get answers that look complete, read well, and quietly miss what the drafter wanted you to see.

For anyone responsible for supervising AI use under the professional rules, that's a nasty combination. A lawyer reviewing the output has no visual cue that anything went wrong.

What I'd do with this

First, I'd go ask our vendors and our own IT folks a simple question: when a document goes into Harvey, Copilot, or whatever agent we're piloting, what happens to the highlighting? I suspect a lot of people won't know the answer, and that's useful information in itself.

Second, I'd test it. Take a handful of real documents where somebody highlighted the key obligations, run them through the tools, and ask for the highlighted items. It would take an afternoon. This is the kind of practical check that KM and library folks are very good at, and it doesn't require a data science team.

Third, I'd think about training. If our lawyers know that color might not survive the trip into an AI tool, they can put the important stuff in words. A comment that says "key obligation" travels a lot better than a yellow highlight.

We spend a lot of time arguing about which model is smartest. This paper is a good reminder that the model can only work with what the plumbing hands it. So, does anyone at your firm know what your document pipeline does with the color yellow?
