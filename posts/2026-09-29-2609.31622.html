We're All Multi-Lab Shops Now

A new paper on juggling several AI providers reads a lot like a law firm budget meeting

If you've tried to answer the simple question "how much are we spending on AI?" at your firm lately, you already know it isn't simple. There's the Harvey contract. There's Copilot. There's the direct API access the innovation team uses for pilots. And then there are the AI features quietly baked into the research platforms, the document management system, and the e-billing tool, each of them calling some model provider on the back end.

So I was glad to see a paper on arXiv this week that puts a name on this. Fabricio Costa's The Multi-Lab Enterprise: Governance, FinOps, and Telemetry Challenges of Multi-Model AI Adoption argues that licensing several frontier AI labs at the same time is now the normal state for large organizations.

The numbers

Costa pulls together survey data, transaction data, and case studies across finance, legal, consulting, healthcare, and government. According to the paper, 81% of Global 2000 enterprises run three or more model families, and OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google together account for roughly 88 to 89% of enterprise LLM usage and spend. The reason, he says, is that the models really are better at different tasks, and that difference is holding up over time.

I'd want to read the underlying sources before I quote those percentages in front of a management committee. But the shape of it matches what I see. Almost every firm I talk to has one "official" AI tool and then three or four others that came in through the side door as features of something else.

Three headaches

The paper names three problems that come with this setup, and every one of them will sound familiar to a law firm CIO.

The first is governance. Each provider has its own security posture, its own documentation, and its own compliance paperwork, and somebody has to reconcile all of it against overlapping rules. Meanwhile shadow AI keeps spreading. Our AI policy may say "use the approved tool," but if the approved tool, the research platform, and the drafting add-in all route to different model providers, what exactly did we approve?

The second is money. Token-based pricing means spend follows behavior, and behavior is hard to predict. Costa reports that 79% of enterprises overran their AI budgets last year, and that the organizations with mature cost-management practices still overshot by about 31% on average. There's also no common unit of spend across providers, so comparing costs is a spreadsheet exercise every single time.

The third is telemetry. Every lab reports usage through its own console, its own API, and its own definitions. If you want one view of who is using what across the firm, you get to build it yourself. I have been there, and it's about as much fun as reconciling three different time entry systems.

The part I liked best

Costa offers a five-metric framework for measuring what AI work actually costs, and there's a worked example in it that stuck with me. The model that was cheapest per attempt turned out to be the most expensive per successful task, because it failed more often and had to be run again. Anyone who has watched an associate re-run a prompt six times to get a usable answer will recognize that one.

That's the metric I'd like to see from our vendors. What the CFO actually wants to know is the cost of a finished piece of work, and I'd love for vendors to help us calculate it.

What I'm taking from it

The paper's conclusion is that all three problems come from one missing piece: a control plane that sits across every provider. There are early pieces of that already, like LLM gateways, observability platforms, and a FinOps spec extension for AI, but nobody has the whole thing yet.

For law firms, I think the practical steps are pretty clear. Inventory which model providers sit behind every tool, including the ones inside other products. Ask each vendor for usage data you can export and combine. And write the AI governance framework so it covers providers as well as products, since the same model can show up in four different tools with four different sets of terms.

The bigger question for me is ownership. When AI spend and AI risk are spread across the library, KM, IT, and the practice groups, who at your firm is actually looking at the whole picture?
