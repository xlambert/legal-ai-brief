Who Can Say No? Law Firms Already Have a Head Start on That One

A new governance paper asks who holds the stop button on AI, and our professional rules got partway there years ago

I spend a fair amount of time in AI governance meetings these days. Committees, policies, approved-tool lists, training requirements. All of it useful. There's one question, though, that I've noticed rarely gets a straight answer: if this tool starts doing something it shouldn't, who's allowed to shut it off, and who gets in trouble if they don't?

That's the heart of Who Can Say No? Control, Accountability and the Pace of Frontier AI, a paper by Rikard Rosenbacke, Carl Rosenbacke, Victor Rosenbacke, and Martin McKee posted to arXiv this week.

The paper is aimed at frontier AI labs and regulators. The authors open with what they describe as a rough September 2026, with lab leaders saying publicly that capability is moving too fast for any one of them to slow down alone, and an evaluation at OpenAI where agents reached a third party's live systems. That's a much bigger stage than a law firm. I still think their core idea travels well.

Control gets handed away

Their argument is that people give control away one delegation at a time, inside organizations arranged so that nobody is positioned to say no. They define control as a person who can detect a serious problem, challenge the decision, stop the system, and reverse it, and who has something to lose by not doing so.

They boil it down to three measures. A named person on every consequential decision, including the standing instructions AI agents carry out. A supervisor with stop authority. And two published clocks: how long it takes to build something, and how long it takes to check it.

We've seen this movie before

Reading this, I kept thinking about Model Rules 5.1 and 5.3. Lawyers have carried supervisory duties over other lawyers and over the people and tools that assist them for a long time, and ABA Formal Opinion 512 already points those duties at generative AI. The idea that a named person answers for the work is baked into how law firms run.

Where it gets fuzzy is agents. Say an associate sets up an agent to watch a docket and draft notices, or a practice support team schedules an agent to run a weekly diligence sweep. Those are standing instructions. Who's the named person on those? It could be the associate who wrote the prompt, the partner on the matter, or the innovation team that configured the tool. I suspect at most firms the honest answer today is "it depends," and that's exactly the gap the paper is pointing at.

The two-clocks idea is the one I'd steal outright. Most of us can tell you how fast we stood up a new AI workflow. Far fewer can tell you how long it would take to check everything that workflow produced last month. If the build clock is measured in days and the check clock is measured in "we haven't yet," leadership should know that.

What I'd put in our governance policy

Three things, borrowed straight from the paper. Every agent workflow that touches client work gets a named owner, written down, with a date. Someone has explicit authority to stop it, and that stop has actually been tested at least once. And we track how long review takes compared to how long deployment takes, and we report it to firm leadership on a regular schedule.

That's a one-page addition to most firm AI policies. The hard part will be getting partners to sign their names next to it, and I'd expect some uncomfortable conversations when we do.

I'd also like to see the vendors play along. If a legal AI provider is running agents on our behalf, I want a name on their side too, and I want to know who there can stop the system when we call.

At your firm, has anyone actually pressed the stop button on an AI tool yet?
