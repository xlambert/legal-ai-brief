Twenty-Five Minutes for a Five-Year VAT Reconstruction

A new benchmark tests the investigative side of knowledge work, and the agent harness matters more than I expected

Most of the AI benchmarks I see for legal and professional work are generative. Draft a memo. Build a spreadsheet from a brief. Then a rubric, or another model, decides whether the output is any good. That's useful, and it still leaves out a big chunk of what our tax, investigations, and litigation support folks actually do all day.

That's why kw-bench: Breaking Frontier Harnesses on Real-World Tasks by George Juraj Salapa caught my eye this morning.

Finding the answer that already exists

Salapa describes the expensive part of knowledge work as investigative. In audit, tax, due diligence, regulatory reporting, or any dispute over numbers, the answer is already out there. It's buried in incomplete and inconsistent records spread across systems, and somebody has to dig it out and defend it to the cent.

The first task in the benchmark is a doozy. Reconstruct five years of VAT across three generations of SAP: a legacy ECC system, a partial S/4HANA migration, and a consultant's redesign. The agent gets raw system extracts with page headers, local number formats, partial link tables, and no documentation of how anything connects. If you've ever been handed a client's data dump the week before a deadline, you know the feeling.

The grading is deterministic, with no LLM judge. The reconstructed figures either match the known answer or they don't.

The harness turned out to be the story

A harness is everything wrapped around the model: the loop, the shell, the file system, the ability to write and run code. Salapa ran four of them (Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, and micro-cc) on the same mid-tier model, DeepSeek v4.1 Flash, for two hours each.

The two independent, model-agnostic harnesses scored 0.542 and 0.528. The two harnesses built by AI labs, running away from their home model, scored zero. According to the paper, what beat them was keeping a lot of weak signals alive over a long session.

Then he ran the task on a frontier model, Claude Opus 5.5. The two harnesses tested there scored 0.9986 and 1.000, in 22 and 25 minutes.

What I'd take from this

The first lesson is for procurement. When a vendor demos an agent, we usually ask which model is underneath. This paper says we should also ask about the harness, and whether the vendor has tested it with the model we'll actually be running. On the same model, swapping the harness moved the score from zero to about half. That's a big swing to leave to chance.

The second lesson is about pricing. Salapa's own framing is that a weeks-long engagement now costs minutes and a few dollars. I'd want to see it replicated across more task families before I'd take that to a pricing committee. If even part of it holds, though, the economics of hourly forensic accounting and tax reconstruction work are going to get uncomfortable fast. Fixed-fee and AFA conversations will look different when the client's own team can run the same agent.

The third lesson is about how we evaluate tools internally. I really like the deterministic grading here. For anything that touches numbers, I want a test with a known right answer. If we're building evaluation sets for our own tools, this is the approach to copy.

A few cautions

This is a single-author paper with one task family so far, built from engagements that specialists described to the author. It hasn't been independently replicated. A near-perfect score on a reconstruction task also still needs someone who understands VAT to check and sign off before the work goes to a client or a tax authority.

Even so, it's the most realistic test of investigative professional work I've seen in a while, and I'm eager to see what task family comes next.

If a five-year reconstruction now takes 25 minutes, how are we going to explain the bill for the old way?
