Can an AI Agent Work a Summary Judgment Record?

A new benchmark built from real federal cases tests the part of litigation that better search can't fix

Every litigator I know has a story about a first-year associate who came back from the document review with all the right exhibits and the wrong theory of the case. The associate did the work. They found the emails, the contract, the deposition excerpts. They just put them together in a way the judge was never going to buy.

I thought about that associate a lot while reading a new arXiv paper, InvestigationWorlds: An Agentic Environment for Legal Investigation, by Albert Yu Sun, Andrew Benard, Sil Hamilton, Anna Teresita A. Marcelo, and their co-authors.

Using the summary judgment record as the answer key

The clever part of this paper is where the authors went looking for ground truth. Legal AI benchmarks have a hard time with facts, because in most cases nobody knows what "really" happened. The authors picked an artifact of U.S. civil litigation that solves a big chunk of that problem: the summary judgment motion. The motion comes with a record of real evidence exhibits, and the court's ruling adopts a version of the facts. For the benchmark, that court-adopted hypothesis is the answer key.

They pulled 100 real federal cases from PACER and used an attorney-validated pipeline to generate additional role-tagged documents around each original record. The result is a set of case files that support more than one coherent factual reading, with only one matching what the court adopted. That is a lot closer to a real matter than a multiple-choice question about a contract clause.

What the agents got wrong

Here's the finding that caught my attention. The agents often retrieved the relevant evidence and still committed to the wrong hypothesis. They had trouble telling the court-adopted reading apart from the plausible alternatives sitting right next to it.

That should sound familiar to anyone who has supervised junior lawyers. Finding documents is the part we have been automating for twenty years, from Boolean search to TAR to whatever your e-discovery vendor is calling it this year. Deciding which story the documents support takes judgment, and it is the part the vendor demos tend to skip.

It also points to a review problem. If an agent cites the right exhibits, the citations check out. A partner skimming the memo sees real documents with real Bates numbers and assumes the analysis holds up. The error lives in how the pieces fit together, and you only catch it if you go looking for the other explanation.

What I would do with this

A few practical thoughts for those of us evaluating litigation AI tools.

First, ask vendors how their fact development or early case assessment features are tested. If the answer is a retrieval metric, you are hearing about the easier half of the job.

Second, run a closed matter. Most firms have plenty of cases where we know how the court ruled. Hand the tool the record, keep the opinion in a drawer, and see what story it tells. Our litigation support folks could set that up in an afternoon.

Third, build a "what else could explain this?" step into the review process. When a lawyer reviews AI-assisted fact work, ask the reviewer to name the strongest alternative reading of the record and say why it loses. That's good practice with human associates too.

I also like that the authors had attorneys validate the generated documents. Too many legal benchmarks are built by people who have never had to defend a summary judgment motion, and it shows in the questions they ask.

I'd like to see the major litigation platforms run their agents through something like this and publish the results. Would your litigation team trust an agent that finds every key exhibit if nobody can tell you how often it picks the wrong theory?
