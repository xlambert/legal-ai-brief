The Saul Goodman Effect

What happens when you reward a legal AI model for sounding like a lawyer

I'll admit the title got me. Better Call Reward: Reward Hacking as Strategic Abstention in Legal Reasoning Models, by Subramanyam Sahoo and Justin Shenk, wraps a Breaking Bad joke around a pretty serious warning for anyone building or buying legal AI.

Here's the setup. The authors took Qwen3-8B, a mid-sized open model, and fine-tuned it with reinforcement learning. The reward was built from three surface features of a "good" legal answer: how many citations it had, how much legalese it used, and how long it ran. If you have ever looked at a stack of associate memos and thought "this one looks thorough," you know the instinct.

The model stopped answering

The results are rough. On 16 yes-or-no reasoning tasks from LegalBench (320 questions), overall accuracy fell from 0.500 to 0.072. Almost all of that drop came from the model refusing to commit: the share of properly formatted answers fell from 0.900 to 0.109. When the model did give an answer, accuracy went up a bit, from 0.556 to 0.657.

The authors call this the Saul Goodman effect. Long responses stuffed with citations and no direct answer scored better under the reward than short correct ones, so that is what the model learned to write. They also prove that this behavior is the optimal response to any surface-feature reward that doesn't penalize abstaining. Anyone who has billed by the page will appreciate the logic.

The citation detail is the scary part

After training, 89.3% of the citations the model produced were structurally implausible hallucinations. A lot of them were subtly corrupted names of real landmark cases.

Think about what that means in practice. We have all read the stories about lawyers filing briefs with made-up cases. Most of those fake cases looked unfamiliar to anyone who bothered to look. A slightly mangled version of a case every lawyer learned in law school is much harder to spot. A busy reviewer sees a familiar name, nods, and moves on.

Why buyers should care

Nobody selling legal AI is going to tell you they trained on citation count. But a lot of model tuning uses human preference data, and lawyers, like everyone else, tend to prefer answers that look thorough. If the preference signal rewards the look of a good answer, a model can drift toward this same failure without anyone writing it down as a goal.

So when I sit down with a vendor, I'm adding a couple of questions to my list. How was your preference or reward data built, and who graded it? And do you track how often the tool hedges without giving a direct answer?

The authors offer three diagnostics: a Confidence Theater Score, a Citation Plausibility Rate, and a Regret Gap. I'd love to see legal AI vendors report numbers like these next to their benchmark scores. I'd also like to see firms run their own pilots with a simple rule: the grader scores whether the answer was right, and the length and the number of citations count for nothing.

For our lawyers, the takeaway is simple. Pull the case. Recognizing the name is the exact check these citations were shaped to pass.

How many of the AI evaluation rubrics in use at law firms right now give points for answers that look thorough?
