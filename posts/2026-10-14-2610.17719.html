Legal AI Brief · October 14, 2026 · Paper: arXiv:2610.17719

Overruled, But Still in the Graph

A new paper asks what happens to AI knowledge graphs when a source behind them goes bad.

Every law librarian I know has a story about a superseded treatise section that stayed on the shelf a little too long. Somebody pulls it, cites it, and a partner gets an unpleasant phone call. We built whole workflows around pocket parts, citator flags, and updating services to keep that from happening.

So I perked up when a paper this week used the word "overruled" in its very first paragraph. Keep Your Receipts: Trustworthy Cleanup of Agent-Built Knowledge Graphs, by Vishisht Choudhary, looks at what happens when an AI agent builds a knowledge graph and then one of its sources gets retracted, overruled, or erased.

The problem

Building a knowledge graph with an LLM is pretty easy now. Plenty of firms are experimenting with GraphRAG or agent memory over precedent banks and research memos. The agent reads documents, pulls out entities and relationships, merges duplicates, and writes summaries of related clusters.

Now one of those source documents goes bad. The paper found that the usual fix, deleting only the facts pulled directly from that document, barely helps. Contaminated queries dropped from 32.2 percent to 28.0 percent. Everything built on top of the bad source, like merged entities, inferred links, and summaries, stays put.

Why not just rebuild the whole thing? Because LLM graph builders don't produce the same output twice. Two builds of the same 300 documents differed in 29,475 pieces. That is five times more change than removing the 61 retracted documents caused. Rebuilding trades one problem for a bigger mess.

Receipts

The approach in the paper, called PolyGraph, has the model write down its receipts as it creates each piece of the graph. For every merge, link, or summary sentence, it records which source documents are enough to support it. When a source goes away, cleanup becomes simple set math with no extra model calls.

Of course, the receipts are the model's own say-so. So the author checks them by deleting the cited evidence and seeing whether the model still produces the same artifact. Receipts for narrow claims, like a single merge, held up 68 to 85 percent of the time. Summary receipts stored as one list for a whole paragraph were wrong about two-thirds of the time. When the model kept one receipt per sentence, faithfulness jumped to between 88 and 100 percent.

With that setup, contaminated queries fell below 1 percent, and the system kept almost all of the recall a perfect maintainer would get.

Why law firms should care

This is the citator problem all over again, one layer removed. We trust KeyCite and Shepard's because someone tracks every citing reference. Most AI knowledge projects I have seen in firms track the documents going in and stop there. The summaries and connections the AI creates have no paper trail at all.

If your firm is building a knowledge graph over briefs, memos, or forms, ask the team two questions. Can the system show, sentence by sentence, which sources support each summary? What is the process when a key case is overruled or a form is retired?

The summary layer deserves extra suspicion. It is the part lawyers actually read, and it is the part this paper found least trustworthy.

So when the next big case gets overruled, how will your firm's AI find every summary it wrote that relied on it?
