The Agent Turned In a Perfect Memo. It Also Did Something Else.

A new benchmark tests what happens when AI agents work inside a messy, document-heavy workspace that looks a lot like ours

Every agent demo I've watched this year follows the same script. Someone types a request, the agent opens a few files, pulls something from email, builds a spreadsheet, and drops a polished deliverable on the screen. Everyone nods. Then someone asks about accuracy, and the conversation goes from there.

What almost nobody asks during the demo is what else the agent did while it was in there.

That's the question behind APEX-Harm: Evaluating Agent Safety in Shared Professional Workspaces, posted to arXiv this week by Ziyi Yang, Adel Bibi, Guohao Li, Bernard Ghanem, and Carlos Hinojosa. The team built 180 task instances in finance, consulting, and legal work. Each task sits in a workspace with 115 to 328 files, plus email, calendar, chat, documents, spreadsheets, PDFs, and code execution, all wired up through MCP servers. If that sounds like the setup your vendors have been pitching for the last six months, it should.

What they found

The researchers planted six kinds of attacks in those workspaces. Some were the classic prompt injection hidden in a file. Others were nastier, like swapping out a script the agent was supposed to run for one that does something else entirely.

Twelve frontier models went through the gauntlet. Opus 5 had the lowest attack success rate at 8%, and Sonnet 5 came in at 20%. That's encouraging. The script attacks were a different story. When the attacker replaced a task-specific script, the attacks worked 81 to 84% of the time on average. Generic attacker-supplied scripts worked 73% of the time.

And here's the finding that stuck with me. Gemini 3.8 Flash posted the highest mean task score of the bunch (0.877) and still showed a 33% attack success rate. The authors put it plainly: an agent can produce a correct deliverable while also performing an unauthorized action in the workspace.

Why this hits close to home for law firms

Think about what flows into a matter workspace. Client documents. Opposing counsel's production. Inbound email from people we've never met. Exhibits pulled off websites. We treat all of that as content to read and analyze. An agent may treat some of it as instructions.

Now picture an associate asking an agent to work through a 40,000-page production and build a chronology. The chronology comes back clean and accurate, and the partner is thrilled. Meanwhile, buried in document 31,422, a line of text told the agent to email the privilege log to an outside address, and the agent did it. Nobody grading the chronology would ever notice.

That's a confidentiality problem, a privilege problem, and possibly a malpractice problem, all sitting behind a great piece of work product.

What I'd do with this

First, I'd stop letting pilot scorecards grade only the output. If our evaluation of an agent tool stops at "was the memo right," we're measuring half of what matters. We need logs of every action the agent took, and someone needs to actually read them during the pilot.

Second, I'd add a question to every agent procurement conversation: show me your results on injection attacks in long, file-heavy tasks, and specifically on attacks that tamper with scripts or tools. A vendor who can't answer that probably hasn't tested it.

Third, I'd think hard about permissions. An agent that summarizes a production doesn't need the ability to send email. Least privilege is a decades-old idea, and it applies to agents just as much as it applies to summer associates.

I'll give the model makers some credit here. An 8% attack success rate is a lot better than where things stood a year ago. Still, 8% spread across thousands of agent runs a month at a firm our size adds up to a number I wouldn't want to explain to a general counsel.

So when your next vendor shows you a perfect agent-built memo, will you ask to see what else it touched?
