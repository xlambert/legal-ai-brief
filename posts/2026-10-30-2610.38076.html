Legal AI Brief · October 30, 2026 · Paper: arXiv:2610.38076

Winning the Prompt Lottery

Reword the grading prompt and the AI benchmark winner can change. Here's what that means for legal AI bake-offs.

Anyone who sat through a law school curve knows the grader matters. Same exam, different professor, different grade. We all accepted it because professors are human.

So here's what got my attention this week. A new paper, Prompt Lottery in LLM-as-a-Judge: Measuring and Mitigating Sensitivity to Syntactic Rephrasing, by Ilija Subasic, Brett Levert, Andrew Rabinovich, and colleagues, shows that AI graders have the same problem, and a much worse case of it.

A quick primer on AI judges

If you've read a legal AI benchmark in the last year, there's a good chance an AI model did the grading. It's called LLM-as-a-judge. You give a model the question, the answer, and a grading prompt, and it scores the answer. It's cheap, it's fast, and it scales in a way that hiring a room full of contract attorneys to grade answers never will.

The assumption baked into all of this is that the grading prompt is a stable measuring tool. Write it once and use it everywhere.

What the researchers found

The authors took validated grading prompts and rewrote them. Same meaning, same instructions, different wording. Think of it as asking the same question two ways.

The verdicts changed. In some settings, the model that came out on top flipped. They also ran a user study with ten human annotators, and the AI judges lost six to nine times more agreement under rewording than the humans did. That pushed the AI judges below the agreement level usually treated as acceptable in usability research.

The good news is that the fix is simple. When they ran several versions of the grading prompt and combined the results, giving more weight to judgments that held steady across versions, things got much more stable.

Why law firms should care

I see a lot of vendor slides with bar charts showing their product beating the competition on some legal benchmark. I've also seen internal bake-offs where a firm used an AI model to grade answers from two or three tools because nobody had time to grade 500 answers by hand. I've done it myself.

If rewording the grading prompt can reverse the ranking, a single-prompt comparison is a pretty thin basis for a six-figure procurement decision. It's also a thin basis for the line in your AI governance file that explains why the firm approved the tool.

What I'd change

For vendors: tell me how many grading prompts you used. If the answer is one, I'm going to discount the chart.

For our own evaluations: write at least three versions of the grading prompt and report the spread along with the score. If the winner changes depending on the wording, that's an important finding by itself, and it probably means the tools are closer than the chart suggests.

For anyone grading legal work with AI: keep a human sample in the loop. The paper's user study is a good reminder that the humans were far more consistent than the AI judges on this kind of rewording.

I still want AI graders in the toolbox. There's no other practical way to handle the volume of testing these tools need. I just want them checked like any other measuring instrument before we trust the reading.

So the question I'll leave you with: how many of the legal AI rankings you've seen this year would still hold up if the grader had to use three different prompts?
