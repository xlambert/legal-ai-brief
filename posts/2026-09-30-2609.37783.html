Is That Dashcam Photo Real? The Detectors Aren't Ready to Tell You

A new benchmark of courtroom-style images shows how far AI manipulation detection has to go before litigators can lean on it

A few years ago, if someone told me a photo exhibit had been doctored, I'd picture a forensic expert with a very expensive license for some very expensive software. Today I picture a bored teenager with a phone and a prompt box. That shift has been bugging me for a while, and a paper that showed up on arXiv this week puts some real numbers behind my worry.

The paper is A Benchmark & Dataset for Detecting AI-Manipulated Visual Evidence in the Court System, from Kelly McConvey and colleagues. What I like about it right away is that the authors built their test set around the kinds of pictures that actually land in front of a judge: surveillance frames, dashcam stills, and the random phone photos people take after a fender bender.

What they built

The corpus has 1,505 images. 720 are authentic controls, and 785 were altered or made from scratch using current generative tools, the kind anyone can use. The manipulations come in three flavors. Some change the conditions of the scene, like lighting or weather. Some make a localized edit to one element in the frame. And some are full fabrications. Every image comes with metadata on where it came from, how it was changed, which generator was used, and what prompt produced it.

Then the authors ran publicly available manipulation detectors against the whole set. Their summary is that the error profiles 'remain problematic for evidentiary use.' That's polite academic language. What it means for those of us on the law firm side is that you shouldn't walk into a hearing with a detector score as your whole argument.

The localized edit is the one that worries me

I think the quiet, localized edits are the bigger risk here. Think about a photo of an intersection where the only thing that changed is the color of the traffic light. Or a warehouse shot where one pallet got moved six feet to the left. Almost every pixel is original, and a detector looking at the whole frame may never notice the one change that decides the case.

It reminds me of the old days of redlining contracts by hand. What caused the trouble was always the single word changed on page 43 that nobody flagged.

What I'd tell our litigators

First, treat detection tools as one input into an expert's opinion. They're handy for triaging a large production of images, and based on this benchmark they aren't strong enough to carry authentication on their own.

Second, start asking about provenance at intake. Where did the client get this photo? Is the original file still on the device? Has it been sent through a messaging app that strips the metadata? Those questions are a lot easier to answer in week one than in month nine.

Third, if you're evaluating a vendor that says it can spot AI-manipulated evidence, ask how it does on court-style imagery specifically. A tool tuned on face swaps may have very little to say about a dashcam frame. The good news is that this dataset is public, along with the prompts and the evaluation scripts, so a vendor that's serious can test against it.

Where this goes next

The rules of evidence folks have been debating how to handle machine-generated and AI-altered exhibits for a couple of years now, and I don't expect a clean answer any time soon. In the meantime, the practical burden falls on the lawyers and the experts they hire. Papers like this one help, because they give everyone a common, public yardstick to test against.

I'd love to hear from litigators who have already had a photo exhibit challenged as AI-altered. What did the court want to see before it was satisfied?
