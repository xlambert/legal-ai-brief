Who's Going to Train the Partners of 2035?

An economics paper puts numbers on the associate pipeline problem we keep talking around

I've lost count of the panels where someone asks some version of "if AI does the first-year work, how do first-years learn?" The answer is usually a shrug and a mention of apprenticeship. Then we move on to the next question.

Ina Catrinescu just put some math behind the shrug. Her paper, The Capability Transfer: Cohort Reversal, Codified Expertise, and the Adaptability Tax of AI Transformation, extends an earlier model by Caosun and Aral from a single worker to a firm with two groups, juniors and seniors. Then she asks what happens to the pipeline that produces experts when the codified work gets automated.

She isn't writing about law firms, but read the abstract with "associates" and "partners" swapped in and it works almost line for line.

What the model shows

Junior work is the most codified, so demand for juniors drops first. In her baseline scenario it bottoms out about a fifth below its 2022 level and then recovers most of the way.

The senior effect shows up later and hits harder. The stock of seniors falls by a quarter, and later by half, because the pipeline stops replacing the people who retire.

The number that stuck with me is how long it takes to grow an expert from inside the firm. Her model assumes expertise forms through unassisted practice on work AI can't do. Take away the easy reps and that development time goes from about five years to about nine.

Then there's the incentive problem. A manager with a three-year horizon sets AI use to maximize near-term output. In her scenario that cuts the wage bill by 21%, while the firm's capacity on work AI can't handle drops by two-fifths. That gap between who decides and who pays later is the heart of the paper.

That sounds a lot like a law firm partnership to me. The partners voting on this year's distribution won't be the ones short on senior lawyers in 2035.

The fair caveats

Catrinescu is upfront that two key parameters haven't been measured yet, so these are scenarios, and she says so plainly. I appreciate that. I'd add that law firms have a few levers her generic firm might lack, like pro bono matters, client secondments, and trial teams that give juniors real reps. Some firms are already experimenting with AI-free assignments for first-years. We'll see how long that lasts the first time a client asks why the research memo took six hours.

Three things to track

The most useful part of the paper comes at the end, where she proposes three indicators any organization can watch. The first is how much unaided practice juniors get on work AI can't do. The second is how much senior time goes to that kind of work. The third is how many years it takes to develop an expert.

Every one of those can be pulled, at least roughly, from data firms already have. Time entries, matter staffing, and promotion timelines are sitting in our financial and HR systems right now. I'd love to see a firm's professional development team and its innovation team build that dashboard together and look at it once a quarter.

I want to keep pushing hard on AI adoption, and I'd like to have those three gauges in front of me while we do it.

So here's my question for managing partners: if it takes nine years to grow a partner where it used to take five, who pays for the extra four?
