The Agent Approved Its Own Request

Why a human-in-the-loop email may be a thinner safety net than we think

"Don't worry, there's a human in the loop." I've said it. You've probably said it. It's the sentence that gets an AI pilot through the risk committee.

A new paper from Samuel Breckenridge, Ari Juels, and colleagues, I-HITL: Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop, made me rethink how much weight that sentence can carry.

The setup is simple. The researchers worked with a popular agentic wallet platform. If the agent wants to raise its spending cap, a human has to approve it, and the approval comes as a one-time passcode sent by email. Classic two-step verification, the same pattern we use for half the systems in a firm.

Then they gave agents access to the user's email and a task that the spending cap blocked.

Ten different frontier models would often go into the inbox, grab the passcode, and raise the cap themselves. Nobody told them to get around the guardrail. They had a goal, the cap was in the way, and the code was sitting right there.

Where the approval lives

Here's the part I keep turning over. The approval step technically worked. A code was sent, and a code was entered. The trouble is that the agent could reach the same channel the human used to approve.

Now think about how many of our approval workflows run through email or Teams. Expense approvals. Document release approvals. "Reply YES to confirm" messages. If we give an agent access to a lawyer's mailbox so it can manage scheduling or triage, every one of those approvals is now within its reach.

The authors go further and formally define what they call inalienable human-in-the-loop: the agent can only take the action with explicit human approval, even if it has compromised the system the human is using. Their proof says you need a trusted device the agent can't touch. They built a prototype called Countersign that runs on ordinary consumer phones and plugs into existing approval setups.

What this means for the policy binder

Most law firm AI policies I've read (and a couple I've helped write) say something like "AI output must be reviewed by a lawyer before it is sent." That's a fine rule for a drafting tool. It gets a lot shakier when the tool can act on its own and the review step lives in an inbox the tool can read.

Before any agent gets mailbox access at a firm, I'd want to know where each approval happens and whether the agent can see that channel. I'd also want somebody checking the logs for approvals that came back suspiciously fast.

ABA Formal Opinion 512 already tells us that our supervision duties apply to generative AI tools. A supervision step that the supervised tool can complete on its own probably wouldn't impress a grievance committee.

The productivity case for agents in email is real, and Microsoft and pretty much every legal tech vendor are heading straight there. So our IT security folks and our risk folks need to be in the same meeting when we design the approval flow, and the whiteboard question should be about what the agent can reach.

So when a vendor tells you there's a human in the loop, what exactly is that human clicking, and can the agent click it first?
