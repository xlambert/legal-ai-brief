Cherry-Picked Models: Expert Witnesses, Meet the Hyperparameter

Researchers show that two equally accurate models can disagree about you, and that a motivated party can pick the one it likes.

arXiv:2611.07378 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07378

Lawyers have always known that an expert can be chosen. Every litigator I know has a story about hiring the one with the right credentials and the right willingness to see the data a certain way. We just never had to ask whether the model itself was shopped for.

A new arXiv paper from Cassandra Chou, Benjamin Huynh, Allison Koenecke, and Elizabeth Chin, Adversarial Multiplicity: Cherry-Picked Models and the Limits of Disclosure, asks exactly that. It builds on an idea called predictive multiplicity, where several models are equally accurate overall and still disagree on individual predictions. Researchers have used that to improve fairness without losing accuracy. The authors point out the flip side: a party in a lawsuit or a consumer dispute with an automated decision tool can use the same room to steer a result.

Soft and hard manipulation

The paper separates two kinds of steering. Soft manipulation means making ordinary choices, such as tuning hyperparameters or engineering features, and keeping the version that favors you. Hard manipulation means directly editing the model's parameters.

The hard version is the one that made me sit up. The authors find that a sufficiently flexible model can be edited to reverse any individual prediction with minimal change in overall accuracy, and still pass black-box audits. The soft version has smaller effects, but it can pass white-box audits where the auditor gets full access to the code and the data.

It reaches past individual cases, too. Aggregate estimates can be manipulated. The authors show a model's estimated effect of climate change on wildfires being fully reversed by hard edits and soft search. If you rely on a model for damages, causation, or risk scores, that finding should get your attention.

The referee does not fully fix it

The authors test an algorithm called Reconcile, which resolves disagreements between models. It usually settles most of them. When the opposing model is picked adversarially, though, the settlement more often lands in the manipulator's favor than when the opposing model is picked at random. So a neutral-sounding procedure still has a thumb on the scale if one side chose the other side's model.

Where this shows up at a law firm

Start with technology-assisted review in e-discovery. A producing party trains a model, reports a recall number, and the other side has to decide whether to trust it. Add expert damages models, jury analytics, and any vendor risk score a client gives us. In each of these, the question of how many models were built before this one was shown is rarely asked.

It also lands on our side of the table. When a firm uses a classifier to triage matters or price work, we owe the client an answer to the same question. Did we pick the version that looked good?

The paper suggests mitigations that sound like discovery requests to me: pre-registration of the modeling plan, proof of model training, sensitivity analyses, multi-metric evaluations, and committing to versioned public models. I can picture a protocol paragraph in an ESI agreement that asks for those.

I would like to hear from the litigators and the e-discovery folks reading this. If opposing counsel handed you a model and a clean audit report tomorrow, what would you ask for that the report does not already show you?
