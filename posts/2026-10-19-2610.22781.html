How Would You Know If Your AI Vendor Downgraded You?

A new paper on contracts for AI services gives law firm buyers a better set of questions.

Every few months somebody on a legal tech listserv posts the same complaint. "Is it just me, or did this tool get dumber this week?" A bunch of people reply that they noticed it too. A few say it's all in our heads. Then the thread dies, because nobody has any way to prove it either way.

A new paper from Boris Velasevic, Eden Saig, and Eric Mazumdar, "Anytime Contracts for AI Services", takes that complaint seriously. Their starting point is simple. An AI provider can quietly route your requests to a cheaper, weaker model. Since model output varies from one run to the next anyway, it's very hard for a customer to show that anything changed. Economists call this moral hazard, and there's a whole branch of contract theory built to deal with it.

The catch is that the classic contract designs assume a deal with a fixed length. That's not how we buy AI. We buy subscriptions and pay-as-you-go API access, and either side can walk away whenever it wants. The authors show that this flexibility breaks the standard designs and opens them up to abuse by providers and by users. Their answer ties payment to statistical tests that stay valid no matter when you stop looking (anytime-valid tests built on likelihood-ratio e-values, for the stats folks). They give an algorithm to compute these contracts and test it on synthetic data and on data from deployed LLMs.

I'll be honest. I'm not going to walk into a negotiation with an e-value formula. But the idea behind it is something every law firm buyer should steal.

The question behind the math

Think about how most AI agreements read today. We pay by the seat or by the token. The vendor promises a product with a name. Maybe there's a vague reference to "the latest models." Very few agreements tell you which model answered a specific request, and fewer still give you a way to check.

Now imagine your firm's research assistant gets switched to a cheaper model on a Tuesday because the vendor's compute bill was getting ugly. Would your lawyers notice? Some might grumble by Thursday. Would anyone be able to point to evidence?

That's the real value of this paper for us. It turns a gut feeling into a list of things we can actually ask for.

What I'd put on the list

First, model-version disclosure. If the vendor changes the underlying model, we should hear about it, and the logs should show which model handled what.

Second, a fixed test set the firm owns. Keep a few dozen questions with known good answers from your own practice areas, and run them on a schedule. Nothing fancy here. It's the paper's continuous monitoring idea, done with a spreadsheet and some patience. If scores drop, you have something concrete to bring to the vendor.

Third, contract terms that connect to that monitoring. That might be an audit right, a service credit, or an easy exit if measured quality falls below an agreed level. The paper makes a point worth hearing here: exit rights cut both ways, and a poorly designed one can be gamed by the customer too. Vendors have legitimate worries, and a good contract should hold up for both sides.

The governance piece

There's a governance issue here as well. Clients are asking more questions about which AI tools touch their matters, and some outside counsel guidelines now ask for that detail. If a vendor quietly swaps the model, the firm could be making representations to clients that are no longer accurate, without anyone at the firm knowing. That's a risk-management problem, and it lands on whoever signed the deal.

I'd love to see the legal AI vendors get ahead of this by publishing model change logs and giving customers a way to verify quality over time. The good ones probably will.

So here's my question for the CIOs and procurement folks reading this: if your main AI vendor swapped in a cheaper model tomorrow, what in your contract would tell you, and what would let you do anything about it?
