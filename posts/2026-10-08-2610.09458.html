Your AI Just Gave You Ohio's Number for a Texas Question

A new study of state-by-state recall shows why 50-state surveys need their own kind of check

Anyone who has spent time around a law library has done a 50-state survey, or watched an associate disappear for a week doing one. The tedious part was always making sure the number you found actually belonged to the state you were writing about, came from the right year, and used the definition you thought it used.

That's why a new paper on arXiv caught my eye this week. Jiayu Feng's Right Number, Wrong State? Measuring Cross-Jurisdiction Substitution in LLM Recall of State Policy asks a simple question: when a large language model gets a state-specific figure wrong, is it making something up, or is it handing you a real number that belongs to some other state?

For Medicaid income-eligibility figures, at least, the second thing happens more often than you'd want.

How the test worked

Feng kept the question wording identical and changed only the jurisdiction, running through all 50 states plus D.C. for three precisely defined Medicaid eligibility figures. The gold values came from an official data book, and Feng checked them against a second source (they agreed on 101 of 102 cells). The protocol was pre-registered, which I appreciate more every time I read one of these papers.

Claude Sonnet 5.5 reproducibly gave another state's current value on 10 of 153 items. GPT-5.6 Sol did it on 25. And reproducibly is the key word here. They gave the same wrong answer on two independent runs.

Think about what that means for anyone who has been told to just run it twice and compare. That trick does nothing here. The model is consistent. It's consistently giving you Ohio.

Why the wrong-state answer worries me

We've spent a couple of years training lawyers to watch for hallucinated citations. A wrong-state number is harder to catch because everything about it checks out except the one thing that matters. It's a real figure from an official source, and it sits comfortably in a Texas memo looking exactly like a Texas number.

A reviewer checking whether the figure is real will say yes and move on. Catching this error means asking whether it is Texas's figure, and that means going back to Texas's own sources.

The part I didn't expect

Feng also found that measuring this problem is tricky. If you count every wrong answer that happens to match some other state's value as a cross-state substitution, you overcount by three to five times. Many of those apparent wrong-state answers were really the asked state's own number from an earlier year or under a different convention.

That's a lesson for us too. When a vendor tells me their tool is 95 percent accurate on state law questions, I want to know how they decided what counted as wrong. And when a firm runs its own pilot, the person grading the answers needs a complete reference set for each jurisdiction, or the results will say more about the grader than the tool.

What I'd do with this

A few practical things come to mind. First, jurisdiction verification should be its own step in any AI-assisted survey workflow, with each figure tied to the asked state's own source. Second, research librarians are exactly the right people to own that step, since it's what they've been doing with print and online surveys for decades. Third, if you're evaluating a research or compliance tool that promises multi-jurisdiction answers, ask the vendor how they test for this specific error.

Medicaid figures are a narrow test, and the paper is careful to say so. Still, I'd expect the same pattern with statutes of limitation, filing fees, notice periods, and every other number that changes at the state line.

So who at your firm checks that the number in the survey actually belongs to the state at the top of the column?
