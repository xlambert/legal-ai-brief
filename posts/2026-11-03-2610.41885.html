What Your Agent's Tool Calls Say About You

I've been connecting a lot of things to Claude lately through MCP. Calendar, mail, a couple of internal tools. It's been great. It also means a steady stream of little requests is flying back and forth between the model and those servers, and I'll admit I had never given much thought to who else can see them.

A paper this week from Donghang Duan, Dongrui Liu and colleagues made me think about it. They asked a simple question: if all you can see is the tool calls an agent makes (only the protocol traffic, with no access to the conversation), how much can you figure out about the user?

They built a benchmark called PrivacyTrace with 1,000 synthetic user profiles and 4,000 agent trajectories across four domains, using real MCP tools from 25 servers. Then they let four mainstream LLMs play the role of the snoop. Working only from tool-call traces, the attackers inferred profile attributes about 26% of the time, compared to about 18% from educated guessing. The tool arguments and the returned results carried most of the signal. And when the attacker could link traces across sessions, the profiling got better.

Twenty-six percent might not sound scary. Think about it in law firm terms, though. A tool call that searches a docket for a specific company, then pulls a state's merger notification rules, then looks up a particular executive's board seats tells a story. You don't need to read the prompt to guess there's a deal in the works. That's exactly the kind of inference our clients pay us to prevent.

So who sees those traces? Depending on the setup, that could be the MCP server operator, a logging service, the agent platform, or whoever is sitting on the network in between. Most of the AI security reviews I've seen focus on where the prompts and documents go. Far fewer ask about tool-call metadata, and this paper treats that metadata as a side channel in its own right.

For CIOs, I think this changes the vendor questionnaire a little. Along with "where does our data go," we should be asking which parties log tool arguments and results, how long they keep them, and whether traces from different sessions can be tied back to a single user. For third-party MCP servers in particular, I'd want to know who runs them and what their retention policy looks like before anyone at the firm connects one to a client matter.

There's a governance piece here too. Rule 1.6 covers information relating to the representation, and I'm not aware of any bar authority that carves out metadata about your AI's tool usage. I wouldn't want to be the firm that tests that question.

The authors call for privacy-aware mechanisms for agent-tool interactions, which is researcher-speak for "somebody needs to build this." In the meantime, the practical step is the boring one. Inventory the MCP connections that exist at the firm, who approved them, and where their logs live. I suspect a lot of firms would be surprised by that list.

Does anyone at your firm actually know how many MCP servers your lawyers have connected this year?
