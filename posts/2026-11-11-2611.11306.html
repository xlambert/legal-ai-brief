My Agent Read the Terms of Service and Scraped the Site Anyway

ScrapeBench finds AI agents almost never follow websites' no-scraping rules, even after they read them, while some refuse to scrape sites that allow it

Paper: arXiv:2611.11306 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11306

Van Buren therefore reinforces our conclusion that the concept of “without authorization” does not apply to public websites.

hiQ Labs, Inc. v. LinkedIn Corp., Ninth Circuit, opinion by Judge Berzon, April 18, 2022

Scraping law has spent the last decade sorting out what a website can actually forbid. AI agents just walked into the middle of that argument.

Ninth Circuit, 2017 to 2022

hiQ Labs used automated bots to collect what LinkedIn users had put on their public profiles. In May 2017, LinkedIn sent hiQ a cease-and-desist letter saying it was violating LinkedIn's User Agreement and demanding that it stop. hiQ went to court instead.

The Ninth Circuit sided with hiQ in a September 2019 opinion upholding a preliminary injunction. In June 2021, the Supreme Court sent the case back for another look in light of Van Buren v. United States, its new decision on computer access. The panel reached the same result in 2022, explaining that a public website “has erected no gates to lift or lower in the first place.”

Northern District of California, January 2024

Meta's fight with Bright Data was a contract case. In Judge Edward Chen's January 23, 2024 order, the question was whether Facebook's and Instagram's terms barred scraping public data while logged off. “Therefore, the Terms cannot bar Bright Data's logged-off scraping activities,” he wrote, and he granted Bright Data summary judgment on the breach of contract claim. Bloomberg Law covered the ruling the next day.

November 2026

In ScrapeBench: Evaluating Legal Compliance of AI Agents in Website Scraping, Joseph Marvin Imperial, Daniel Slate and Noam Kolt asked AI agents to scrape 1,626 popular websites that explicitly prohibit scraping in their terms of service or robots.txt files. Most agents complied with those prohibitions less than 5% of the time, and the rate stayed low even when the agents checked the terms first.

On 269 sites that don't prohibit scraping, some agents refused anyway. Claude Opus 4.6 over-refused 98.5% of the time. A human baseline of 180 participants complied at about the same rate as the agents and over-refused even more.

Back at the firm

The cases show the legal answer turns on details like whether the scraper was logged in and what the user actually agreed to. That's a judgment call, and this paper says agents are bad at it in both directions: they read the rules and ignore them, or they refuse work that was fine.

Research agents are going to end up on every competitive intelligence and business development desk at law firms, if they aren't there already. I'd put a line in the firm's AI policy about what a research agent may collect from third-party sites, and I'd have the agent log every site it scraped so somebody can check it against that policy.
