My Agent Read the Terms of Service and Scraped the Site Anyway

ScrapeBench tests whether AI agents follow website scraping rules, and the results should get the attention of anyone running research agents.

Back when I ran a law library, we had a standing rule about database terms. If a vendor contract said no bulk downloading, the librarians made sure nobody bulk downloaded, even when an eager associate swore it would save the client money. Somebody had to read the license and say no.

I thought about that rule this week while reading ScrapeBench: Evaluating Legal Compliance of AI Agents in Website Scraping, from Joseph Marvin Imperial, Daniel Slate, and Noam Kolt. They asked a simple question. When you tell an AI agent to scrape a website that prohibits scraping, does it stop?

The short answer is no

The team collected 1,626 popular websites that explicitly prohibit scraping through their terms of service or their robots.txt file. Then they told a range of closed and open-weight agents to scrape them. Most agents followed the site rules less than 5% of the time.

Here's the part that got me. Even when agents actively checked the terms of service and robots.txt, they mostly kept going. They read the rule and scraped anyway. That's the associate who reads the license agreement and downloads the whole database because the partner asked for it.

Sometimes the agent says no to lawful work

The researchers also tested 269 sites that do not prohibit scraping. Some agents refused anyway. Claude Opus 4.6 refused 98.5% of the time on sites where scraping was allowed. So we have agents that ignore the rules and agents that refuse perfectly lawful work, and in neither case does the agent seem to be reasoning about what the terms actually say.

They also ran 180 people through similar tasks. The humans followed the rules at about the same rate as the agents and over-refused a lot more often. I'm not sure whether that makes me feel better.

Why law firms should care

Firms are building and buying agents for competitive intelligence, docket monitoring, public records research, and business development. Every one of those agents touches websites with terms of service. If an associate points one at a competitor's site or a court portal and the agent scrapes it in breach of the terms, the contract claim and any computer-access issues land on the firm, and possibly on a client.

The fact that the agent checked the terms first may make things worse. It's hard to argue nobody knew the rule when the tool's own logs show it read the rule.

On the other side, if your research tool seems uselessly timid, this paper gives you part of the explanation. Over-refusal has a real cost. It pushes people toward whatever tool will just do the job, and that is how shadow AI gets started at a firm.

What I'd put in place now

I'd want three things. First, a short written rule for agents acting on the open web: who approves target sites, and what the agent should do when the terms prohibit automated access. Second, a vendor question about whether the tool checks terms and robots.txt and what it does with what it finds. Third, logs we can actually review when somebody asks what the agent did.

Noam Kolt has been writing about how we govern AI agents for a while now, and this paper gives the rest of us a real number to bring to the risk committee.

Here's the question I'd love to put to a guest on the podcast: if an associate's agent breaches a site's terms, who should carry that, the associate, the firm, or the vendor that built the agent?
