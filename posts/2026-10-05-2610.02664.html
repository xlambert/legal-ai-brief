Did You Forget What I Told You an Hour Ago?

A new paper puts a number on AI agents losing track of their own guardrails

Most partners can tell you about the time they gave an instruction at the start of a project and found out later it didn't stick. "Nothing goes to the client without my review." "Keep their GC off the distribution list." People forget. It turns out AI agents forget too, and now somebody has measured how often.

The paper is A GHOST in Long-Horizon Agents: Governance Hazard from Overlooked Safety Constraints across Turns, from XinPeng Shen and colleagues. The authors call the failure mode GHOST, which stands for Governance Hazard from Overlooked Safety Constraints across Turns. (Yes, they worked hard for that acronym. I'll allow it.)

What GHOST looks like

Here's the setup. Nobody is attacking the agent. There's no prompt injection, no jailbreak, no bad actor. The agent is just working through a long task, and somewhere along the way it takes an action that breaks a safety rule it was given many turns earlier.

On GPT-5.5, that happened 11.5% of the time in their experiments.

The authors also back it up with some math. If the risk of a violation at each step doesn't shrink quickly enough as the session goes on, a long enough run will almost surely hit one. In plain English, the longer the session, the worse your odds.

Why I'm paying attention

I run a long agent workflow myself. The nightly arXiv review that found this paper is one of them, and it carries a long list of rules I set up front. The rule I worry about most is always the one I wrote first, because by the end of a long run it's buried under hours of other work.

Now think about law firm work. Ethical walls. Privilege. "Do not file." "Do not email opposing counsel directly." Agent sessions in Harvey, Legora, Claude, and the rest are getting longer every month, and the instructions that matter most are often given once at the start.

Some of those actions can't be undone. A sent email stays sent. A filed document is on the docket. For that kind of action, 11.5% is a number nobody should be comfortable with.

What the fix looks like

The authors propose STAR-Guard, which works in two layers. First, before the agent acts, it pulls back up whatever earlier constraints apply to that action. Second, a deterministic audit layer checks the proposed action before it reaches the outside world. In their GPT-5.5 experiments, they saw no GHOST events with STAR-Guard in place.

That second layer is the part I'd underline. It's a rule-based gate that sits outside the model.

For law firms, that suggests a few questions to put to vendors. Where do standing instructions live: only in the chat history, or in a policy layer the agent checks before acting? Is there a hard check outside the model before anything leaves the building? And has anyone tested what happens when the rule was given an hour and fifty tool calls ago?

You can test that last one yourself. Give the agent a constraint on turn one, run a long realistic workflow, and watch what happens near the end. It won't take long, and the results will tell you a lot about the product.

If your firm's AI agent were going to break one of your rules, would you find out before it hit send?
