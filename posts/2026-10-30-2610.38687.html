Knowing When to Say 'I Don't Know'

AI models apply rules to facts pretty well. A new benchmark shows what happens when the facts run out.

Every first-year associate learns the same lesson at some point, usually the hard way. A partner hands you a fact pattern, you run it through the rule, and you come back with a confident answer. Then the partner asks one question: "How do you know the client was a resident?" And you realize you don't. You assumed it.

I thought about that moment a lot this week while reading Deogen: Automatic Benchmark Generation for Reasoning over Policies, a new paper from a team that includes Scott Shapiro and Ruzica Piskac. The researchers wanted to test what they call deontic reasoning, which is a fancy way of saying applying rules to facts to reach a verdict. That happens to be most of what lawyers do all day.

How the test works

The clever part is how they built the test. They took written policy rules and translated them into formal logic. From there they could build a decision tree of every possible way the facts might play out. Each path down the tree becomes a test question. If the path reaches the end, there's a correct verdict. If the path stops partway, the facts aren't enough to decide, and the right answer is to abstain.

Because the questions are generated automatically, you can make thousands of them from a single policy. Anyone who has tried to hand-build an evaluation set for a legal AI tool knows how much time that saves.

The numbers that jumped out

When the facts were complete, frontier models got the verdict right 89 to 92 percent of the time. That's a strong result, and I suspect most vendors would be happy to put it on a slide.

When the facts were incomplete, accuracy dropped to somewhere between 22 and 40 percent. The models were told explicitly to abstain when they didn't have enough information. Most of the time, they gave a verdict anyway.

In other words, the models did exactly what that first-year associate did. They assumed the missing fact and kept going.

Why this matters at a law firm

Think about where firms want to use AI for rule application. New business intake. Conflicts screening. Checking a matter against outside counsel guidelines. First-pass analysis on whether a client qualifies for an exemption. In every one of those, the facts are rarely complete on the first pass. A good human reviewer says, "I need to know X before I can answer." According to this paper, the model will usually just answer.

If you're evaluating a legal AI product, look hard at the demo. Clean fact patterns show you the 90 percent number. Ask the vendor to show you what happens when a key fact is missing. Better yet, build a few of those test cases yourself from your own firm policies. The DeoGen approach shows it can be done systematically, and I'd love to see someone in our industry adapt it for engagement letters or outside counsel guidelines.

There's also a supervision angle. Our professional responsibility rules expect lawyers to supervise the work of the assistants they rely on, and I don't know anyone who thinks an AI tool gets a pass on that. A tool that never says "I don't know" puts the whole burden of spotting gaps on the reviewing lawyer. Busy lawyers reading something that looks like a finished answer are exactly the people who won't go hunting for the missing fact.

What I'd do with this

If I were rolling out a rule-application tool tomorrow, I'd add one requirement to the testing plan: a set of incomplete-fact scenarios where the only right answer is "not enough information." I'd track that number separately from overall accuracy, and I'd want it reported every time the vendor updates the model.

I'd also add "insufficient information" as an expected output in our AI use guidance, so lawyers know the tool is allowed to say it and get a little suspicious when it never does.

So here's my question for the folks building and buying these tools: when was the last time your legal AI told you it didn't have enough facts to answer?
