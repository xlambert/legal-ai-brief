Who Is Watching What the Agents Say to Each Other?

A new paper on multi-agent privacy leakage reads a lot like an ethical wall problem.

I have sat through more multi-agent demos this year than I can count. A coordinator agent hands research to one agent, drafting to another, and a third checks the citations. It looks great on the screen. What I almost never see in those demos is anyone showing me what the agents said to each other along the way.

That question sits at the heart of a paper that crossed my desk this week, When Agents Talk, Secrets Travel by Sunday Oyinlola Ogundoyin and Muhammad Ikram. They looked at what happens to sensitive information as it moves through multi-agent systems, and the results should get the attention of every law firm risk manager.

What they found

The researchers ran 485 scenarios across three common setups: a coordinator with worker agents, peers talking directly to each other, and a hierarchy. They watched seven communication channels. Overall leakage stayed about the same in every setup, around 61 to 63 percent. The part that caught my eye was where the information ended up. Disclosure to recipients that only existed because of how the agents were wired together went from zero to 52.2 percent.

Nobody hacked anything. Nobody acted maliciously. The information just flowed where the architecture let it flow.

They also found that the choice of orchestration framework drove a 150-fold spread in leakage. So two platforms that look alike in a demo can behave very differently with your data.

Why this sounds familiar

If you have worked in a law firm for more than a week, you know about ethical walls. A lateral who joins from another firm gets screened from matters where there is a conflict. Our records systems lock down the documents, and we train people on what they can and can't discuss.

All of that assumes the thing on the other side of the wall is a person opening a file. A research agent that read privileged material on Matter A can mention it to a drafting agent working on Matter B. Our DMS permissions would never see that conversation happen.

Think of it like the office kitchen. We put the confidential files in locked cabinets, and then everyone chats freely by the coffee machine. Agents are going to be very chatty at the coffee machine.

The fix they tested

The authors built a sanitizer called RECONS that checks who is about to receive a message, and in what context, before it decides what to remove. It cut leftover disclosure from 96.7 percent to 29.25 percent and kept 94.1 percent of the sharing that was supposed to happen. Blanket redaction kept only 20.5 percent, which basically breaks the workflow.

Even their fix let almost a third of the problem through, and most of the misses came from failing to spot the sensitive item in the first place. That tells me I should be very skeptical of any product that claims to have solved this.

What I would ask vendors

If I were sitting across from a vendor pitching a multi-agent legal workflow, I would want to know a few things. Can you log every message one agent sends to another, along with the recipient? Can I set rules based on matter and recipient, the way our conflicts system already works? Have you tested what happens to leakage when the orchestration setup changes?

I would also bring the general counsel's office in early. Model Rule 1.6 and a stack of outside counsel guidelines already require us to protect this information, and none of them carve out an exception for software.

So here is what I keep coming back to. If an agent hands privileged information to another agent sitting on the wrong side of an ethical wall, who at your firm would even know it happened?
