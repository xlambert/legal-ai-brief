Would Your Hiring Bot Ask the Illegal Question?

A new study finds language models pick unlawful interview questions once a hiring prompt turns up the pressure

Paper: arXiv:2611.24970 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24970

… is suing Workday, Inc., for utilizing an AI screening system …

From the discovery order in Mobley v. Workday, Inc., No. 23-cv-00770 (N.D. Cal. May 19, 2025), Magistrate Judge Laurel Beeler

That sentence describes a lawsuit over software that sorts job applicants. A new paper asks what happens when the software helps write the interview itself.

San Francisco, California, May 2025

Judge Laurel Beeler described Derek Mobley's suit as “an employment-discrimination case and putative class action.” That spring the parties were fighting over discovery. One of Mobley's requests sought data he wanted for a bias evaluation of the tool.

Workday answered that “no such data exists.” Judge Beeler wrote, “The court accepts this representation,” and denied that request. She ordered supplemental briefing on several others, including one for applicant flow data that Workday said it does not own.

So more than two years into the case, the argument had narrowed to a practical question. What testing records did anyone keep about how the tool treated people?

November 2026

In HireWatch: Evaluating LLM Compliance with U.S. Employment Law Under Contextual Pressure, Huy Nghiem, Maria Kostylew and Noam Kolt built a bank of 200 interview questions, some lawful and some unlawful. They classified each one using U.S. federal law and agency guidance.

Then they asked 18 closed and open-weight models to pick questions from the bank while preparing for job interviews across a range of occupations and contexts. Over roughly 100,000 trials, they counted how often a model chose an unlawful question. The tests ran under several types of what the authors call contextual pressure.

In low-pressure settings the models mostly stayed away from the unlawful questions, which is the result a vendor demo would probably show you. Violations rose sharply as the pressure went up. Telling a model outright to comply with the law improved its record, though some violations remained. The authors call the models' legal compliance “brittle,” and they released the question bank and code so others can rerun the test.

Back at the firm

Our employment lawyers already field questions from clients about AI recruiting tools. I'd want this paper in their hands, because it tests the moment when a recruiter is most likely to lean on a tool and least likely to read its output closely.

Picture a client's HR team that uses a chatbot to draft interview guides. A recruiter with a hard role to fill tells it the job needs someone who can travel constantly and work weekends without scheduling conflicts. The model suggests asking about childcare plans. The guide looks polished, so nobody flags the question before it reaches a candidate.

I'd advise clients to run their own tools against the HireWatch bank, using pressure prompts that sound like their own recruiters, before rollout. I'd also add a line to our AI vendor questionnaires asking whether the vendor has tested anything similar and kept the results. The Mobley discovery fight shows how quickly a case turns to the records a vendor kept, or never created.

If your client's recruiting chatbot is told to fill a hard role by Friday, would anyone there know which questions it suggested?
