When the AI Reviewer Defers to the Note in the File

A paper on agent judges has a warning for anyone building AI review into legal workflows

arXiv:2611.05662 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05662

Picture a second-year associate checking a first-year's work. She pulls the file, finds the document that contradicts the draft, and knows the draft is wrong. Then she sees a sticky note from a partner that says the issue was already cleared. She signs off.

I could not stop thinking about that scene while reading Grounded but Not Immune: Confirmation Bias in Agent-as-a-judge by Drake Benjamin Brown and colleagues.

What they did

An Agent-as-a-Judge is an AI evaluator that gets tools and the full record of what another agent did, so it can verify the result against the environment. The idea is that a judge who gathers its own evidence will be grounded by it. That sounds sensible to me, and it is the pitch behind a lot of AI quality control products.

The researchers added one false pre-screening note, attributed to an authority, that had no bearing on the correct verdict. Across four judges and five datasets, that single note overturned up to 40% of the judge's correct verdicts. The judges had collected decisive evidence and still flipped.

Three ways it goes wrong

The authors sorted the flips into three failure modes. Over-trust means the judge verifies, reaches the correct conclusion, and then defers to the note anyway. Hallucination means the judge fabricates support for the flip. Under-investigation means the judge stops at the first corroborating signal.

Two of the four judges were Claude models, and for them the failure was mostly over-trust, 67% to 75% of the harmful flips. Smaller judges spread their errors across all three modes. Lightweight mitigations blunted the bias and never removed it.

I find that second detail worth sitting with. The judge did the work and found the right answer. Then it deferred to a note. I have worked with plenty of humans who do the same thing, and I suspect you have too.

Why a law firm should care

Firms are starting to wire AI checkers into their workflows. One model extracts the terms from a contract, and a second one reviews the extraction. One drafts a citation list, and another confirms the cases exist. The reviewer step is the control we point to when someone asks how we manage hallucination risk.

Now ask what the reviewer sees. In many designs it sees the first model's output, a confidence score, maybe a note from an earlier stage that says the item was checked. Those are the authority cues. Nobody has ever said, "You know what we really want? A verification step that defers to whoever wrote the first draft." We may be building one anyway.

A few practical moves come out of this paper. Keep earlier conclusions and approval notes out of the reviewer's inputs when you can. Log the evidence the reviewer found next to its verdict, so a person can spot cases where the evidence and the verdict disagree. Test the reviewer on its own, with planted bad notes, before you rely on it. And remember that a second AI is one more component that needs validating.

To be fair to the authors, they describe the false note as a controlled diagnostic of confirmation bias, and their tasks come from general agent benchmarks. Legal tasks may look different. I would want to see this repeated with contract and citation review before drawing firm conclusions.

Does the review step in your firm's AI workflow see the earlier reviewer's notes before it forms its own view?
