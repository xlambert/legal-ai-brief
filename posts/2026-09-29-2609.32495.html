Hearsay: Who Wrote the Agent's Log?

A new paper asks whether anyone can trust the record an AI agent writes about itself

Every agent demo I've sat through in the past year has a screen I think of as the "trust us" screen. It's the activity log. Step one, the agent read the document. Step two, it called the search tool. Step three, it drafted the summary. Nice timestamps, clean formatting, maybe a little green check mark next to each step.

I've always wondered who wrote that log. A paper posted to arXiv this week, with the wonderful title Hearsay: Can an Auditor Trust the Record a Deployed Agent Harness Writes?, answers that question, and the answer isn't comforting.

The record is written by the thing being audited

The authors, Jiahong Dai and colleagues, focus on the agent harness. That's the code that wraps a model and turns it into an agent: it passes instructions to the model, runs the tools, and writes down what happened. When a run goes wrong and somebody investigates, the harness's record is usually all they get.

The researchers call a record evidentiary when a reader who wasn't there can check it without trusting whoever wrote it. They looked at sixteen deployed agent frameworks. None of them writes a record like that in full.

Then they tested it. Five harnesses ran fourteen tasks, and three blinded LLM examiners plus a human panel read the records. The examiners correctly named the fault in 74 to 91% of the 140 runs, which sounds good. But when the researchers checked what the examiners cited, fewer than one citation in ten pointed at anything the harness didn't write itself. And when the researchers deleted, rewrote, or fabricated entries in the logs, the best examiner caught about half of them.

Any lawyer reading this will see the problem right away. We have a whole body of evidence law about out-of-court statements offered for their truth, and this is a log offered for its truth with nobody available to cross-examine.

A second author

The fix they tested is simple. Keep an append-only log of everything that passes between the harness and the model, store it outside the harness, and compare it against the harness's own record in both directions. That independent log caught all 28 omissions and fabrications the researchers made a harness commit while it ran.

They also tried the fix I would have guessed first, a hash chain over the harness's own record to make it tamper-evident. It passed all 28 fakes. A hash shows that nobody changed the record after the fact. If the harness wrote something wrong in the first place, the hash just protects the wrong entry.

Why this matters for firms

We're starting to let agents take real actions on client work. They pull documents, draft correspondence, update matter records, and fill out forms. Sooner or later one of those actions will be disputed by a client, by opposing counsel, by a court, or by our own malpractice carrier. When that happens, the firm will need to show what the agent actually did.

If the only record comes from the agent's own harness, we're in a weak spot. So here are the questions I'm adding to our agent vendor reviews. Does the product keep an independent log of what went to the model and what came back? Is that log stored somewhere the agent can't write to? Can the firm get a copy, in a format we can actually read, without filing a support ticket?

I'd also add a logging requirement to the AI governance framework for any agent that acts on client matters. A simple independent log of what went to the model and what came back would cover most of it.

Which leads to the question I'd put to every vendor in this space: if one of your agent's actions ends up in front of a judge, what record would you hand over, and who wrote it?
