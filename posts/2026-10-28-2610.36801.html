Are You Getting the Model You Paid For?

A new statistical audit can spot when an AI API quietly routes your requests to a cheaper model

Here's a question I hadn't thought to ask until this week. When a legal AI vendor tells us their product runs on a particular frontier model, how would we know if it didn't?

Most of the tools we license are built on top of someone else's model API. The vendor sends our request to the provider, gets text back, and shows it to us. The text doesn't come with a receipt. We take everyone's word for which model wrote it.

A paper posted this week, API-TAIL: Detecting Model Substitution in LLM APIs through Temperature-Aligned Likelihood Tails, by Haocheng Mei, Yuchuan Tian, Ye Yuan, Chao Xu, Allen Chen, and Kai Han, takes that problem head on.

The problem

The authors describe a simple cost-cutting move. A provider routes some fraction of requests to a cheaper substitute model while billing for the advertised one. If it's only a small share of traffic, nobody notices. The output still reads fine most of the time.

Catching this is harder than it sounds, because honest providers also change the output in ways they don't document. They adjust temperature, sampling settings, repetition penalties, and default system prompts. So any audit has to tell the difference between a provider changing a setting and a provider swapping the model.

What they built

The key observation in API-TAIL is that legitimate configuration changes tend to nudge every response in a batch by a similar amount. A substituted model shows up as a handful of individual responses that look odd for the model being claimed. So the audit first adjusts for the batch-wide shift by fitting a single effective temperature. Then it counts how many individual responses still sit far out in the tail. If that count passes a calibrated threshold, the endpoint gets flagged.

Tested against real commercial APIs, the method kept false alarms under control across a range of honest configurations and caught even low-rate substitution with a small number of queries. It also beat existing audit methods that lost their detection power under the same conditions.

I'll admit the statistics are above my pay grade in places. The idea is easy enough to follow, though. It reminds me of a wine buyer who knows a vineyard's style well enough to spot the few bottles in a case that came from somewhere else, even after the vintage changed.

Why I care about this at a law firm

The first reason is procurement. Model choice drives price tiers in most AI contracts. If we're paying for the premium model, we should have a way to confirm we're getting it, or at least a contract term that lets us audit. This paper shows that kind of audit is workable from the outside, using only the text the API returns.

The second is quality. Our lawyers' trust in a tool is built on how it performs. If a vendor quietly downgrades a slice of requests during peak load, the work product gets worse in ways that are hard to trace. A lawyer just notices the tool seems flaky some days.

The third is what we say to clients and courts. Firms are starting to disclose which AI tools they use, sometimes because a judge's standing order requires it. If we tell a court a particular model was used, we should be able to back that up.

What I'd do with it

I'd bring this paper to the next renewal conversation with any AI vendor and ask a simple question: how do you verify which model answers our requests, and what do you tell us when your upstream provider changes something? I'd also want contract language on model changes and notice. For our own direct API use, this is the kind of spot check an innovation team could run every few months.

So the next time a vendor tells you which model is under the hood, what will you ask them to show you?
