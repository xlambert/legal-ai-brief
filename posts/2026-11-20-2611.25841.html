Whose Harm Makes It Into the 10-K?

A new paper matches 9,001 AI incidents against SEC filings and finds that harms to outsiders rarely reach the risk factors

Paper: arXiv:2611.25841 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25841

Securities disclosure thus records the risks that AI actors face rather than the harms that people subject to AI bear.

From the abstract of Whose Risks Count? What 1,901 AI Incidents Reveal About Corporate Disclosures, arXiv, November 2026

The SEC has already shown it cares what companies say about their AI. This paper looks at what they leave unsaid.

The SEC, March 2024

In March 2024 the agency settled an AI washing matter with Global Predictions, an investment adviser. Bloomberg Law reported on March 18 that the SEC said the firm made “false and misleading statements” about its purported use of AI. Global Predictions told Bloomberg it “cooperated fully with the inquiry and is pleased to put this behind us.”

Linda Masina and Janet Chanchal, legal analysts at Bloomberg Law, later wrote that the firm had marketed itself as the “first regulated AI financial advisor” and called the matter “particularly instructive on the role of social media in AI washing.”

By November 2024, Ben Miller of Bloomberg Law was reporting that the crackdown looked set to continue. He quoted Gurbir Grewal, the SEC's former enforcement director, describing AI washing as “old school fraud using new school buzzwords.” Amy Jane Longo of Ropes & Gray told him, “The AI-washing cases that we’ve seen so far are low-hanging fruit.”

Every one of those cases was about a company overstating its AI. Nobody was asking yet about AI harms a company never mentioned.

November 2026

In Whose Risks Count?, Simeon Allmendinger, Edyta Paulina Bogucka, Niklas Kühl and Daniele Quercia matched 9,001 AI incidents to 18,229 SEC filings from 328 companies that provide or deploy AI, sorting both by risk type.

Their starting point is materiality, the test for what a filing must disclose, which turns on what a reasonable investor would consider important. Under that test a harm to people outside the company reaches a filing only once it becomes a financial risk to the company.

The numbers bear that out. Of 1,901 incidents with filings both before and after them, half fell in a risk type the company had never disclosed. Filings covered the risk types of 67% of system failures and only 2% of social system harms. The companies themselves reported fewer than 2% of the incidents. The authors suggest that public authorities could make those harms financially material by holding companies accountable for them, and they released their matched data as DISC, the Disclosure-Incident Securities Corpus.

Back at the firm

Our securities lawyers drafting AI risk factors for public company clients now have a dataset of real incidents, sorted by risk type, to test a draft against. Picture a lender whose risk factors mention AI outages while its AI also decides credit applications. This paper suggests that gap is common, and anyone with the dataset can spot it.

The paper also changes how I'd read a vendor's 10-K. When our innovation team reviews a publicly traded AI vendor, the risk factors tell us what worries its investors. They may say little about our risk as a user. I'd ask vendors directly for their incident history and treat the filing as one input among several.

When your firm reviews a public AI vendor, whose risks are you reading about?
