42.9%: Grading Legal Research Agents Like a Partner Grades a Memo

Legal Research Bench sets an all-or-nothing bar, and the best model clears it on fewer than half the questions

When I was working in law libraries, one of the first things I learned was that the associate who brought back nine good cases and one overruled one hadn't really brought back nine good cases. They'd brought back a problem. The partner reading the memo didn't hand out partial credit. One bad cite, and the whole memo got a second look.

That's why I liked a new paper this week more than most of the benchmark papers that cross my desk. Legal Research Bench: Measuring End-to-End Reliability in Long-Horizon Legal Research Agents, by Katrina Drozdov, Oliver Chen, Langston Nashold, and Rayan Krishnan, grades AI research agents the way a partner grades that memo.

How the test works

The authors worked with experts to write 413 open-ended U.S. legal research questions. Each one comes with a gold answer, the authorities that support it, and a pass/fail rubric. Thirteen frontier models got a real toolkit: web search, case law search, page parsing, and retrieval. Then came the hard part. A response only counts as correct if it meets every item on the rubric and every authority it cites actually checks out.

They also checked their LLM grader against practicing attorneys to make sure the scores track what a lawyer would say. I appreciate that step. A lot of benchmarks skip it.

The number

The best performer was Claude Opus 4.8, and it was fully correct on 42.9% of the questions. That's the top of the leaderboard.

A couple of other findings jumped out at me. Scores varied quite a bit by area of law, and they dropped on questions where the agent had to reconcile conflicting authorities. Anyone who has done real research knows that's where the work lives. The questions that eat up an afternoon usually involve a circuit split, a recent amendment, and a district court that didn't get the memo.

The finding I keep thinking about is that more turns, more tool calls, and more spending did not predict better accuracy. Letting the agent grind longer didn't help. That cuts against a lot of the vendor pitches I've heard this year about deep research modes that run for twenty minutes.

What I'd do with this at a firm

First, I'd use it in vendor conversations. Every legal research AI vendor has an accuracy number. Ask them how they define correct, and whether a response with one bad citation counts as a pass. If their number comes from a looser standard, that's fine, but you need to know it before you compare their figure to this one.

Second, I'd use it to decide where human review goes. If I'm running a research workflow with an agent in it, the questions that involve conflicting authority or recent changes in the law need a lawyer's eyes on every cite. That's the same supervision we've always expected for junior work, and now there's a published number behind it.

Third, I'd put it in training. Lawyers who've had a few good experiences with an AI research tool start to trust it the way they'd trust a strong second-year associate. 42.9% is a handy number to have in your back pocket when that trust gets ahead of the evidence.

I'll also give the agents some credit. Passing an all-or-nothing test on 42.9% of expert questions means an agent got a lot of hard questions completely right, and many of the misses may still be useful starting points. The real issue is whether the workflow around the tool is built for something that misses the full standard more often than it hits it.

The bigger picture

What I like most about this benchmark is that it measures the thing we actually care about. Lawyers get paid for answers they can stand behind in front of a judge or a client, and that's the standard this test applies. I hope vendors start reporting against it, or something like it, so we can stop comparing numbers that were never measuring the same thing.

So here's my question for firms that have already rolled out an AI research agent: who is checking the 57% of answers that wouldn't pass this test?
