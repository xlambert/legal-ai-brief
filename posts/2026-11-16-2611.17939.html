Run It Again: What One Benchmark Score Hides

Seven identical runs of a shipped finance agent produced seven different scores, which is a lesson for anyone buying legal AI off a leaderboard

Paper: arXiv:2611.17939 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17939

“Can you trust a finance agent you did not build? We take the reference agent shipped with the Vals Finance Agent Benchmark v2, leave it untuned, and ask four questions a leaderboard score does not answer.”

From the abstract of Can You Trust Your Finance Agent? Drift, Diagnosis and Auditable Intervention on One Shipped Financial Research Agent

Legal tech has its own Vals leaderboard, and law firms have been reading it for nearly two years.

The Vals Legal AI Report, February 2025

Vals AI published its first legal AI benchmark study in February 2025. The Vals Legal AI Report tested four products, Harvey Assistant, Thomson Reuters' CoCounsel, vLex's Vincent AI and Vecflow's Oliver, on seven legal tasks that included document Q&A and redlining. Artificial Lawyer reported that “Lexis+AI (from LexisNexis) was initially evaluated but withdrew from the sections studied in this report.”

Bob Ambrogi at LawSites reported that CoCounsel posted the top summarization score, 77.2%, and that the lawyer control group beat every tool on redlining. Scores like these are easy to carry into a procurement meeting.

November 2026

Vals also publishes a Finance Agent Benchmark, and its reference agent is the subject of the new paper by Cosimo Galeone, Anna Ettorre and their coauthors. They left the agent untuned and ran it seven times on the same 18 questions. A rubric criterion is one yes-or-no check a grader applies to an answer. The agent passed anywhere from 105 to 123 of 149 criteria, and the bill moved by as much as 17%.

Then they asked who owns each failure. Of 20 failed runs, 13 traced to the agent's instructions and 4 to the model. Tools accounted for the remaining 3, and the data for none. Four blind readers from three model families agreed with the authors' automated analyzer 94% of the time, about the rate at which they agreed with one another.

The last test is the one I'd copy. An optimization loop made one targeted change per round and kept it only if it cleared a fixed threshold. Tuned for cost, the final setup ran at 0.34 times the baseline cost on held-out questions, with accuracy within noise. Tuned for accuracy, it gained 14 points on the development questions and only 1.5 on held-out ones, which the authors' own rule, written before the test, counts as noise. They are careful to call the whole thing a case study of one agent and nine test questions.

Back at the firm

The spread between 105 and 123 is the number I'd bring to a vendor meeting. The agent and the questions never changed, yet the score moved by 18 criteria.

When a vendor shows us a single benchmark score, I'd ask how many times they ran it and what the range looked like. I'd also borrow the authors' ownership question. If most failures live in the instructions a vendor writes around the model, then the vendor's configuration work deserves as much scrutiny as the model name on the sales sheet, and that is something we can test during a pilot.

Picture a pilot where an associate runs the same twenty diligence questions through a tool on Monday and again on Thursday. If the answers shift, the firm needs to know how much before the tool reaches a client matter, and it needs a written threshold for how much shift it will accept.

If your legal AI vendor reran its best benchmark seven times, would it show you the lowest score?
