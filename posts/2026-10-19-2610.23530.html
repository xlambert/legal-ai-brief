Everyone's a Builder Now. Who's Checking the Bots?

A study of 217 teacher-built chatbots is a preview of what happens when lawyers build their own AI assistants.

One of the selling points I hear in almost every legal AI demo now is some version of "and your lawyers can build their own." Harvey, Legora, Copilot, and others all let a lawyer write plain-language instructions, attach a few documents, and share a custom assistant with the practice group. I like it. Lawyers know their workflows better than anyone in IT does, and giving them the tools to build is a good thing.

I've been wondering what happens six months later, though, after a few hundred of these assistants are out there and nobody remembers who built which one.

A new paper gives us a pretty good preview, even though it has nothing to do with law. "EduWild: How Teacher-Configured Chatbots Fail in Real-World Use and How to Fix Them", by Ziqi Liu, Ruiwei Xiao, Yuxuan Li, and John Stamper, looks at chatbots that teachers set up themselves using natural-language instructions. Swap "teacher" for "partner" and "student" for "associate" or "client," and the setup will look awfully familiar.

What they found

The team collected 1,726 conversations with 217 teacher-built chatbots, including 900 conversations from the students those bots were actually built for. They built a 29-category list of failures and applied it with an AI grader calibrated against human coders.

The most common problems were the bots ignoring what the teacher told them to do. They skipped required workflow steps in 53.8% of student conversations. They left out the setup of expectations in 33.4%. And in 37.1% they buried the student with too much information or too many questions at once.

Think about what that would look like in a firm. A partner builds an intake assistant that's supposed to ask about conflicts before anything else, and half the time it jumps straight to the substance. Or a research assistant that's supposed to flag when it's unsure decides to sound confident anyway. Nobody would see it unless they were looking.

The good news

The authors went on to fix things. They wrote a prompt-based harness (think of it as a standard wrapper around each teacher's instructions) aimed at the six most frequent failure types. In a study with 200 college students across ten bots, failures per conversation dropped from about 1.4 to about 0.5. That's a 65% cut from a fix that didn't require rebuilding anything.

That's the part I'd take to a knowledge management or innovation team. A shared harness, a short checklist, and a quick review before an assistant gets shared beyond its builder could catch a lot of this.

So who owns it?

Here's where it gets uncomfortable. Most of our governance frameworks were written for tools the firm buys and IT deploys. A partner-built assistant shared with twenty lawyers sits somewhere between a vendor product and an IT project, and it's producing work that touches client matters.

Locking it all down would kill the thing that makes these tools useful. What I'd like to see is a light approval path: a named owner for every shared assistant, a basic test against its own instructions, and a date to look at it again. The EduWild failure list is a decent starting point for that test, since many of its categories (skipped steps, missing setup, information overload) map right onto legal workflows.

It's good training material too. Showing lawyers real examples of a bot ignoring its own instructions is the kind of AI literacy session people actually remember.

When a lawyer at your firm builds an assistant and shares it with the group, who checks that it actually follows the instructions it was given?
