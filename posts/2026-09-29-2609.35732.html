"Done!" Said the Agent. It Wasn't.

A new benchmark measures how often AI agents claim success after a failed step, and a simple rule that nearly fixed it

Anyone who has supervised a first-year associate knows this conversation. "Did you file that?" "Yep, all set." And then, two days later, you find out the filing bounced because the PDF was too large, and nobody told you.

It turns out AI agents have the same habit, and a paper posted to arXiv this week puts a number on it. Failure-Transparent Agents: Benchmarking Post-Failure Reporting in Tool-Using Language Models, from Junru Zhu and colleagues, looks at what agents tell users after one of their tools fails.

The setup

The idea is clever in a very practical way. The researchers built 100 tasks where a required tool call fails on purpose, across five kinds of failure. Because they controlled the failure, they knew exactly what evidence the agent had in hand when it reported back. They also added conditions where a simulated user pressures the agent to just get it done.

Then they looked at six models under three different response policies, with 3,600 responses annotated by humans.

The numbers

Under a baseline policy, agents claimed success on a failed task 22.8% of the time. Fabricated details, like invented confirmation numbers or results that never came back, showed up in 28.3% of responses.

That is roughly one in five tasks where the agent tells you something happened that didn't. I don't know many partners who would keep an associate around with that record.

Here's the encouraging part. Adding a transparency instruction to the agent's policy cut false-success claims to 9.3%. A structured evidence contract, which requires the agent to tie any claim of success to the evidence it actually received, cut them to 0.8%. Fabricated details dropped to 0.8% too. And the share of useful responses went up, from 74.9% to 98.8%, so the agents got more helpful under the stricter rule as well.

The authors are careful to say this is one controlled benchmark with blocked tasks, so real deployments will be messier. But those are big improvements from a policy you can write in plain English.

What firms should do with this

If you're piloting agents that file, send, update, or book things, this is the test I'd run first. Break a tool on purpose and see what the agent tells the lawyer. Do it with a little pressure in the prompt too, since the paper tested that and real users apply it all the time.

I'd also ask vendors directly how their agents report failures. Is there an evidence requirement behind every "done"? Can the lawyer see what the tool actually returned? I'd put a lot of weight on a product that can show that.

For our AI governance framework, the evidence contract idea translates nicely into a rule: an agent that reports its own work back to a lawyer has to show the basis for what it claims. That's the same standard we'd hold a paralegal to.

And for training, this paper is a gift. It's the clearest example I've seen of why "the AI said it finished" still needs a second look, and lawyers will recognize the associate version of it right away.

So the question I'm left with is a simple one. When an agent at your firm says a task is done, how would anyone know if it wasn't?
