The Agent Got the Right Answer. Did It Follow the Rules?

A new benchmark finds most "successful" AI agent runs skipped required steps along the way.

Back in my law library days, I'd occasionally see a research memo that landed on the right answer more or less by accident. Right case, but nobody checked subsequent history. Right rule, but nobody looked at the regs. The answer was fine that time, but I always sent those memos back with questions.

I thought about those memos while reading CXBench: A Benchmark for Process-Compliant LLM Agents in Regulated Customer-Experience Workflows, a new paper from Boxi Yu, Lionel Briand, Emir Muñoz, and colleagues.

The researchers built a benchmark for AI agents working in regulated customer-service settings across six domains. These are the kinds of workflows where a correct final result isn't enough. The agent also has to give the required disclosures, get consent, and do things in the right order. Each task comes with "rule cards" that tie a regulatory obligation to something you can see in the agent's trace.

Then they scored seven models on 120 tasks, grading the outcome and the process separately.

Here's the number that jumped out at me. Of the runs that got the right outcome, 80.1% failed at least one process check. Only 13.8% of all runs passed both. The authors call these "false passes," and I think that's a great term. The dashboard says green even though the required step never happened.

Even better, scoring the process changed which models came out on top. The ranking you'd get from outcomes alone looks noticeably different.

Why law firms should care

Think about the agentic tools showing up in legal right now. Client intake. Conflicts checks. Engagement letters. Litigation hold notices. Outside counsel billing guideline compliance. Every one of those has required steps baked into it, many of them coming from ethics rules or client contracts.

An intake agent that captures the right client information but never runs the conflicts question before promising a callback is a problem. An e-billing agent that produces a clean invoice but ignores the client's task code rules is going to get the invoice kicked back, or worse, cut.

Most vendor demos I've seen grade the end state. Did the invoice go out? Did the matter get opened? That's what this paper is warning about.

What I'd ask for

When I'm evaluating an agent now, I want to see traces. Every tool call, every message, in order. I want the vendor to show me how they test process, separately from outcome, and what their false-pass rate looks like on their own test set.

I also like the rule-card idea for our own governance work. Take each obligation that applies to a workflow, write down what evidence of compliance would look like in the trace, and check for it. It's the kind of thing a KM or risk team could build without a PhD, and it gives the AI governance committee something concrete to review.

To be fair, this is one benchmark, the domains are customer service with no legal workflows in the set, and real deployments usually have a human somewhere in the loop. But the gap is big enough that I'd want to know where our tools sit before trusting them with anything client-facing.

If your firm deployed an intake agent tomorrow, who would be reading the traces to make sure it asked the conflicts questions before it promised anything?
