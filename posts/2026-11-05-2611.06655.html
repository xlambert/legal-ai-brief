Same Question, Different Answer

A UK public-sector AI assurance study that law firm AI committees should read

arXiv:2611.06655 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06655

Ask a model the same question twice and you would like the same answer. Everybody knows these systems are probabilistic. How often do we test for it before a tool goes live at the firm?

Louise Yorke put numbers on it in Beyond accuracy: what AI assurance needs to establish in public services. She had eight GPT-4o configurations sort 1,200 questions from UK Prime Minister's Questions into 21 policy topics, then compared the results with human coders and with the model's own repeat runs.

The numbers

Accuracy landed between 68.2% and 70.3%. A vendor would happily put that on a slide. Underneath it, 24.3% of the questions got a different label when the run was repeated with nothing changed. Topic shares and rankings moved with them. The story you would tell about what Parliament cares about shifts depending on which run you happened to keep.

The configurations also agreed with one another more than they agreed with the human coders. Think about that for a second. If you ran three setups and saw them line up, you would feel good about it. This paper shows that agreement among AI runs can come from shared errors, so a consensus among models tells you very little about whether the answer is right.

Then there is review routing. Under the paper's score-extraction method, the share of items flagged for human review ranged from 0.9% to 41.0%. One setup would send almost nothing to a person. Another would send four in ten. If a firm tells its partners that a human reviews anything the system flags, the flag threshold decides how much review actually happens.

What the policy documents say

Yorke also read 37 UK and EU policy documents on AI testing and oversight. Both sets expect testing and oversight. Their reporting expectations do not consistently cover changing answers, shared errors, selective evaluation, substantive conclusions or review routing. Some UK guidance addresses stability explicitly. The UK sample leans on public bodies checking their own use, and the EU puts substantial testing duties on providers while leaving them real choices on measures and acceptance thresholds.

Law firms sit outside the public sector, and we still produce evidence and advice that clients rely on. Deposition summaries, contract classifications and privilege tagging are all AI-produced outputs of the same kind. Clients, their outside counsel guidelines and, increasingly, regulators will ask how we know we can rely on them.

What I would do on Monday

Three things. Repeat your test runs and report how many labels changed. Compare against human-coded samples, and use another model only as a supplement. Write down the review-routing threshold and who chose it. The paper's own conclusion is that organisations should justify reliance through checks beyond aggregate accuracy, with clear responsibility, access to expertise and scrutiny of results.

One caution from me. This study used political questions and one model family, and 21 topics is a coarse scheme. Legal classification tasks might behave better or worse. That is the reason to run your own repeat test, since nobody else's number will tell you.

When did your firm last run the same AI task twice and count how many answers changed?
