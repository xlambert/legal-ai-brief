Your Vendor Demo Graded Itself

A global bank's bakeoff of ten agentic AI platforms found its scoring panel was mostly measuring the presentation

Legal tech has already learned what happens when the only numbers come from the people selling the product. It took an outside team and a fight over access to find out.

Stanford, California, 2024

In May 2024, a Stanford team that included Faiz Surani and Daniel E. Ho published a benchmark of AI legal research tools. The vendors had marketed these products as able to "avoid" hallucinations, and the team pointed to promises to guarantee "hallucination-free" legal citations.

The researchers built "a pre-registered dataset of over 200 open-ended legal queries." Lexis+ AI and Ask Practical Law AI each produced incorrect information more than 17% of the time. Westlaw's AI-Assisted Research wasn't in that first round. Bob Ambrogi reported at LawSites that Thomson Reuters had denied the team's "multiple requests" for access, so the study reviewed Ask Practical Law AI instead.

Thomson Reuters then gave the authors access. In the June redo, Westlaw hallucinated 33% of the time, nearly twice the 17% rate for Lexis+ AI. The marketing had described a product that the tests did not find.

November 2026

In A Capability Framework for Evaluating Agentic AI Platforms in Regulated Enterprises, Abbas Raza Ali, Muhammad Ajmal Siddiqui and Moona Zahid report on a bakeoff run by the innovation laboratory of a global bank. An agentic AI platform is software where language model agents plan, call tools, keep memory and change the state of enterprise systems. A cross-functional panel scored ten of them on nine capability dimensions across financial services workloads that included counterparty credit risk and retrieval-augmented generation.

The scores sorted the platforms into tiers that held up under different weightings. Then the authors checked their own instrument. Cronbach's alpha measures how closely a set of ratings move together, and the nine supposedly separate dimensions came in at 0.98. Eight of the nine tracked the total score almost perfectly. In practice, the panel rated one overall impression nine times.

The authors are candid about why. Enterprises, they write, buy these platforms "largely on the strength of vendor demonstrations," and their own panel scored from demonstrations too. Their proposed fix is a gate-then-score protocol with four layers covering capability, execution reliability, governance gates and business value. It adds reliability metrics estimated over repeated runs of the same task, along with a reproducibility protocol.

Back at the firm

Law firms run this kind of bakeoff all the time. Picture a committee of partners and IT staff watching vendors demo contract review agents over two weeks. Each vendor brings its best prompt and its cleanest document. The scoresheet has ten rows, and the vendor with the smoothest presenter wins most of them.

I'd change two things. The firm supplies the documents, including the ugly scanned lease and the agreement whose definitions section runs nine pages. Then every vendor runs the same tasks several times with nobody from the vendor in the room, and we score the traces afterward. If one platform looks great on every row of our rubric, I'd treat that as a warning about the rubric before I'd call it good news about the product. A governance gate belongs at the front too, since a tool that can't show us its logs shouldn't reach the scoring stage at all.

When your firm picks its next AI platform, how much of the score will come from a run your own team controlled?
