The Detector Said 'AI.' Now What?

A new paper explains why AI detectors make a shaky enforcement tool for AI policies, and what a fairer compliance check could look like

Every few months someone at a firm asks me whether we can run an AI detector on something. Sometimes it's a writing sample from a lateral candidate. Sometimes it's an associate's research memo that "sounds a little too smooth." Once it was the other side's brief, from a partner who was hoping for a sanctions motion. I usually answer with a long sigh and a short lecture about false positives.

Now I have a paper to hand them. Policy-Conditioned AI-Use Detection: An Evidentiary Framework for Academic Publishing, by Jairo Diaz-Rodriguez and Mumin Jia, was posted to arXiv this week. It's written for conferences and journals, but swap "reviewer" for "associate" and "venue" for "law firm" and it reads like it was written for us.

The detector answers a different question

The authors point out something that seems obvious once you hear it. Academic venues now have detailed AI rules. Authors may use AI for some things, reviewers for others, and what has to be disclosed changes by role and by task. A detector estimates one thing: whether a model wrote the text. Whether a person followed the rule that applied to them is a separate question.

Our firm AI policies look a lot like those venue rules. A lawyer can use an approved tool to summarize a deposition. A lawyer can't paste client data into a consumer chatbot. Disclosure to the client might be required on one matter and not on another. A detector that says "62% likely AI" tells you nothing about which of those situations you're in.

The math problem

The part that got my attention is the base-rate math. The authors work through peer review and show that at plausible violation rates, a detector running at a strong operating point still flags more compliant authors than people who actually broke the rule. Think about that for a second. If most people are following the policy, even a pretty good detector will point the finger at more innocent people than guilty ones.

Anyone who sat through a statistics lecture on medical screening tests has seen this movie. A test that's 95% accurate for a rare disease still produces a pile of false alarms. AI policy violations at a well-run firm should be rare too, and that is exactly the situation where detectors look their worst.

What they suggest

The authors propose what they call policy-conditioned AI-use detection. The rule becomes an explicit input. The output is a set of hypotheses with the supporting evidence and a stated level of uncertainty, and the evaluation reports how often it catches violations at a false positive rate the organization picks in advance. They also list what a venue has to put in place for any of this to work: structured disclosure, routing work through approved tools (which also protects confidentiality), and a way for someone to contest a finding.

That list is the useful part for law firms. If you want to know whether people are following your AI policy, the best evidence is the stuff you already control: which tools were used, on which matter, with what disclosure. Your approved platforms log most of that today. A detector score is a weak substitute for records you could be keeping right now.

Courts and clients are next

I don't think this stays inside academic publishing. Judges with AI standing orders, clients with outside counsel guidelines that address AI, and bar regulators are all going to want some way of checking compliance. Some of them will reach for a detector because it's the easiest thing to buy. When that happens, firms should be ready to ask which rule is being tested, what the false positive rate is and who set it, and how a finding gets contested.

For our own policies, I'd like to build the disclosure and logging now while it's cheap. Retrofitting an audit trail after a client asks for one is a lot less fun.

So here's my question: if a client ran a detector on your firm's work tomorrow and it came back "AI detected," what records could you show them?
