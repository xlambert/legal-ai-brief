Who Validated the Harness?

What a banking compliance study tells law firm AI committees about vendor testing

arXiv:2611.06116 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06116

Every AI vendor demo I sit through starts with the model. Which one is under the hood, what it scored on the bar exam, how it did on the latest benchmark. Then someone on the firm side asks which model the tool uses, writes it down, and the evaluation file gets a checkmark.

A new paper by Mehmet Murat Huyut, The Harness Is the Model, makes that checkmark look pretty thin.

What they tested

The study dropped four open-weight models into a simulated retail bank. The bank had eleven rule families that paraphrase EU consumer-credit, payments, investor-protection, data-protection and anti-money-laundering provisions. Each model ran inside five different harnesses. A harness is the wrapper around the model: what it gets to see, which tools it can call, and who checks its work.

One harness made a single structured decision. One was a tool-using loop. One made the model find the policy through search. One loaded a long, harmless customer history. One added a second copy of the same model as a compliance reviewer. That adds up to 3,120 two-turn episodes, each scored from the tool trace of what the agent actually did.

What moved

Averaged across all four models, the harness barely mattered. The correct-case rate ran from 87.3% to 92.1%. If you stopped there you would shrug.

Inside a single model the picture changed. One model swung 11.5 percentage points between its best and worst harness, enough to move it from first place to last among the four. Another model showed no detectable difference at all. The author is upfront that the interaction result is suggestive and falls short of conclusive. I appreciate that. It should still make anyone who signs off on an AI tool pause.

Two more findings caught my eye. The same-model reviewer cut violations from 9.6% to 6.4% without causing extra over-refusals, which the author had predicted would not happen. A cheap second look helped. And rules that required date arithmetic failed in every harness. Every one. Think about how many legal deadlines, notice periods and limitation dates hinge on counting days correctly.

Then there is the finding I would put on a slide. In 26% of the violations, the message to the customer denied the action the agent had just executed. The agent said no to the customer while the tool log showed it had gone ahead anyway. A reviewer reading only the transcript would score that as a correct refusal. The action log tells the true story, and the author's recommendation says so: validate the model-harness pair, and read the action log.

What I would do with it

When a lawyer tells me an AI tool was tested, I want to know what was tested. The model alone in a chat window? Or the whole system, with the document management connection, the retrieval step, the permissions, and the review step somebody bolted on later? Most pilots I hear about run on the transcript. Somebody reads the answers and decides they look good. Very few firms check what the system did behind the answers, like which documents it opened, which fields it updated, or what it sent.

This is a bank simulation with open-weight models, and I would not carry the percentages over to a law firm. The structure of the problem carries over fine. We buy tools that wrap a model, and the wrapper changes every few months through vendor updates, new connectors and new review steps. Each change can move the results for one model more than another.

A practical starting point for an AI governance committee: ask vendors to say what changed in the harness with each release, rerun your own test set after each change, and put the action log in front of whoever signs off. Add a second pass for anything that depends on dates.

Who at your firm owns the action log review for the AI tools already in production?
