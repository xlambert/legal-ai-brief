Who Wrote the Answer Key? A Benchmark Audit Worth Reading Before Your Next AI Pilot

A new arXiv paper audited nine benchmarks that grade AI on written rules, and the scorecards had problems of their own.

arXiv:2611.07042 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07042

Every AI vendor deck I see has a benchmark slide. A bar chart, a percentage, a competitor in gray, and our guy in blue sitting a few points higher. We nod at the slide and move on to pricing.

Yanru Xiao and Yifei Cai just gave me a reason to stop nodding. Their paper, Follow the Policy, Fail the Benchmark: Auditing and Revising Policy-Based Decision Benchmarks, audits nine public benchmarks that test whether AI systems can apply written rules to financial and operational cases. These are the kind of tests that tell us whether a model can read a policy and reach the right decision. That sounds a lot like what we want a legal AI tool to do with a tax rule or a contract clause.

What they found

The authors traced the answer keys back to their sources, ran the underlying programs, and ran controlled checks. They found targets with no support in the source material, reference errors that survived a replay, and errors that carried forward as if they were solved steps. They also found scorers that rejected acceptable answers and scorers that gave credit to incomplete ones.

Then they did something I wish more people would do. They took the model responses as they were, left the references alone, and just fixed the scoring. On the 96 numeric cases in LegalBench SARA, a statutory reasoning set built around tax rules, correcting the scorer raised acceptance by more than 80 percentage points for each of the three models they tested. Same answers from the models. Same reference answers. Eighty points of difference came from the grader.

In RuleArena Tax, a required zero floor had been left out. Restoring it changed 27 of 300 references and narrowed the gap that had been observed between models. So a ranking we might have used to pick a vendor moved when somebody checked the arithmetic.

Why a law firm should care

Think about how a firm actually buys. Somebody on the innovation team or in knowledge management reads the vendor's published numbers, maybe a couple of academic leaderboards, and builds a shortlist. If the leaderboard has a bad scorer, we may be eliminating a tool that did fine and keeping one that looked better than it was.

I also think about the pilots we run internally. When a practice group builds its own test set of twenty tax or regulatory questions, who checks the answer key? In my experience the answer key gets written by a busy senior associate on a Thursday night. That key deserves the same skepticism the paper gives to the public ones.

One more point. The paper shows that errors in intermediate answers can change the final decision in a multi-step task. That matches what lawyers see when an AI tool gets the first step of a calculation slightly wrong and everything after it looks confident and tidy. A correct-looking final answer tells you very little about the steps underneath it.

What I would do Monday morning

Ask every vendor for the benchmark name, the version, and who maintains the answer key. Ask whether they re-scored any failures by hand. If they say they did, ask to see ten of them. If they never looked, that is useful to know.

For our own evaluations, I would have a second person rebuild a sample of the answer key from the source rule before any model sees a question. It costs a few hours and it protects a purchase decision that costs a lot more than that.

The authors released their audit evidence and revisions as a versioned collection they call BAR, so other people can re-run the evaluations. I would like to see legal tech vendors say which version of any benchmark they used. Would your current vendors be comfortable telling you?
