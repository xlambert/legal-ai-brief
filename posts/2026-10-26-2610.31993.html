Which Jurisdiction Does Your AI Think It's In?

An Australian clinical benchmark that anyone evaluating legal AI tools should read

Anyone who has worked in a Texas firm for more than a week has watched it happen. Someone asks a general AI tool a procedure question and gets a perfectly confident answer, built on the federal rules, or California's rules, or the version of the Texas rule from before the last amendment. It reads well, and it's wrong for the court you're actually in.

This week I found the best study of that problem I've seen, and it isn't about law at all. AusClinBench: a deterministically scored benchmark of large language models against current Australian clinical policy, by Daniel Epstein, tests AI models against current Australian clinical rules. If you swap "clinical guideline" for "local rule," it reads like it was written for us.

How the test works

Epstein wrote 142 questions from current Australian guidelines and schedules, covering things like prescription authority, billing codes, practitioner obligations, triage red flags, and immunisation. Each question has one verifiable answer, scored by exact match. He tested 28 models between July and October of this year.

Here's the clever part. Every question ran twice: once blind, and once with the governing clause pasted in. That separates two very different failures. Either the model doesn't remember the current rule, or it has the rule in front of it and still can't apply it.

What came back

Blind accuracy ranged from 51.5% to 92.7%, and the best models couldn't be told apart statistically. Of 791 failed model-question pairs, 684 of them, about 86%, were wrong blind and right once the clause was supplied. So most of the failures were memory problems, and handing the model the actual text fixed them.

The confident-error rate is the number that would worry me as a risk partner. It ran from 6.6% to 47.8% depending on the model. And on five immunisation questions where the guideline gives no directive at all, 82% of the answers stated one anyway.

Then there's the jurisdiction problem. On some questions, the wrong answers gave the rule for a different population, an older rule, or another country's rule. Sound familiar?

What a law firm should do with this

First, it backs up what a lot of us have been saying about grounding. If most errors are recall errors that go away when the current text is supplied, then the tools worth paying for are the ones that retrieve the governing rule and show it to you.

Second, I'd steal the test design outright. Pick 50 questions from the Texas Rules of Civil Procedure, a couple of federal district local rules you practice in all the time, and a handful of recent ethics opinions. Write down the one right answer for each. Run every tool you're evaluating blind, then with the rule text supplied. You'll learn more from that afternoon than from most vendor demos.

Third, add a few questions where the honest answer is "the rule doesn't say." That 82% number tells you how often a model will fill a gap with something that sounds authoritative.

Epstein also proposes treating the model as an ingredient in a clinical product, with a minimum standard on local rules that sits beside the product's own evaluation. I like that framing for legal research platforms too. I'd bet most firms haven't tested whether the model underneath their research platform knows their jurisdiction.

One fair caveat from the paper itself: the answer keys are one general practitioner's work and haven't been independently reviewed. That's a reason to build our own keys carefully, with a lawyer and a librarian both signing off.

So who at your firm is writing the first 50 local-rule questions?
