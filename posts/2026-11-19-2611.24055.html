Who Grades the Grader's Reading of the Law?

A new benchmark checks LLM-written legal rules against what Korean companies actually disclosed, and finds the usual grading method flatters them

Paper: arXiv:2611.24055 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24055

Grading legal programs against the grader's own reading can overstate how well they work.

From the abstract of arXiv:2611.24055, November 2026

Turning statutes into programs has a testing problem, and Jinhyung Bae found a way around it by using what companies actually reported to regulators and investors.

November 2026

In Grading Executable Legal Rules Against Independent Records, Bae takes a question with real money behind it: was a given company a small or medium enterprise (SME) on a given date? An executable legal rule is a program that takes the facts and the law and returns that kind of answer. Large language models now write them too.

The usual test has someone read the statute and write an answer key, and the program is scored against that key. Bae's benchmark, LexTemporis, uses something harder to argue with. It checks the programs against 10,901 firm-years of SME status disclosed by Korean listed companies, paired with every version of the governing rules. An appendix adds comparison cases from U.S. income tax and German hospital minimum volumes.

The date of a decision changed outcomes mainly through provisions outside the main statutory text. A transitional provision is the part of a law that says how a change applies to people already covered by the old version. One of them, on its own, raised agreement with the disclosures from the 83 to 86 percent range to 89 or 90 percent. Bae also ran the records against the author's own reading of a contested provision, and the disclosures favored that reading in 130 of 189 firm-years where the readings diverged.

Four LLMs wrote the rules, each given a different selection of the law. A pre-registered comparison found no condition that came out better across all four models. The exploratory results are the part I'd underline. Giving a model the right provisions looked better when graded against the author's reading than when graded against the records. For three of the models that advantage shrank, and for one it fell to between a quarter and three-fifths of its size. Per firm-year, the advantage was several times larger exactly where the author's reading was wrong.

That last finding is partly built into the design, as Bae says. A program that reproduces a wrong reading more faithfully has to lose when the grader is reality. The conclusion is still plain enough for any of us to use: grading legal programs against the grader's own reading can overstate how well they work.

Back at the firm

Most legal AI acceptance tests look like the usual test Bae describes. A partner or a knowledge lawyer writes the expected answers, and the tool gets scored on how often it agrees. That's a reasonable place to start, and this paper shows where it breaks. If the partner misreads a provision, the tool that agrees with the partner gets the credit.

Picture a tax group testing a tool on whether clients qualified for a small business exemption in a transition year. The answer key comes from the same senior associate who wrote the original memos, so the test can only confirm the associate's reading. Nobody in the room would notice if that reading had drifted from what the agency actually did.

Where outcomes exist, I'd grade against them. Agency determinations and court results are records of what happened, and firms sit on years of them in closed matters. I'd also flag the transitional and effective-date provisions in every test set and weight them heavily, since that's where Bae found the outcomes moving.
