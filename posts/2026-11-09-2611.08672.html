80,465 Tasks Later: What a Hospital Learned About Rolling Out AI

A health system's AI records tool caught on fast when training came with access, which lines up with what the ABA told lawyers in 2024

Paper: arXiv:2611.08672 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08672

Supervisory obligations also include ensuring that subordinate lawyers and nonlawyers are trained, including in the ethical and practical use of the GAI tools relevant to their work as well as on risks associated with relevant GAI use.

ABA Formal Opinion 512, “Generative Artificial Intelligence Tools”, July 29, 2024

Most firms read that sentence as a compliance item. A new paper from a hospital system suggests it may be the best adoption strategy we have.

July 2024

Opinion 512 was the ABA's first formal ethics opinion on generative AI, as Bob Ambrogi noted at LawSites the day after it came out. It says lawyers need “a reasonable understanding of the capabilities and limitations of the specific GAI technology” they use, and it says they “need not become GAI experts.”

It also puts the job on firm leadership. “Managerial lawyers must establish clear policies regarding the law firm’s permissible use of GAI,” the opinion says, and supervisors have to make reasonable efforts to see that everyone follows them.

November 2026

In Real-World Adoption, Use, and Governance of an LLM-Based Electronic Health Record Search and Synthesis Platform, Jiunn-Tyng Yeh and colleagues report on Scout, a tool built into the electronic health record at a multi-hospital academic health system. It searches a patient's chart and gives answers with citations back to the record.

Over 11 months, 2,635 users ran 80,465 tasks. The system-wide launch included a required training course, and 1,768 clinical users started using Scout within the first 29 days. Daily new users jumped from 2.9 to 61.0 at launch. Of the practitioners who finished the course, 77.9% went on to use the tool, with a median of 1.7 days between finishing the course and the first task.

Use spread across more than 20 specialty categories, and 752 tasks were saved for reuse. It was also lopsided. The busiest fifth of users ran 59.0% of the tasks. The authors' conclusion is that fast, broad adoption needs clinicians designing and evaluating the tasks, along with training that's tied to getting access.

Back at the firm

That 1.7 days is the number I keep coming back to. People finished the course and started using the tool almost immediately, because the course was the door to the tool. It's easy to do it the other way, with licenses handed out one week and a lunch-and-learn three weeks later, and by then the curious people have formed their habits and the skeptical ones have moved on.

I'd make finishing the training the thing that turns on the license, the same day. And I'd find the fifth of users who'll do most of the work early, because they're the ones who should be designing the prompts and workflows for everybody else. The authors give clinicians the credit for designing and evaluating the tasks. For a law firm, that means the practice groups write and test the prompts, with the innovation team supporting them.

At your firm, does finishing the AI training turn on the tool, or does the tool show up whenever IT gets to it?
