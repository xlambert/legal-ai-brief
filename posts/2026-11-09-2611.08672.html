What 80,465 Tasks Tell Us About Rolling Out AI to Professionals

A health system's eleven months with an AI search tool reads a lot like a law firm rollout.

arXiv:2611.08672 · Read the paper

I like papers that report real usage numbers. Vendor decks give you adoption percentages with no denominator. This one gives you the denominators, the timing, and the lessons the authors drew from them.

The paper is Real-World Adoption, Use, and Governance of an LLM-Based Electronic Health Record Search and Synthesis Platform, by Jiunn-Tyng Yeh, Michael Gao, Marshall Nichols, Anika Mohan, Tareq Aljurf, Michael Revoir, Matthew Gardner, Bradley Hintze, William Ratliff, Suresh Balu, and Will Knechtle. Scout is a large language model platform built into the electronic health record. It searches and synthesizes patient records and cites what it finds. The authors followed a staged deployment across a multi-hospital academic health system for 11 months.

The numbers

In that time 2,635 users completed 80,465 tasks. Of those, 1,768 clinical users started within the first 29 days of system-wide launch. Daily initiation jumped 21.2-fold at launch, from 2.9 users per day to 61.0. Among the 495 users who started during launch week, 310 did it in the first two days.

The launch included a required training course. Among care practitioners who finished it, 77.9% went on to use Scout, with a median of 1.7 days between finishing the course and the first use. That is a short gap. People took the course and used the tool right away.

Use was concentrated. The top fifth of users accounted for 59.0% of tasks and 61.9% of tokens. The authors recorded 46,485 distinct prompts covering retrieval, synthesis, extraction, evaluation, and drafting, and 752 tasks were saved for reuse.

What I take from it

A hospital differs from a law firm in many ways, so I would read across with care. The setting has a familiar shape: busy professionals, high-stakes records, and a tool that answers with citations. I will borrow three things.

First, tie training to access. Required training that unlocks the tool gets people into it quickly. A voluntary lunch-and-learn that you hope people attend gets you a thin crowd.

Second, expect a power-user group. If a fifth of your users do 60% of the work, those people are your best source of workflow ideas and your best test group. The 752 saved tasks look like a prompt library to me, and a firm could build one from its own heavy users.

Third, size governance to reach. The authors call for traceable governance proportional to reach. A tool used by 2,600 people needs more logging, review, and support than a pilot with 20. They also list clinician-led task design and evaluation, plus centralized capacity planning and user support. Swap in lawyer-led and the list works the same way.

I also want to know who checks the answers. The authors treat evaluation as part of the design, and I agree. At a law firm that means a licensed lawyer reads a sample of outputs against the sources every month and writes down what they found. It takes time on the calendar, and it is the only way to know whether the tool is helping.

The launch spike also tells me to staff support for week one. Questions show up on day one, and a help desk that opens in week three has missed the moment.

If you tied access to your firm's AI tools to a short required course, how many days would pass before people started using them?
