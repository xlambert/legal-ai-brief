Your AI Bias Audit Has a Shelf Life

A 13-month study finds the same model, under the same name, changed its bias pattern, so last year's audit may describe a model you no longer use

Paper: arXiv:2611.20901 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20901

“We theorize that bias resides in the model class and persists, whereas audit evidence is anchored to a single model and expires when the provider decides.”

From the abstract of Mika Facchini, Auditing a Moving Target, arXiv, November 2026

New York City was one of the first places to write a law around the idea that a bias audit settles the question for a while.

New York City, July 2023

On July 5, 2023, Jonathan Kestenbaum wrote in Bloomberg Law that the city's Automated Employment Decision Tool law now required employers to “perform an annual audit of their recruitment technology.” The audit had to come from a third party. Employers also had to post the results on their public website.

Seven months later, Matt Reynolds of the ABA Journal reported that the city could fine employers “starting at $500 and up to $1,500 for subsequent violations.” He also flagged a question the law left open: “it’s unclear who qualifies as an independent auditor and the criteria or standards they would use to test the AI.” The clock, in any case, was set at once a year.

November 2026

In Auditing a Moving Target: Evidence Anchoring and the Expiry of Fairness Audits of Provider-Served Language Models, Mika Facchini tested how long that kind of evidence holds up. Models from five providers decided whether and how much to invest in startups whose founders differed only in gender, origin and university. Facchini ran the same audit three times over 13 months, for 248,400 API calls in total.

The first result should worry anyone holding a vendor's bias report. The same model, called under the same identifier a year later, showed a significantly different bias pattern. When a provider retired a model, its successor carried the same bias as its predecessor, though in a different size and form.

Facchini also read the providers' own documents. They change and retire models at their discretion, with notice periods running from zero to six months.

The paper calls its theory evidence anchoring. Audit evidence is tied to the one model that was tested and expires when the provider changes it, while the bias stays with the broader family of models. Facchini recommends auditing that family and keeping a version record for every model response, so that any model change triggers a fresh audit.

Back at the firm

Plenty of law firms now put language models in front of decisions about people. Picture a recruiting team that uses an AI tool to sort lateral résumés. The vendor hands over a clean bias report from last spring, and it goes into the procurement file.

That report may describe a model the vendor has since swapped out. I'd put two requirements into every AI contract that touches decisions about people. The vendor tells us which model version produced each output. The vendor also gives us notice when that version changes, so we can rerun our own test set before we rely on the tool again.

If a provider can retire a model with no notice at all, an annual audit cycle tells us very little. Our review schedule has to key off model changes.

When did your AI vendor last tell you which model actually produced the answer you're relying on?
