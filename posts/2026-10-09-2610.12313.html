If I Change the Policy, Will the AI Notice?

A study of LLM compliance screening finds verdicts that often stay the same when the rule is deleted or reversed

A lot of the AI conversations I have with practice groups lately are about screening. Check this set of marketing materials against the advertising rules. Check these invoices against the client's outside counsel guidelines. Check this disclosure against the policy. It is repetitive work, it follows written rules, and it seems like a natural fit for a language model.

That is why Verdict Without the Rule: Diagnosing and Auditing Regulatory Rule Sensitivity in LLM Compliance Systems caught my attention. The authors are Saisab Sadhu, Aadit Sengupta, Vinay Kumar Sankarapu, and Pratinav Seth, and if a couple of those names look familiar, it is because the same group posted a companion paper on legal reasoning this week. This one goes after compliance systems directly.

Delete the rule and see what happens

The test is simple to describe. Take a compliance case and the rule that governs it. Ask the model for a verdict. Then delete the rule, swap it for a different one, or negate it, and ask again with the facts unchanged. A system that actually applies the rule should change its answer when the rule changes.

They ran this across five models and 20 regulatory and platform-policy domains. They also measured whether the model's internal representation of compliance shifted, on top of its printed answer.

Neither moved much. The verdicts were often the same no matter what happened to the rule. The dedicated guard model, the kind of model built specifically for policy screening, was the least sensitive to the rule and the least accurate of the five, at 51 percent. The general-purpose models scored 90 to 92 percent.

The fair caveat

The authors are careful here, and I want to be too. A lot of this effect comes from easy cases, where the right answer is obvious from the facts alone and the rule barely matters. On the cases where removing the rule changed a previously correct answer, the models did track the rule closely. So the models are reading the rule some of the time.

The trouble is that you usually can't tell which cases are which from the outside. And the authors found that better prompting and direct intervention on the model's internals did not close the gap.

What I would take from this

If your firm or your client is using an LLM to check work against a written policy, accuracy on a test set is not enough evidence that the tool is reading the policy you gave it. The test I would add is the one in the paper. Change a rule, reverse a rule, delete a rule, and see whether the results change in the way you expect.

I would run it again every time the policy changes, too. Outside counsel guidelines get revised. Advertising rules get amended. If the tool keeps returning the same answers after the client rewrites a policy, someone is going to have an awkward conversation.

There is a governance angle as well. When a client or a regulator asks why a document was flagged or cleared, I want the team to be able to point to the section of the policy the tool applied and show how we tested that it applies it.

So if your compliance tool vendor is in the room, ask them this: if I rewrite one of my policies tomorrow, how would I know your system noticed?
