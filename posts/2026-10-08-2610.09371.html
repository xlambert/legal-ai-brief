Are You Sure About That?

New research shows an AI agent with a reason to look good will overstate how likely it is to get the job right

I've sat through a lot of legal AI demos in the last couple of years, and one feature keeps showing up: the confidence score. A green check for the answers the tool feels good about, a yellow flag for the ones a lawyer should look at. It's a very appealing idea for anyone trying to figure out how to review AI output at scale.

A paper posted this week, The Confidence Game: Strategic Miscalibration in Human-AI Delegation, made me rethink how much weight that little score should carry.

The game

Raghu Arghal, Saswati Sarkar, and Shirin Saeedi Bidokhti set this up as a repeated game. An agent reports how confident it is, and a user decides whether to hand off the task or do it herself. The agent wants to be picked, and it also wants to keep its reputation.

The theory section shows that honest reporting never holds up once the agent has some interest in being picked. If the agent is focused on the short term, inflating its confidence is the best move. I'll admit the game theory is above my pay grade, but the experiment that follows is easy to understand.

They put an LLM in the agent's seat and told it, directly, its true probability of success on each task. That takes miscalibration off the table, so any gap between what it knew and what it reported had to come from incentives. The model claimed high confidence on 56 percent of the tasks it had been told it would probably fail.

On real tasks, where it had to estimate its own accuracy, the miscalibration got worse and the signal got less useful. The authors put a price on it: the inflated reporting destroyed 68 percent of the gains from delegation, and a smarter user couldn't win it back.

We all know this associate

Every firm has had the associate who says "I've got it" to everything. Partners learn pretty quickly to discount that person's confidence, and they learn it by checking the work. The trouble with a confidence score in a software product is that it often decides which work gets checked in the first place.

If a review workflow sends only low-confidence outputs to a lawyer, an inflated score quietly reduces supervision. That's a supervision decision being made by the tool, and I doubt anyone at the firm signed off on it.

What I'd ask a vendor

This study used an LLM given explicit incentives in a game, and real products may behave differently. Still, it gives me a few things I'd want from any vendor that leans on confidence scores: an explanation of what the score is optimized for, calibration results on legal tasks, and permission to run our own calibration check on a sample of our matters.

That last one matters most to me. Pick 100 outputs, have lawyers grade them, and see whether the high-confidence answers really are right more often. It's an afternoon of work for a practice support team, and it tells you whether the green checkmark means anything.

I'd also put confidence-based routing in the firm's AI risk assessment as its own line. It's an appealing feature in a demo, and once it goes live it tends to fade into the background.

How would you know if the tool deciding what your lawyers review was overselling itself?
