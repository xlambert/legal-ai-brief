Show Your Work... and the AI Grader Goes Easy on You

A new study finds AI judges accept wrong answers more often when an explanation comes along for the ride.

There's an old law school joke that you can get partial credit on any exam answer as long as you write "it depends" and then spend two pages explaining why. I never tested that theory on a real professor (I liked passing). But a new paper suggests the AI graders we are all starting to rely on might fall for a version of it.

"A Plausible Explanation Is Enough to Mislead Multimodal Judges" by Anglin Cai and colleagues builds a benchmark called JUPE, where the answer to a visual question stays the same and the only thing that changes is the explanation attached to it. Then the team put 14 different multimodal AI judges through it.

Here's what they found. When a wrong answer came with an ordinary, reasonable-sounding explanation, the judges were much more willing to accept it. A plain justification cut the rate at which judges rejected wrong answers by 12.7 percentage points. A chain-of-thought rationale cut it by 13.8. Those everyday explanations worked about as well as explanations deliberately written to persuade. The authors call this "plausibility capture."

The part that caught my eye is the signal detection analysis. The judges' basic ability to tell good answers from bad ones stayed about where it was, and their threshold for saying yes dropped. They got more generous. Anyone who has graded papers at 11 p.m. knows that feeling.

Why this matters for legal AI

If you've sat through a legal AI demo in the last year, you've seen a benchmark slide. A lot of those numbers come from one LLM grading another LLM's answers. That's a reasonable way to score thousands of responses, and I'm not knocking it. Plenty of firms use automated graders in their pilots, because nobody has the attorney hours to hand-grade everything.

Almost every legal AI tool now gives you its reasoning along with its answer. That's a feature we asked for. We want to see the work. This paper says that if the grader also sees the work, the grade probably drifts up.

So I have a new question for the next vendor meeting: when you scored this, did your judge see the explanation? And did you run it both ways?

I don't think that's an unfair question. It's the same thing we'd ask about any test. Who graded it, and what did they get to look at?

The human version of the same problem

I'll admit people do this too. I've watched plenty of smart lawyers read a confident memo with a clean, well-organized rationale and nod right along. Associates figure this out fast. A good-looking explanation buys a lot of goodwill with a busy reviewer.

That's part of why the paper is useful for training. It puts a number on a bias most of us know from experience. When we teach lawyers how to review AI output, "the explanation sounds right" belongs on the list of things that should make you slow down a little. The supervision rules (Model Rules 5.1 and 5.3) still apply when the first reviewer is software, and they still apply when the second reviewer is software too.

What I'd do with this

A few practical thoughts. If your firm uses an AI judge for quality control on drafts or research answers, test it with and without the model's reasoning visible. If the scores move, you've learned something important about your QC process. If you're comparing vendors, ask for scores where the judge only sees the final answer. And when you're the human in the loop, read the answer first and the explanation second.

The authors tried some mitigations, and they helped a bit on some models. Nothing made the problem go away. That tells me this will be with us for a while, and a lot of the work falls on how we design our own review process.

Has anyone at your firm checked whether the AI quality scores you rely on change when the grader can see the model's reasoning?
