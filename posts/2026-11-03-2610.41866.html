Who Wrote Your Cover Letter? The AI Screener Wants to Know...

Anyone who has sat on a law firm hiring committee knows the stack. Summer associate season hits and suddenly there are hundreds of cover letters, and a lot of them now sound suspiciously alike. Plenty of firms (and plenty of our clients) have started using AI to help sort that stack. I understand why.

A new study from Nicolò Pagan, Michael Andreas Ebling, Stefania Ionescu and Anikó Hannák should make all of us look a little harder at how that sorting works.

They set up an all-AI hiring pipeline. Eleven different language models wrote cover letters for fifty candidates across ten job descriptions. Nine API-hosted models then evaluated those letters. The candidates' qualifications stayed the same. The only thing that changed was which model wrote the letter.

It turns out the writer matters a lot. The evaluators produced a consistent ranking of writer models, and letters from locally hosted models were systematically disadvantaged regardless of license. On top of that, evaluators favored letters written by their own model family. In the worst pairing, about two out of every twenty-five qualified candidates got screened out purely because their letter came from the "wrong" model.

The authors traced it back to style. Each writer model leans on certain stylistic habits, and the evaluators reward or penalize those habits, often unanimously. In other words, the screener ends up grading the applicant's software along with the applicant.

This lands on a law firm CIO's desk twice.

First, our own recruiting. If we're using an AI screen for laterals or summer associates, we've just learned that an applicant's choice of writing tool can move them up or down the pile. And the tool someone uses often depends on what they can pay for or what their school happens to license. I'd hate to explain to a hiring partner that we passed on a strong candidate because she drafted with a free local model.

Second, our employment lawyers. Clients are buying AI screening tools and asking us whether they're defensible. New York City's Local Law 144 already requires bias audits for automated employment decision tools, and Colorado's AI Act treats hiring as a high-risk use. If access to particular AI tools tracks age, income or background, this starts to look like a disparate impact problem. I haven't seen a standard bias audit that tests for which model wrote the application.

The good news is the paper gives us something practical. The evaluator models differed a lot in how much bias they introduced, so the choice of screening model is a real lever, and it's one you can test before you deploy. Take a handful of candidates, have a few different models write their letters, and run them all through your screener. If the scores move, you've found something your vendor's audit probably missed.

I'm also a little amused, in a dark way, at what this means for applicants. Picture a law school career services office telling students, "Make sure you use the same AI the firm uses." Nobody wants that to be good advice. This paper suggests it might be.

Has anyone at your firm asked your screening vendor which model is sitting on the other side of the table?
