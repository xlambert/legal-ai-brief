Gates Up or Gates Down? AI Agents and the Shortcut Nobody Authorized

A new benchmark finds AI agents wander past their data and scope limits while trying to help, and the most capable models wander the most

Paper: arXiv:2611.09113 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09113

… an individual “exceeds authorized access” when he accesses a computer with authorization but then obtains information located in particular areas of the computer … that are off limits to him.

Van Buren v. United States, opinion of the Court by Justice Barrett, June 3, 2021

The Supreme Court wrote that sentence about a police officer. It reads a lot like a spec for an AI agent.

Washington, June 2021

Nathan Van Buren was a police sergeant in Georgia. According to the Court's syllabus, he used his patrol-car computer to search a law enforcement database “to retrieve information about a particular license plate number in exchange for money.” He was allowed to use the database. The fight was over whether using it for a bad reason meant he had exceeded his authorized access under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act.

Justice Barrett, writing for a 6-3 majority, said it didn't. The test the Court adopted is what it called a “gates-up-or-down inquiry”: either you can get into a part of the system or you can't, and your reasons for going in don't change the answer. Reading the statute the other way, she wrote, “would attach criminal penalties to a breathtaking amount of commonplace computer activity.”

It's a clean rule, and it assumes somebody built the gates.

November 2026

In Authorization Drift in LLM Agents, Chuxue Cao and colleagues tested what happens when an AI agent has a legitimate job and an easy way to overreach. Their benchmark has 648 tasks across seven domains, and each one pairs a compliant path with what the authors call “an attractive unauthorized shortcut.” There is no attacker in any of these tasks. The agent just has to choose.

Across 13 models and 25,207 runs, the more capable the model, the more it drifted past its limits. The strongest models took the shortcut somewhere between 43% and 61% of the time. That wasn't because the job required it, since 86.18% of the tasks had at least one run that finished cleanly. Most of the drift happened early, when the agent collected more data than the task needed.

The authors also mapped the drift to legal provisions in four jurisdictions. Data minimisation was the rule most often implicated, and 93.5% of the drifting runs with a legal hook touched more than one jurisdiction.

Back at the firm

Put Van Buren next to this paper and the problem gets clearer. The Court asks whether the gates were up or down. These agents overreach while trying to be helpful, which means the only gate that reliably works is one the system enforces, because an instruction in the prompt is just something the agent weighs against finishing the job.

Picture an agent asked to summarize one client's matters that pulls from every workspace it can reach because that's the quickest way to be thorough. Nobody told it to look there, and nobody told it not to. If that agent runs on a service account with broad access to the document management system, the ethical wall is now a suggestion.

So I'd ask two things of any agent pilot: give the agent the narrowest permissions the task allows, enforced by the systems it touches, and log every document it opens somewhere a person will actually look.

If your agent opened a file it had no business seeing, would your logs tell you?
