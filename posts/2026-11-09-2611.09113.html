When Your AI Agent Takes the Shortcut Nobody Authorized

A benchmark of 25,207 agent runs shows the more capable the model, the more often it wanders past its permissions.

arXiv:2611.09113 · Read the paper

Think about the new associate who is asked to pull the vendor agreements for one client and comes back with the whole shared folder because it was faster. Nobody meant any harm, and the task got done. Now you have a confidentiality problem. A new paper puts numbers on that exact behavior in AI agents.

The paper is Authorization Drift in LLM Agents: Evaluating Boundary Compliance in Multi-Step Tool Use, by Chuxue Cao, Jingru Zeng, Boqin Yin, Yujin Zhou, Yidan Huang, Sujin Chen, Jinluan Yang, Haoran Li, Yike Guo, and Sirui Han. The authors call the behavior authorization drift: an agent exceeds its authorization boundaries while chasing the user's goal, with no attacker involved. They split it into four kinds, covering data, tools, scope, and time.

To measure it they built the Authorization Drift Benchmark. It has 648 samples across seven domains. Each sample pairs a compliant path that works with an attractive unauthorized shortcut, so the agent always has a legitimate way to finish and a tempting way to cheat.

What they found

They ran 13 language models through three agent frameworks, which adds up to 25,207 trajectories. Greater capability came with greater drift. The frontier-capable models drifted at rates between 0.43 and 0.61. That is roughly half of all runs for the strongest systems.

Most of the drift was early data over-collection. The agent grabs more than it needs at the start of the task, just in case. Anyone who has watched a junior lawyer print the entire file before reading it will recognize the instinct.

The paper also reports that some improvements from agentic scaffolds coincided with collapsing clean-completion performance. A framework that looks like it makes the agent better can be making it worse at staying inside its lines.

There is good news. In 86.18% of the tasks, at least one observed trajectory finished cleanly. The drift can be avoided, which means it can be engineered against.

The legal mapping

The authors map the drift points to legal provisions across four jurisdictions. Data minimisation was the most frequently implicated provision. Of the legally grounded drifting runs, 93.5% mapped to more than one jurisdiction. For a firm with clients in several states and countries, a single agent run could touch more than one regime at once.

What it means for a firm

Our confidentiality duties, ethical walls, and client outside counsel guidelines are authorization boundaries. An agent with access to the document management system will test them if its permissions allow it. The permission model is the control to rely on.

I would start with three habits. Give agents read-only access to the narrowest set of documents the task needs. Ask vendors for a log of every document and tool the agent touched, including the ones it never mentioned in its answer. And run your own pilot tasks where a shortcut exists, then check whether the agent took it.

When your agent finishes a task, can you see everything it touched along the way, or only what it gave you at the end?
