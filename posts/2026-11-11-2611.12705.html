Three Agents Agreed. So What Did They Miss?

A short theory paper says the only way to know what AI screeners missed is the check Judge Peck approved in 2012: sample the discard pile

Paper: arXiv:2611.12705 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12705

After the seventh round, to determine if the computer is well trained and stable, MSL would review a random sample (of 2,399 documents) from the discards (i.e., documents coded as nonrelevant) to make sure the documents determined by the software to not be relevant do not, in fact, contain highly-relevant documents.

Da Silva Moore v. Publicis Groupe, S.D.N.Y., Magistrate Judge Andrew J. Peck, February 24, 2012

That's a protocol from the first court opinion to approve computer-assisted review. Fourteen years later, a new paper argues that this one step is still the only thing that tells you what the machines missed.

New York, February 2012

The case involved more than three million emails. Judge Peck didn't mince words about the alternative: “Linear manual review is simply too expensive where, as here, there are over three million emails to review.” He wrote that his opinion “appears to be the first in which a Court has approved of the use of computer-assisted review.”

The protocol he approved trained the software in rounds and then checked the pile the software had thrown out. By Rio Tinto v. Vale in 2015, he was calling it “black letter law that where the producing party wants to utilize TAR for document review, courts will permit it.”

November 2026

Technology-assisted review has since grown into AI agents that screen documents for a human reviewer. In Multi-Agent Screening: What Agreement and Acceptance Cannot Tell You, Benjamin Chang and Dell Zhang ask what each kind of evidence can prove about those agents' mistakes, even with unlimited data. Recall, the share of the relevant documents a review actually finds, is the number that matters here.

Their answers are blunt. When several agents agree, all you've learned is that they concur, however many agents you add. When the human accepts what the agents pass, you learn precision, but recall only gets a floor. Knowing which agent found which document can put a ceiling on recall, but only if you assume the agents' finds aren't negatively correlated.

Without assumptions about the agents, the only way to pin down recall is to review documents the agents didn't pass. A random sample of the discards estimates how many relevant documents were missed, to whatever accuracy you choose, and the sample you need doesn't grow as you add agents.

Back at the firm

Multi-agent review is going to be sold with agreement as the quality check: three agents looked at every document and they all concurred. This paper says plainly that agreement can't do that job. The check that does work is the one Judge Peck built into the Da Silva Moore protocol.

The good news is the cost. Because the sample doesn't grow with the number of agents, a firm can add agents for speed without paying more to validate them. I'd make a discard sample a standard line in every AI review protocol we sign, and I'd want the vendor's recall number to come from it.

When your review vendor reports recall, did anyone pull a sample from what the agents threw away?
