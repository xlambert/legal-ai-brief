Three Agents Agreed. So What Did They Miss?

A short theory paper makes the case for sampling the discard pile in agentic e-discovery review.

I've started hearing a new pitch in review demos. Instead of one model coding documents, the vendor runs several agents, and when they agree, the result gets treated as high confidence. It sounds reasonable. If three smart reviewers all say a document isn't relevant, it probably isn't. Right?

A paper out this week by Benjamin Chang and Dell Zhang, Multi-Agent Screening: What Agreement and Acceptance Cannot Tell You, takes that idea apart in a clean, mathematical way. It's short, it's theoretical, and anyone who has negotiated a TAR protocol should read it.

The setup

The authors look at any situation where AI agents screen items before a costly human assessor looks at them. They name e-discovery, agentic search, and AI-assisted paper review as examples. The human only judges what the agents pass along. The question is what you can actually know about the agents' errors from the evidence you have, even with unlimited data.

What each kind of evidence tells you

Agreement among agents shows that they agree. That's it. Adding more agents doesn't change that, and agents built on similar models can easily share the same blind spots.

The assessor accepting what the agents pass tells you about precision. It gives you only a lower bound on recall. You know about the relevant documents you found. You have no idea how many you missed.

If you know which agents found which relevant items, you can get an upper bound on recall, as long as the agents' finds aren't negatively correlated. If the agents are interchangeable, that same information also caps how much one more agent can add.

Then comes the part anyone from the TAR world will recognize. Without assumptions about the agents, the only way to pin down recall is to judge items the agents did not pass. A random sample of the discard pile estimates how many relevant items were missed, to whatever accuracy you want, and the sample you need doesn't grow with the number of agents.

We've been here before

This is elusion testing. Courts and litigants spent years working out how to validate technology-assisted review, and sampling the null set became a standard part of defending a protocol. My worry is that agentic review shows up with a fresh vocabulary (consensus, confidence, multi-agent verification) and people assume the old validation questions no longer apply.

This paper says they apply exactly as before. If anything, they matter more, because a stack of agreeing agents looks very convincing in a demo.

What I'd tell our litigation support team

Keep sampling the discard pile. When a vendor shows you agent agreement rates, ask what elusion testing looks like on top of that. When you draft an ESI protocol that allows agentic review, write the sampling into it.

I'd also bring this into training. It's a concrete way to teach newer lawyers the difference between precision and recall, and to show why a pile of good documents tells you nothing about the ones you never saw.

The authors don't run experiments here, since it's a theory paper. I'm fine with that. The value is in having a clear argument we can hand to opposing counsel or a special master when the question comes up.

So here's what I keep wondering. When agentic review starts showing up in front of judges, how long before someone asks to see the elusion sample?
