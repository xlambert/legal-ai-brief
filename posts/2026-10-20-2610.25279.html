Does the Lawyer in the Loop Make the Same Mistakes as the AI?

A health care paper gives law firms a way to put human review to the test.

Pretty much every law firm AI policy I've read has some version of the same sentence. "A lawyer must review all AI-generated work product before it is used." Ours does too. It's the right instinct, and it's also the sentence we lean on whenever someone asks how we know the AI output is safe to use.

So I paid attention when I came across Complementary Vulnerabilities: Auditing and Governing Clinician-LLM Error Overlap by Mohsen Amoei, Kathleen Fraser, and Dan Poenaru. It's written for health systems, but swap "clinician" for "associate" and it reads like it was written for us.

Their argument is simple. Adding an AI as a second check only reduces errors to the extent the AI and the human fail on different cases. If they both stumble on the same tricky fact patterns, the second check adds very little. They call the good version "complementary vulnerabilities," where the human and the model break down for different reasons on different cases.

Then they point out how fragile that is. The independence wears away when the clinician starts deferring to the model. It also wears away when the model is tuned or prompted to agree with the clinician. Either way, you end up with two reviewers who miss the same things.

Sound familiar?

I've watched this happen with cite-checking tools for years. The first few weeks, people check everything. A few months in, if the tool has been right a hundred times in a row, the checking gets lighter. That's human nature, and it's exactly the deference the authors are worried about.

Now think about a junior associate reviewing an AI-drafted research memo on an area of law they've never touched. Are they likely to catch the unusual statutory exception the model glossed over? Or are they going to miss it for the same reason the model did, because it's buried and nobody flagged it?

A test we could actually run

The part of the paper I like most is the practical proposal. Before deployment, each site measures error overlap on its own cases. Then it keeps measuring over time.

For a firm, that could look like this. Pick a set of closed matters where we know the right answer. Have a few lawyers do a task without AI. Have the AI do the same task. Then compare which issues each one missed. If the misses barely overlap, human review is adding real value. If they overlap a lot, we've learned that our review step needs to be redesigned for that kind of work, maybe with a checklist that targets the model's known blind spots.

Do it again six months after rollout, when people have gotten comfortable, and see whether the overlap has grown.

None of this is expensive. It's a pilot design question, and it gives the AI governance committee real numbers to work with when it signs off on a tool.

I'll admit I don't know what we'd find. My guess is that the overlap is higher than we'd like on routine tasks and lower on judgment-heavy work, and that it creeps up as people trust the tool more. I'd like to find out with data.

Has anyone at your firm actually measured what your human review catches?
