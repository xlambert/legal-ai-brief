Copilot Made People Talk More? Huh.

A 12,000-person study of a staged AI rollout, and what it means for how law firms measure adoption

I'll admit my bias up front. When we started putting generative AI tools in front of lawyers, one of my quiet worries was that it would make the junior folks more isolated. Why walk down the hall and ask a senior associate how to approach a motion when the chatbot will give you an outline in ten seconds?

A new paper on arXiv suggests I may have been worrying about the wrong thing. The Effects of Generative AI on Collaboration and Social Networks Among Knowledge Workers, by Giuseppe Soda, Alessandro Iorio, and Leonardo Rizzo, looks at more than 12,000 knowledge workers over 13 months while their organization rolled out Microsoft Copilot in stages.

What they found

The data here is the kind we rarely get. It's passively collected behavioral data on how people actually communicated, across channels, before and after they got access. Because the rollout was staged, some groups got Copilot earlier than others. That gives the researchers a natural experiment: compare people who had the tool to similar people who didn't have it yet. They used two different statistical designs to do that, which makes me trust the results more than a single model would.

The result? People using generative AI spent more time collaborating. They interacted with more colleagues, and their networks got less clustered, meaning they reached outside their usual little group more often.

Then comes the wrinkle I find most interesting. Those same users concentrated more of their collaboration on people they already had strong working relationships with. They spread out, and they also leaned harder on the colleagues they already trusted.

The law firm version of this

The authors suggest the reason is that AI output needs interpretation, verification, and someone to vouch for it. That rings true to me. I can picture exactly how this plays out at a firm. An associate gets a first draft of a research memo from an AI tool. Before it goes anywhere near a client, they take it to the partner or senior associate they already trust and ask, "Does this look right to you?"

Multiply that by a few hundred associates and you can see the problem. The review work flows toward a small number of trusted people, and those people probably don't know they've become the firm's informal AI quality control desk.

That has real consequences. It's where supervision under the professional conduct rules actually happens. It's also a capacity problem. If three senior associates in a practice group are quietly checking everybody's AI output, that's time nobody planned for and nobody is billing correctly.

What I'd measure

Most of our adoption dashboards count licenses activated, active users, and prompts per week. Those numbers are fine, but they tell us almost nothing about how work is changing. This paper is a nudge to look at the people around the users. Who is getting more questions? Whose calendar filled up after the rollout reached their group?

I'll add the caveats. This is one organization, using one general-purpose tool, and it isn't a law firm. More collaboration time could also mean more meetings, and nobody I know is asking for more meetings. But a staged rollout across 12,000 people is a much better source of evidence than the vendor case studies we usually get.

If your firm staged its rollout, have you looked at what happened to the people sitting next to the users?
