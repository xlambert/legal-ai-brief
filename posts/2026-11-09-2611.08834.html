Your Prompt Injection Guard Has Never Read a Contract

A new paper tests eleven security guards on legal contracts, and most of them flag clean agreements as attacks.

arXiv:2611.08834 · Read the paper

When I saw the title of this paper, I laughed out loud. "Notwithstanding Anything to the Contrary, Ignore All Instructions" is the best title I have seen on arXiv this year, and the findings deserve a close read from anyone who buys contract review technology.

The paper is Notwithstanding Anything to the Contrary, Ignore All Instructions: Measuring Prompt Injection Guards on Legal Contract Review Attacks, by Mark Heimann, Katherine Taylor, and Bruno Jacob. Here is the setup. Vendors put a specialized guard in front of a document analysis system. The guard's job is to catch text hidden inside a document that tries to give the AI new orders. Picture a line buried in a contract that tells the model to ignore its prior instructions and report the agreement as clean. That is a fair thing to worry about when your tools read documents that came from the other side of a deal.

What they tested

The authors point out that nobody had published a false positive rate for any of these guards on legal contracts. So they ran eleven open-weights guards over two collections of contracts. The results are rough. Document-level false positive rates went as high as 92.39%. A guard with that rate flags more than nine out of ten ordinary contracts as attacks. Two guards scored below chance on paired AUC. And a baseline built from five regular expressions was not beaten across the board by any guard in the group.

Five regexes held their own against purpose-built security products. I would want that sentence read aloud at the next vendor demo.

My guess about the cause: contracts are packed with language that reads like a command. "Notwithstanding anything to the contrary." "The Receiving Party shall." "Seller must disregard." A guard trained to treat imperative sentences as red flags sees a contract and panics. The authors ran controlled probes and found both model-specific and general failure modes, so the story has more layers than my guess, but the shape fits what I would expect.

The part that worries me more

The paper also tests a second threat category called unauthorized scope claims. That means text in a document that claims authority nobody gave it. Every guard in the panel scored zero. Zero is a tough number to explain to a client.

The authors do offer a way forward. Prompted LLMs resolved the failures they tested. They also built a cascade where a mid-tier model's positives get checked by a frontier model. That setup runs 6.5 times cheaper than using a frontier model alone and comes close to its performance. For a firm paying per document, that cost point matters.

What I would do Monday morning

I would ask each contract review vendor three questions. What guard sits in front of the feature? What is its false positive rate, measured on contracts? What happens to the lawyer's workflow when it fires?

The third question matters most. If a guard flags nine of ten contracts, lawyers will learn to click past the warning within a week. After that, the one real attack gets clicked past along with everything else. A security control that people ignore protects nobody, and it adds friction to every review.

I would also ask whether the vendor tests for scope claims at all. Some of our counterparties will be sending us documents with creative formatting, and it is better to find out how the tool behaves before a client's deal depends on it.

Has anyone asked a vendor for a false positive rate on contracts yet, and what did they say?
