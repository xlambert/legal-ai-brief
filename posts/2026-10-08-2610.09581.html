What Did the Agent Rely On?

A study of AI-reconstructed agent logs shows a right answer can point to the wrong record

Ask anyone in litigation support which question from a partner they dread most, and I'd bet a good number of them say "where did this come from?" Finding a fact is usually easy enough. Showing the exact document, page, and version that supports it, months later and after three people have touched the file, is the hard part.

That question is about to get a lot more complicated as firms start running AI agents that take actions on matters. A new paper, Correct Answers, Unsupported Findings: Evidence Binding in Forensic Reconstruction of LLM Agent Logs, tests what happens when you ask an AI to answer it about another AI.

The setup

The researchers took 64 saved agent runs from AgentDojo, a test environment where an agent handles banking tasks. Each run left behind tool logs, the agent's own explanations, and citation identifiers. They then asked two LLMs to reconstruct which logged record supported each finding, and varied how much of the evidence the readers could see.

Here's the result that made me sit up. With the original identifiers and no table linking citation IDs to the underlying records, Sonnet found every literal source location. But in 26 of the 28 cases that required tying a citation to its record, it made a claim about the source that the evidence didn't support. And 22 of those answers still matched the reference answer.

Read that again. If you only graded the final facts, you'd have given the AI a near-perfect score. It got the right answer while pointing at support it couldn't actually establish.

Lawyers have seen this before

This is the brief that cites a real case for a proposition the case doesn't hold. The conclusion might even be correct. Every judge and senior partner I know treats that as a problem anyway.

The fix the researchers found is pretty unglamorous. Preserve explicit bindings between each citation identifier and the record it points to. When they added those bindings, grounded reconstruction improved for both models. Simply renaming the identifiers to make them clearer didn't reliably help. And a plain deterministic comparator, with no LLM at all, either got the bounded task right or declined to answer every single time.

I love that last finding, mostly because it's the kind of boring tool you can explain to a client.

Bring the records people into the room

If your firm is piloting agents that file, send, docket, or update matter records, logging is going to come up, usually as a checkbox asking whether the system keeps logs. This paper points to better questions: whether each tool call and citation can be tied to a specific preserved record, who writes those records, and whether the agent itself can change them.

Our records management and KM folks have been thinking about chain of custody for a long time. This is their territory, and they should be part of the conversation when agent logging gets designed. I'd much prefer they shape it at the start than get handed a pile of logs after something goes wrong.

The paper is narrow (64 cases in one banking simulation), and the authors say so. But I expect the pattern to show up anywhere we ask a machine to explain what another machine did. Malpractice reviews, client audits, and discovery requests about a firm's own use of AI are all on the way.

When a client asks you to show what your agent relied on, will your logs let you answer?
