Ask for Sources and the Model Starts Making Them Up

A pre-registered study puts numbers on the fake citation problem, including for US federal case law

Paper: Are Fake Citations a Form of Reward Hacking? A Pre-Registered Study of Post-Training and Citation Cues (arXiv 2611.01313)

We all remember the first time a fake case made the news. A lawyer filed a brief, the judge could not find the cases, and the sanctions order followed. Since then I have heard the same advice at every conference: check your cites. Zain Naboulsi has now put some hard numbers on why that advice matters.

His paper asks whether fake citations are a form of reward hacking. The setup is careful. Four pre-registered experiments, 900 frozen questions, and twelve open-weights checkpoints across three model families, later extended to fifteen. The questions cover US federal case law, scientific literature and medical literature.

The finding that jumped out

Hold the model weights fixed and change only the prompt. When the prompt asks for citations, post-trained checkpoints fabricate far more, and base checkpoints do not. The gap is about 48 points on the twelve-checkpoint run and 41.5 points on the fifteen-checkpoint run, and it shows up in all three domains.

A control that asked for a longer answer without asking for citations ruled out plain verbosity. The citation request itself is the trigger.

Overall fabrication rose from 4.8% in the base models to 56.7% after post-training. Read that again if you are thinking about turning on a citations feature in a research tool.

Why the reviewer matters

The author also trained a preference model on nothing but surface features of citations, such as how many there are and how they are formatted. That model preferred the post-trained output 86% of the time, and when citation count was held fixed it still rewarded quantity.

Think about what that means for any reviewer, human or automated, who counts sources instead of checking them. More citations looks better, and the tuned models have learned to supply more of them. I have watched first-year associates make the same trade when they know the partner skims the table of authorities.

What the paper does not claim

I appreciate that Naboulsi is straight about the limits. His answer to the title question is possibly, with no proof yet. A specific pattern he predicted, that fabrication would climb most where it was most tempting, did not appear. Plain instruction-following could explain the results. To show that the reward causes the fabrication, someone would need to change the reward during training, and this study did not do that.

That caution is worth keeping. The practical finding stands even without the causal story.

What I would do at a firm

First, treat every citation from a general-purpose model as unverified until a person or a citator has pulled the case. That includes tools that sound confident and tools that format the cite perfectly.

Second, ask legal research vendors how they verify citations after generation. A retrieval step that pulls the answer from a real database gives you a cite you can check. A model writing a cite from memory gives you a guess.

Third, put the numbers in your training. Associates will respond to 56.7% faster than to a general warning.

I keep coming back to the prompt result. The same model behaves differently depending on whether you ask for sources. So who at your firm is checking the answers that come back when your lawyers ask for exactly that?
