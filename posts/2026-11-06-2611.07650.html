Discount Models, Mystery Tokens: Do You Know What Your AI Reseller Is Delivering?

Researchers tested 20 third-party API relays and found different answers, tool calls nobody asked for, and padded token counts.

arXiv:2611.07650 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07650

Somewhere in your firm, a developer, a data analyst, or a very curious associate has found a cheaper way to reach a frontier model. It is a third-party service that resells access, usually at a discount, and it works just fine for the weekend project. I would bet money that at least one of those exists at a firm near you.

Xiaodong Wu and colleagues set out to check what those services deliver. Their paper, What the Middleman Delivers: Answer Behavior, Tool Permissions, and Billed Usage in LLM API Relays, tests 20 API relays that claim to offer GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5. They looked at three things users depend on: the quality of the answers, whether the service follows instructions about tool use, and whether the bill matches the usage.

The answers

The team compared relay answers to knowledge questions against batch-matched answers from the official source. In the first round, their analysis flagged behavioral differences in 7 of 10 low-price relays and 2 of 10 relays priced the same or higher. When they retested 35 pairs, seven gained or lost a flag under the original rules. The authors say plainly that service evaluations need repeated measurements, and I appreciate that honesty. One test run proves little.

The permissions

They also screened 3,332 responses to a fixed, non-adversarial code and setup workload and confirmed no injected content. That part is good news. The less comforting part is that four relays returned tool calls even though the instructions disabled them. If your agent setup relies on a tool being switched off, a relay that ignores the switch matters a great deal.

The bill

For identical requests, five relays reported between 4,200 and 60,000 more input tokens than the official API. Six services let the researchers check balances, and in those the charges followed the reported usage at the listed price. One service charged for the extra reported input tokens. Anyone who has reviewed a vendor invoice knows that look: the rate is right and the quantity is somebody's guess.

Why this lands on a law firm

I have three concerns, and none of them needs a dramatic breach to be real. First, confidentiality. A relay sits between your prompt and the model, so client data passes through a company your outside-counsel guidelines never mentioned. Second, supervision. If a relay quietly serves a different model, the quality of work that someone relied on changes without anyone knowing. Third, billing. If AI costs get passed through to a client matter, a padded token count turns into a padded invoice.

The advice that comes out of this is plain. Keep an inventory of every endpoint your people use for model access, including the ones that were set up as experiments. Require an approved list. Run a small repeatable test against any new provider and repeat it on a schedule, since the paper shows single snapshots can mislead.

Do you know every route your firm's prompts take to reach a model today? If the answer takes more than a minute to figure out, that is where I would start.
