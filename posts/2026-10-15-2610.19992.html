Who Graded the Robot's Bar Exam?

A new paper on LLM grading of legal answers hands us a checklist for every legal AI benchmark claim

I've lost count of the vendor decks I've seen this year with a slide that says something like "Our model scored 87% on [insert legal benchmark here]." Somebody in the room always nods. I usually raise my hand and ask the annoying librarian question: who graded it?

Most of the time, the answer is another AI. And most of the time, the person presenting has no idea how that grading was set up.

So I was happy to see a new paper from Sebastian Nagl on arXiv this week, SoK: Grading Open-Text Legal Answers, from Law School Exams to Trustworthy Rubric Judges. It's a systematization paper, which is academic for "I read everything so you don't have to." And it goes straight at the question I keep asking.

What the Paper Looked At

Law students get tested with open-text answers. Case solutions, essays, memos. Anyone who has ever graded a legal writing assignment knows these are expensive to grade, and human graders disagree with each other a lot. LLMs now offer to grade these at scale, both in teaching and in the benchmarks that test legal AI tools.

Nagl pulls eight requirements from educational measurement and from the law that governs exams. Validity. Reliability. Reasons for the grade. Credit for defensible alternative answers. Resistance to gaming. Human oversight. Then he lines up eight ways of grading, from a professor with a red pen and no rubric all the way to LLM judges with rubrics written for each question, and checks each one against those requirements.

Three Things That Jumped Out at Me

First, a rubric written for the specific task is the strongest lever for getting an LLM judge to agree with human graders. That makes sense to anyone who has ever built a grading guide. The catch is that the agreement numbers people report answer different questions, and they rarely come with the human-to-human agreement you'd need to read them against. If two professors only agree with each other 70% of the time, what does 80% agreement between a model and one professor really tell you?

Second, the weakest evidence sits right where exam law asks the most. Alternative solutions. Follow-on errors (you blew the first issue, but your analysis of the second one was right given that mistake). A human examiner correcting the model's call. Those are the places where legal analysis gets interesting, and they are where the validation is thinnest.

Third, rubric-based grading is the most auditable approach, because you can check the standard, how it was applied, and the final decision.

Why a Law Firm Should Care

You might be thinking this is a law school problem. I'd push back on that. Every time a firm evaluates a legal AI tool, someone is grading open-text legal answers. Sometimes that's a practice group partner reading twenty memos. Lately it's just as likely to be a vendor's LLM judge producing a number for a slide.

Nagl's version of a "trustworthy rubric judge" makes a pretty good checklist for the next demo. Ask for the rubric, and see whether it gives credit for a defensible alternative answer. Ask whether the judge was calibrated against independent human graders. Ask whether the runs were repeated and logged. And ask whether a human examiner actually read every answer.

The same goes for our own internal testing. When my team runs a bake-off between two tools, we're building a little benchmark, whether we call it that or not. If we let a model grade the outputs, we owe our lawyers the same discipline we'd ask of a vendor.

The Training Angle

There's also a training angle here. Firms are starting to use AI to give feedback on associate writing. That's a grading exercise with real consequences for someone's development, and maybe their review. The same safeguards apply, and I'd want a partner reading the work alongside the model.

I'll admit I'm a bit biased. Librarians have been asking "how do you know that?" for a very long time, and it's nice to see a paper hand us some better follow-up questions.

The next time a vendor puts a benchmark score on the screen, are you going to ask who graded it?
