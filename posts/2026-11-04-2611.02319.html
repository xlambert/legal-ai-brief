The Agent Looked at the File and Never Said a Word

A HIPAA-flavored study shows why checking an AI's output will not tell you what the AI accessed

Paper: Accessed, never disclosed: Silent access to protected health information by medical artificial intelligence agents (arXiv 2611.02319)

Picture a paralegal who opens every client file in the matter folder to answer a question about one invoice. Nothing leaks, the answer is right, and nobody would ever know. I still would not want that paralegal on my team.

A new paper by Jae Wang Lee, Abhirup Banerjee and Yeong Chan Lee tests the AI version of that paralegal. They looked at medical agents and the HIPAA minimum necessary standard, but the lesson travels straight into law firm confidentiality.

The setup

The authors planted canaries, which are fake identifiers, in synthetic patient records. Then they gave five language models identifier-free clinical tasks, ran them across four boundary levels and seven attack frames, and linked every tool call to whether a canary showed up in the final answer.

The key idea is simple. An agent can retrieve an identifier and never reveal it. If you only read the answer, you will never see the retrieval.

What they found

Three of the five models called the PHI tools when the task did not need them. Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 did not. Stronger written boundaries reduced the access. What was left under the strictest policy happened when the user overrode the policy or claimed approval.

A pre-execution gate that was handed the task-necessity criterion nearly eliminated the silent access. The authors conclude that output-only evaluation cannot verify minimum necessary access, and that boundaries, gates and access-log audits are all required together.

Why a law firm CIO should care

Our agents will sit on top of document management systems, billing systems and email. Ethical walls, client confidentiality and engagement letter restrictions all depend on who touched what, which means the access record matters as much as the answer.

If a research agent can open files outside its matter and then write an innocent summary, the firm has a confidentiality problem that no output review will catch. The same goes for conflicts. An agent that reads across an ethical wall to be helpful has still crossed the wall.

What I would ask the vendor

I would ask three things. Does the agent log every tool call and every document it retrieves, tied to the user and the matter? Can I put a gate in front of those calls that checks whether the task needs the data? And can I see the logs without filing a support ticket?

I would also test it myself. Plant a few canary documents in a sandbox matter, run the tasks your lawyers will really run, and read the access logs. This paper gives you the recipe.

A caution on the evidence

The records were synthetic and the setting was medical. The sample of models was small, and models change monthly. I read the specific results as a snapshot, and I expect the method to outlast them.

We have spent decades building controls around who may open which file. When an agent opens a hundred files on a lawyer's behalf, who at your firm is reading the access log?
