The Citation Was Real. The Support Wasn't.

A new benchmark built from New York Court of Appeals opinions shows where AI cite-checkers still slip

Every law librarian I know has a story about a brief that cited a real case for something the case never said. Long before anyone was worried about generative AI, the overstated parenthetical was a classic. The case exists, the quote is close enough, and the proposition is just a little bit bigger than what the court actually held. A tired cite-checker at 11 p.m. waves it through.

So when I saw a new paper on arXiv this week from M. Mikail Demir and M. Abdullah Canbaz called When Citations Mislead? A Claim-Level Benchmark for Legal Hallucination Detection, I went straight to the results section. They are asking the question I wish more legal AI vendors would answer out loud: when a model reads a legal claim and the authority cited for it, can it tell whether the authority actually supports the claim?

What they built

The benchmark is called PARCEL. The authors took recent decisions from the New York State Court of Appeals and built 3,396 claims written in the style of a brief parenthetical. Each claim is labeled Supported, Refuted, or Not Found, based on the opinion itself. Then they tested several current large language models, zero-shot, on sorting the claims into those three buckets.

I like this design a lot. It is close to the task a junior associate or a research attorney actually performs, and it uses real opinions from a real court. It also separates two different kinds of failure. A claim the opinion contradicts is one problem. A claim the opinion simply never addresses is a quieter, sneakier one.

The good news and the catch

The best models reached 0.97 accuracy. That is a strong number, and I suspect some vendors will put it on a slide.

The catch is in where the remaining errors land. The models still marked unsupported claims as supported, even when they had the full text of the opinion to work with. Missing support was harder for them to detect than a direct contradiction. And fabricated citations that looked plausible caused the largest drop in performance of anything they tested.

Think about what that means in practice. The errors that survive are the ones that look the most like good work. A model is pretty good at noticing when the court said the opposite of your claim, and it slips more often when the court never addressed your claim at all. That kind of error is exactly the one that ends up in a sanctions order.

What I would do with this

First, I would treat PARCEL as a template for an acceptance test. If you are evaluating a drafting tool or a cite-checking feature, build a small set of your own claims from opinions in your jurisdictions. Include some that are flatly wrong, some that are subtly overstated, and a few citations that look right and don't exist. Then see what the tool says. Twenty of those will tell you a lot.

Second, I would keep the human cite-check step in the firm's AI use policy for anything that gets filed. I know that sounds obvious. But a 97 percent score is exactly the kind of number that tempts people to skip the step, and this paper tells us the remaining 3 percent is concentrated in the errors that are hardest to spot by eye.

Third, I would ask vendors a very specific question. Ask how the product handles a real case cited for a proposition it does not quite support. If the answer is a general statement about retrieval and grounding, push for an example.

The authors say PARCEL is meant to test claim-level groundedness in legal RAG systems, and I hope vendors pick it up and publish their numbers against it. I would also love to see someone build the same thing from Texas or federal appellate opinions.

Which raises the question I keep coming back to: when your cite-checking tool says a claim is supported, what does your team actually do next?
