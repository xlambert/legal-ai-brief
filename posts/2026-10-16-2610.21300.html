Same File, Same Prompt, Different Answer

A medical device paper shows why temperature zero won't give your AI review an audit trail

Years ago, when I was running a law library, a partner asked me to pull a regulation exactly the way we had pulled it for a brief two years earlier. He wanted to show a court what we had looked at and when. We could do it, mostly because the books didn't change once they hit the shelf and the online services kept dated versions. I've been thinking about that request a lot lately, because I'm not sure we could answer it today for AI-assisted work.

Ryosuke Suzuki's new paper, Making every stage of medical device document assessment with large language models deterministic, puts some hard numbers on the problem. The setup will look familiar to anyone who has sat through a contract review demo. A regulatory expert defines an assessment item, and the LLM reads the documents and returns a label (Satisfied or Not satisfied), a reason, and quoted evidence.

Suzuki sent the same request, over and over, to fourteen commercial deployments. That's seven models from three providers, reached through six different routes, each set to the most deterministic sampling settings it would accept. Every one of them gave differing assessments for at least one request. In twelve of the fourteen, the label itself changed. Same document, same question, and the answer flipped between Satisfied and Not satisfied.

Temperature zero didn't save anyone

The trick most of us have been told is to turn the temperature down to zero. Suzuki did the equivalent, and the variation kept coming. Cutting back on reasoning didn't help either. One finding really jumped out at me: the same open-weight model was reproducible on one serving setup and not on another. The model was identical in both cases, and the serving setup alone decided whether the results repeated.

Then he went the other direction and built a pipeline that is locked down at every stage: text extraction and OCR, chunking, embedding, indexing, retrieval, and inference. That pipeline produced byte-identical output across reruns, interruptions, edits, different machines, and different days. The catch is that it only held when the GPU model and the inference engine's scheduling settings stayed the same, so those details belong in the record along with the prompt.

Why a law firm should care

Swap "infusion pump requirement" for "change of control clause" and you have half of what the legal AI market is selling right now. Diligence review, contract playbooks, compliance checklists. Each one produces a label, a reason, and a quote, and lawyers are going to rely on those labels.

So here's the uncomfortable question. If a client or a court asks us to show how we reached a conclusion in a diligence memo, and we rerun the review, what happens when the answer comes back different? Which version did the associate rely on? Can we even tell?

Firms will keep using these tools, and they should. What this paper changes for me is the list of questions I'd bring to procurement. Can you replay a specific past answer exactly? What do you log besides the prompt and the output? Do you record the model version, the serving route, and the hardware? If the vendor is calling a provider's API, do they even control those things?

What I'd do with this

For high-stakes work, I'd treat the AI's label as a draft until a person checks the quoted evidence, and I'd keep the output that was actually relied on in the file. That sounds obvious, but a lot of workflows I've seen just show the current answer on screen and move on.

I'd also add a reproducibility question to our AI governance checklist. Most firm AI policies I've read talk about confidentiality and hallucinations. Very few talk about whether the same question gets the same answer twice.

Suzuki's paper uses blood glucose meters and infusion pumps as its test case, and the lesson carries straight over to regulated legal work. If your firm had to recreate an AI-assisted review from six months ago, could you do it?
