Who Else Is Reading Your Prompts?

A new audit of 42 AI chat platforms found trackers collecting conversation titles, prompts, and email addresses

When a law firm vets an AI tool, most of the energy goes into the model. Does the vendor train on our data? How long do they keep it? Is there a zero data retention agreement? Those are the right questions, and we've gotten pretty good at asking them.

A paper posted this week made me realize we may be looking at only part of the plumbing.

In Penny for Your Prompts: A Measurement Study of Contextual Leakage in AI Chat Platforms, Paschalis Bekos, Billy Tsouvalas, Nick Nikiforakis, and Michalis Polychronakis audited the web interfaces of 42 AI chatbot platforms. Some were general assistants. Others were built for specific fields, including health, education, and legal. The researchers focused on everything loaded on the chat page around the model: the analytics scripts, the advertising pixels, and the session replay tools that record what users do on a site.

What they found

Twenty-one of the 42 platforms sent conversation titles to third-party trackers. Nine sent the user's prompts word for word. A substantial number also sent the user's email address to trackers, which means someone outside the AI company can connect what was asked with who asked it.

To get there, the team instrumented the browser down at the JavaScript engine level, so they could trace each outgoing piece of data back to the exact user action that triggered it. That level of detail is why I take the numbers seriously.

Think about what a conversation title looks like on most of these tools. The AI writes it for you, based on your first question. So a title might read "Non-compete enforceability for departing sales VP" or "Responding to a subpoena for customer records." That's a pretty good summary of a client matter, and it's the piece that leaked most often.

Why this matters for law firms

The model contract doesn't reach this. You could have airtight terms with the model provider and still have a marketing script on the vendor's web page shipping prompts off to an ad network. It's a bit like vetting the courier service carefully and never asking who else has a key to the mailroom.

It also matters for the tools we didn't approve. Lawyers use free and consumer chatbots on their phones and home laptops, especially when they're in a hurry. Those are exactly the kinds of platforms most likely to run ad and analytics scripts.

So what would I do with this on Monday? A few things.

First, I'd add questions about third-party scripts to our AI vendor security review. What analytics, advertising, or session replay tools load on the chat interface? Do any of them capture page content or form inputs? Can they be turned off for our tenant?

Second, I'd ask our security team to open the browser developer tools on the AI products we license and watch the network requests while someone types a test prompt. It takes about fifteen minutes, and it's the same basic idea these researchers used, minus the fancy instrumentation.

Third, I'd use this paper in our next training session on acceptable AI use. Telling lawyers "don't put client information in unapproved tools" sticks a lot better when you can show them that nine platforms passed prompts verbatim to trackers.

The bigger picture

Model Rule 1.6 asks us to make reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized disclosure of client information. For years, reasonable efforts for web tools meant encryption and access controls. With AI chat interfaces, the thing being typed is often the most sensitive part of the matter, and the page around it may be built with the same ad tech as a shopping site.

I don't think anyone needs to panic. Most enterprise legal AI vendors are probably more careful than the consumer platforms in this study. Still, I'd want to check, and the paper shows a fairly cheap way to do it.

When your firm last approved an AI tool, did anyone look at what the chat page itself was loading?
