Cited but Not Consulted: When the AI Names the Statute and Ignores It

A new counterfactual test shows that a legal citation in an AI explanation may have little to do with the answer

One of the first things lawyers tell me they like about the newer legal AI tools is that the tools show their work. Ask a question, get an answer, and the answer comes with the statute or the case that supports it. It feels like the machine is reasoning the way a lawyer does.

A paper posted to arXiv this week makes me want to slow down on that feeling. Saisab Sadhu, Shreeyans Arora, and Pratinav Seth wrote Cited but Not Consulted: A Counterfactual Audit of Legal Chain-of-Thought Faithfulness, and the test they ran is clever enough that I wish I had thought of it.

Swap the authority, watch the verdict

Here is the setup. Keep the facts of a case exactly the same. Ask the model for a verdict and require it to name the governing authority. Then swap that authority for an unrelated one and see whether the verdict changes. If the model's answer really follows from the law it cites, changing the law should change the answer at least some of the time.

They ran this on seven open-weight models, from 8 billion to 70 billion parameters, across four benchmarks: CaseHOLD, ECHR, SCOTUS, and ContractNLI. They also decoded how the model's verdict developed inside its hidden states, so they were looking past the text the model printed.

The models were good at naming the right authority, getting it right 66.7 percent to 100 percent of the time. But swapping the authority barely moved the verdict. On CaseHOLD the verdict changed somewhere between 0 and 21.7 percent of the time. On ECHR and SCOTUS it was 30 to 77 percent, and on ContractNLI about 43 to 50 percent. Bigger models did not fix it, and neither did a purpose-built legal reasoning model.

And then the part that worried me

The authors also hid adversarial instructions inside the case facts. Those instructions steered the verdict 73 to 96 percent of the time. So the model was more responsive to a sentence slipped into the facts than to the law it said it was applying.

If you work at a firm that runs AI over documents from opposing counsel, from third parties, or from a data room, sit with that for a minute. The documents you review can contain text, and that text can talk to the model.

Why this matters for firms

I think the biggest practical issue is what we do with AI explanations after the fact. The authors point this out themselves: their findings bear on using generated legal explanations as compliance or audit artifacts. If a client keeps an AI's reasoning as a record of why a decision was made, that record may describe a path the model never took.

There is also a testing lesson here that any KM or innovation team can use. When you evaluate a legal reasoning tool, change the governing authority and see whether the answer moves. Swap in a different statute, a different contract clause, or a case from another jurisdiction. If the answer stays put every time, you have learned something about what the tool is really doing.

To be fair, these are open-weight models in a research setting, and the commercial legal tools use retrieval and other machinery on top. I would still want to see the same test run against the products we buy. It would not take long, and the vendors should be the first ones to want the results.

So here is my question for the vendors and for the firms using them: when your tool cites the statute behind its answer, how do you know the answer came from the statute?
