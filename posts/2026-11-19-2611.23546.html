When the Model Knows Better and Says Otherwise

A new paper finds frontier models can tell a research question is still open, then describe it to you as partly answered

Paper: arXiv:2611.23546 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23546

Technological advances are commonplace and there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool for assistance.

Judge Castel, Mata v. Avianca, Inc., No. 1:22-cv-01461 (S.D.N.Y. June 22, 2023)

The word doing the work in that sentence is "reliable." The lawyers in front of Judge Castel had not checked whether their tool was.

New York, New York, 2023

The plaintiff's lawyers in Mata v. Avianca, Inc. filed papers citing decisions with names like Varghese and Miller. Nobody could find them. "The 'Miller' decision does not exist," Judge Castel wrote. The Clerk of the Eleventh Circuit confirmed that the Varghese decision "is not an authentic ruling of the Court."

The lawyers eventually acknowledged that six of the decisions "were generated by ChatGPT and do not exist." When one of them had gone back to ChatGPT about a citation, it "responded that it had supplied 'real' authorities." In June 2023 the court imposed a penalty of $5,000 on the respondents, jointly and severally.

Mata became the standard warning about fabricated cases, and citation checkers were built to catch exactly that. The new paper describes a quieter failure. I think it's the one more likely to get past a careful lawyer, because every source in the answer is real.

November 2026

In Language Models Make New Research Sound Old, Guy Zyskind asked frontier models whether research questions had already been studied. He drew 40 questions from newly accepted papers, so the right answer was usually that nobody had done the work yet. Asked directly, GPT-6-Astra judged 98% of them unstudied. In its ordinary answers, it said so plainly only 8% of the time.

The rest of the time the model leaned toward "partly studied" and overstated what related papers established. Zyskind calls the distance between what a model judges and what it tells you a reporting gap. All six frontier systems he tested showed gaps of 30 to 90 percentage points. Across 22 models given identical papers, higher capability came with more accurate judgments and with larger reporting gaps.

The failure only ran one way. When Zyskind supplied the papers that did answer a question, models said so in 88 to 100% of ordinary answers. His fix was mechanical. He built a fixed answer from each model's own judgment and checked the cited papers separately, which raised Astra's plain reporting of open status from 8% to 95%.

Back at the firm

Swap "research question" for "legal question" and this is a familiar memo. Picture an associate asking a research tool whether any court in the state has addressed a novel indemnity issue. The tool can tell the answer is probably no. It writes three paragraphs about cases that brush up against the issue anyway, and the associate reads them as partial authority.

A citation checker passes that memo, since every case in it exists. The damage sits in the characterization. I'd add a separate step to any AI research workflow: for each case the tool cites, ask in a fresh session what that case actually held, then compare the two descriptions. Zyskind's results suggest the model will often get it right the second time.

I'd also ask vendors for a number most of them don't publish, which is how often their tool answers "no authority found" when that is the true answer.

When the honest answer is that no court has decided your issue, how often does your research tool say so?
