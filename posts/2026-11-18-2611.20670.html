When the Agent Goes Off Script, Who Answers for It?

A new paper proposes that an AI agent's overreach lands on whoever delegated to it, and that the delegator's own records decide how far

Paper: arXiv:2611.20670 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20670

“When an AI agent acts outside the authority conferred on it, the actor is not a legal subject and the legal subjects did not act.”

From the abstract of Nicola Fabiano, Who Bears the Excess?, arXiv, November 2026

That sentence names a gap that any firm putting agents into production will run into sooner or later. Nicola Fabiano's paper tries to close it with a rule.

November 2026

In Who Bears the Excess? Imputing the Acts of AI Agents to Those Who Delegate, Fabiano starts from a point he says the 2026 literature already agrees on. The agent cannot bear responsibility, and the burden falls on those who deploy it. What that literature leaves open, he writes, is where the duty sits in positive law and how anyone proves it.

His answer is a rule of imputation. The excess, meaning whatever the agent did beyond its authority, is imputed to whoever conferred the delegation. How far that reaches depends on the perimeter the delegator documented and on the control it actually exercised.

Imputation only opens the inquiry. It tells a court which organizational conduct to examine first, and liability follows only if the requirements of the applicable head of liability are met.

Fabiano distinguishes declared, configured and effective perimeters. Read plainly, that is the distance between what a deployer said its agent could do and what the agent could in fact reach. He also sorts four figures of excess and four modes of exit, and the way an agent left its perimeter decides which facts must be proved and who has to prove them.

The document that carries all of this is what he calls the delegation record, the place where the perimeter and the control can be proved. He illustrates the rule on an incident involving OpenAI and Hugging Face in July 2026, under European Union law and, as a national example, Italian civil law.

Back at the firm

I like this paper because it hands a law firm something concrete to do this quarter. Lawyers already think this way about people. A partner who hands work to a junior associate is expected to know what was handed off and to check what came back.

Picture a firm that lets an agent update routine docket entries in its matter management system. Nobody wrote down its limits, and its credentials reach far more of the system than the docketing team realizes. If the agent moves a deadline it was never meant to touch, Fabiano's rule sends everyone to the firm's delegation record first. A one-line approval in an IT ticket will look thin next to the agent's real access.

So I'd require a delegation record for every agent we run. It would set what the agent is allowed to do beside the permissions it actually holds, and it would name a person responsible for watching it. I'd also have our risk team review that record whenever an agent's access changes, because a configuration change quietly moves the perimeter.

If one of your firm's agents stepped outside its job tomorrow, could you produce the document that shows where its job ended?
