Your Old Templates Just Got Interesting Again

Marc Lauritsen and Hannes Westermann on pairing classic legal document automation with generative AI

Every law firm I know has a closet full of document automation. Maybe it's a HotDocs library someone built in 2009. Maybe it's a set of intake questionnaires that feed engagement letters, or a wills and trusts system that a long-departed partner was very proud of. Some of it still runs every day. Some of it hasn't been touched since the person who built it retired.

With all the energy going into generative AI, I've heard more than one person ask whether that old template work is now just legacy junk we should quietly turn off. A new paper on arXiv makes a pretty good case for keeping it.

Who wrote it, and why that matters

In With the Old: Enhancing 'Classical' Document Automation with Generative AI is by Marc Lauritsen and Hannes Westermann. If you've been around legal document assembly for any length of time, you know Marc's name. He's been building and writing about legal knowledge systems since well before most of us had heard the phrase "legal tech." When someone with that background sits down to think about how the old tools and the new ones fit together, I pay attention.

The paper looks at document automation rooted in expert systems and other symbolic approaches. Those are the rule-driven tools that ask you a series of questions and assemble a document based on your answers. The authors explore how those systems can enhance generative AI, and how generative AI can enhance them. They also report early experiments using large language models to spot and fix problems in text written by laypeople.

The abstract is short and the experiments are preliminary, so I won't oversell the results. The framing is what got my attention.

Why this fits how firms actually work

Rule-based templates are good at structure. They know which clause goes with which jurisdiction, which questions to ask, and what the document is supposed to look like. They're also auditable. You can open the logic and see why a paragraph showed up.

Where they always struggled was the free text. The client's description of the problem. The facts section. The answer a layperson typed into a box that doesn't quite fit any of the options. That's exactly the stuff LLMs handle well.

So picture a self-help intake tool for a pro bono clinic. The template drives the structure and the legal logic. The LLM reads what the person typed, flags the parts that are confusing or contradictory, and suggests cleaner language before anything gets assembled. The lawyer still reviews the result, but they're reviewing a document built on rules they trust.

I like that story a lot. It keeps years of careful template work in play.

The KM angle

This also hands KM and practice support teams a stronger argument at budget time. The templates, clause banks, and question trees they built are the structure that keeps AI output consistent and reviewable. I'd be making that case to the executive committee right now.

I'd love to see Marc and Hannes push these experiments further, especially on law firm documents and not just layperson text.

Which raises the question for your own firm: who still knows how your old document automation actually works, and are they in the room when you plan your AI projects?
