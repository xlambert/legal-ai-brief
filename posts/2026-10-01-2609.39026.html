Every Citation Checks Out. So Why Is the Memo Wrong?

A new paper on deep research agents puts numbers behind a problem research librarians have known about for decades

When I was running a law library, one of the first things I looked for in a research memo was what was missing. The citations were usually fine. Associates weren't citing cases that didn't exist (at least not before 2023). The trouble was the memo that found three cases supporting the client and stopped looking.

That memory came right back when I read Search Shapes Conclusions: Auditing Evidence Selection Bias in Deep Research Agents, posted to arXiv this week by Shuyao Xiao and colleagues. It's about the deep research agents that are now built into nearly every legal AI platform, and it puts some math behind a problem librarians have complained about forever.

The agent reads a sample

Deep research agents search, read, and then write a report with citations. Most of the quality checking we do on them, and most of what vendors talk about, is citation accuracy. Does the cited source exist, and does it say what the report claims?

The authors point out that a report can pass every citation check and still reach a skewed conclusion. The agent's early findings steer its later searches. What it finds first shapes what it looks for next, which documents it opens, and when it decides it's done. So the set of documents it actually reads is a selective sample of everything that was available. If the first few results lean one way, the whole report can lean that way too.

Any lawyer who has run a Westlaw search, liked the first page of results, and stopped there knows exactly how this happens. The agent just does it faster and with nicer formatting.

Measuring the tilt

The authors propose a correction method they call CESS. It uses the agent's own logs, specifically the probability of picking each document and of continuing to search, to estimate what the full pool of available documents would have said. On a public Open Deep Research agent, it cut the error against the full-pool average by about 60% and cut the estimate's sensitivity to opposing document rankings by about 87%.

The test questions came from a medical systematic review benchmark, so I wouldn't carry those numbers straight into a legal setting. The underlying point carries over just fine. Where the agent starts reading has a lot to do with where it ends up.

What I'd ask vendors

I don't expect Harvey, Lexis, Westlaw, or anyone else to bolt this exact method onto their products next quarter. I do think it gives us better questions for vendor demos.

I'd want to see which documents the agent considered and which ones it actually opened. I'd want the report to say when it stopped searching and why. And I'd want to know how much the answer changes if I run the same question with a different first search.

For our own lawyers, this is a training point. Citation checking is necessary, and it's the floor. When a deep research report comes back with a confident conclusion, the next step is to go looking for the strongest authority on the other side. If the agent didn't surface any, that's worth a few minutes of your own searching before you rely on the memo.

This is also where research librarians earn their keep. Spotting a one-sided search is something a lot of them have been doing for decades.

So when your deep research tool hands you a memo, how do you know what it didn't read?
