Would Your AI Vendor Put Money Behind Its Promises?

A proposal for agent insurance gives firms a new way to think about liability in AI contracts.

Anyone who has negotiated an AI vendor agreement over the past couple of years knows where the conversation stalls. We get through security, data use, and pricing without much drama. Then we hit limitation of liability, and everyone settles in for a long afternoon.

That is why a paper from Cheng Hua, Taro Tsuchiya, Nicolas Christin, and Giulia Fanti caught my attention this week. Holding Agents Accountable with Verifiable Agent Insurance asks a simple question. Once an agent misbehaves, how does the user get made whole?

How it works

The authors call it verifiable agent insurance, or VAI. The agent provider writes down a list of promises in a form a computer can check, something like "do not delete files without user approval." Those promises go into a smart contract on a blockchain, along with money in escrow. If a user thinks the agent broke a promise, a verifier reviews the agent's logged execution traces. If the violation checks out, the user gets paid from the escrow.

Yes, there is a blockchain in here. I can already hear some of you groaning. Stick with me, because the blockchain is the least interesting part for law firms.

The finding that matters

The authors tested VAI on three well-known agent benchmarks. When agents tucked authorization details into ordinary conversation, the system caught violations in 42.2 percent of the runs that had them. When the agents were changed to emit structured records of who authorized what, detection jumped to 83.0 percent.

That gap is the real story for me. If an agent can't produce a clean record showing who approved an action, nobody can audit it later. That goes for an insurer, an escrow agent, a court, or our own general counsel.

What this means for firms

I like this paper because it makes the liability discussion specific. Vendors are understandably nervous about open-ended liability for whatever a probabilistic system might do. Firms are understandably nervous about agents that can file, send, or delete things. A short list of checkable promises backed by real money is something both sides could actually price.

Picture a contract where the vendor commits that its agent will never send an external email without a named user's approval, never touch a document outside the assigned matter workspace, and always log the approving user. Each promise has a dollar amount attached. That is a much easier conversation than arguing over a liability cap set at some multiple of annual fees.

A firm could set that up with ordinary contracts. It would take an agent that keeps good records, a neutral party who can review them, and clear terms on what happens next. Our procurement teams already know how to write escrow provisions.

Where I would start

If I were adding one item to our AI vendor questionnaire tomorrow, it would come straight from this paper. Does your agent emit a structured record of user authorization for every action that changes data or leaves the firm? If the answer is no, the vendor is asking us to trust their logs after the fact, and those logs may not tell us much.

I would also want our malpractice carrier in the room for this one. Insurers are going to start asking these questions of firms, so we may as well start asking them of vendors.

Which promise would you want a legal AI vendor to back with real money first?
