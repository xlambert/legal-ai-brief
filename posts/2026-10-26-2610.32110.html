Who Is Reading the Fine Print on the Search Results?

A small arXiv paper about tool output says a lot about how we should test legal AI research tools

Every law librarian I know has a story about the search that "found nothing." The associate swears the database had no cases on point. Then someone pulls up the search history and sees it was limited to one jurisdiction, or a five-year date range, or the first page of results. The search ran exactly as built. Nobody looked at the settings attached to it.

That old problem showed up this week in a paper on arXiv, and it's the most practical thing I've read about AI agents in a while. It's called Who Verifies the Tools? How Warped Formatters Mislead Agents and Their Verifiers, by William Jeffries, and it comes out of a production research system used by the Pilsudski Institute in New York.

What went wrong

Here's the setup. The system uses an AI agent that calls a set of tools (searches, lookups, record retrievals) and then writes an answer. A second model acts as a verifier and checks the answer before it goes to the user. Pretty standard design. If you've sat through a legal AI demo this year, you've seen some version of it.

Every tool in the system did its math correctly. The trouble was in the text each tool printed back to the agent. That output left out things like the search limit, the cap on results, or which set of identifiers a number came from. So the agent got a partial picture, wrote a confident answer from it, and the verifier signed off.

Jeffries tested this directly. He fed the same tool results to agents in two versions: one with the omissions, and one where the formatter spelled out the missing details. Only the wording changed. Unsupported claims from the agent dropped substantially for three of the four failure types. And in one test the verifier went from accepting the wrong answers 90 times out of 90 to rejecting them 90 times out of 90.

Read that last number again. The verifier was the same model both times, and the only change was a few lines of text saying what the search hadn't covered.

Why this hits close to home for law firms

Think about how this plays out in a legal research tool. The agent runs a case law search, the tool returns the top 25 results, and the model writes up "the relevant authority" without ever being told that there were 400 hits and it only saw 25. Or a docket tool that covers one federal district and says nothing about it. The answer looks complete. The verifier, reading the same output, has no reason to think otherwise.

I like the two-model design, and I think it's here to stay. But this paper is a good reminder that the checker can only check what it's shown. If the tool layer hides its own limits, both models end up working from the same incomplete output.

So what do we actually do with this? Start with the vendors selling us agentic research products. The first thing I'd ask is whether the model sees how many results exist versus how many it actually retrieved. I'd also want to know which sources and date ranges were in scope, and whether the verifier reads the raw tool output or the agent's summary of it.

For those of us building our own tools in-house, the fix is pretty boring. Make the tool output say what it didn't do. Result caps, filters, jurisdiction limits, all of it, in plain text the model will read.

The part I'd steal

The paper also includes the audit prompt the team used with Codex and Claude Code. When they pointed it at an older version of their own system, it rediscovered the defects that were still live and found twelve more they hadn't catalogued. They also ran it against OpenClaw and Hermes, two well-known open-source agent systems, found the same formatter problem in both, and opened fixes. The OpenClaw fix has already been merged.

That's the part I appreciate most. The paper comes with something a firm's development team could run against its own tools this week.

So when your research tool hands an answer to the checking model, who at your firm has looked at what the tool left out?
