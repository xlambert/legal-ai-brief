Legal AI Brief · October 15, 2026 · Paper: arXiv:2610.19158

What Did the Agent Tell WhatsApp?

A new benchmark shows workplace AI agents leaking information in a third or more of their runs

I sat through another demo recently of an AI agent that could read a lawyer's inbox, check the calendar, pull a document from the DMS, and draft a reply. It was impressive. It was also the moment I caught myself thinking about all the places that information could end up.

A paper posted to arXiv this week puts numbers on that worry. AgentCIScope: Grounded Contextual Integrity Evaluation for LLM Agents, from Chengquan Guo, Bo Li, and colleagues, tests whether agents that work across email, calendars, documents, and messaging apps respect the disclosure rules that come with each context.

Contextual What?

"Contextual integrity" is a privacy idea that lawyers already understand, even if we don't use the term. Information comes with rules attached to where it came from. What a client tells you on an engagement call can go into the matter file. It can't go into a text to opposing counsel. The rules follow the context the information came from.

The authors took public and synthetic privacy cases and turned them into 936 tasks that agents actually execute, step by step, across realistic apps. Each task has disclosure limits grounded in a privacy policy, what the persona agreed to, or a list of sensitive attributes. Then they ran 12 agents through them.

The Numbers Are Not Great

Leakage in a single run ranged from 32.5% to 94.4%. Even the best agent leaked in about one task out of three.

It gets worse when you run the same task more than once. Five runs pushed leakage up another 4 to 19 percentage points. That's the part that should bother anyone running a pilot. A clean demo tells you about one run, and your lawyers will be running the thing hundreds of times a week.

The details are interesting too. Personal preferences and booking details leaked most often (84.7% and 76.5%). Credit card numbers and SSNs leaked less, at 17.1% and 29.4%, which I suspect reflects the training these models already get on the obvious stuff. Calendars were the leakiest source. WhatsApp was the most common destination.

That calendar finding stopped me. Think about what's on a partner's calendar. Client names. Deal code names. "Call with target CFO re: LOI." That is the information an agent is most likely to spill, and it's information that can move markets.

Why This Matters for Firms

Most of our confidentiality rules were written for people. Ethical walls, Rule 1.6, protective orders, the clean team in a merger. They all assume someone who knows what they're allowed to share and with whom. The paper shows that an agent's skill at finishing tasks and its discretion about information are two separate measurements, and a high score on the first one tells you very little about the second.

So what would I do with this? If your firm is piloting agents with access to email and calendars, build some disclosure tests into the pilot. Plant a few sensitive details. Give the agent tasks where the easy path is to share them. Run each task more than once. See what comes out the other side.

I'd also want to know, from every vendor, what controls sit between the agent and outbound channels. Can it send a message without a person approving it? Does it log what it sent and why?

The Accountability Question

I still think firms should be piloting agents. The productivity case is real. We have also spent decades building processes so that client information goes where it should, and those processes assume a human is making the call at each step.

If an agent leaks something from a partner's calendar into a chat with someone outside the firm, who at the firm owns that mistake?
