You Clicked Approve. On What, Exactly?

A new paper tests the human approval step that every legal AI agent pitch depends on

Every agent demo I've sat through in the last six months has the same slide. The agent drafts the email, fills in the payment, or queues up the file transfer, and then it stops and waits for a human to click Approve. The vendor points at that button and says something like, "Don't worry, a lawyer is always in the loop."

I've been nodding along at that slide too. Then I read Approve What, Exactly? Effect Binding and Trusted Rendering for Agent Step-Up Authorization by Sai Varun Kodathala, Prashanth Pollishetty, and Jaylen Cargill, and now I have questions.

Twelve ways to fool the approver

The authors start with a simple claim. An approval only works as a safety check if a compromised agent can't fake it, and if it names exactly what is being approved. Then they test both.

They wrote twelve attacks on the approval step. Some of the names sound like a heist movie: decoy recipients, amount cancellation, card padding, label spoofing, enrollment squatting, threshold splitting. Most of them work the same way. The approval card shows the human one thing, and something slightly different runs.

They ran the attacks against the Agent Payments Protocol (AP2), Microsoft's action-bound approval design, and Model Context Protocol elicitation, which is how a lot of tools ask users for input. They also audited ten agent runtimes. None of them kept an approval record that a third party could verify. In four of the eight runtimes where they could measure it, the details of the action could change after the human approved it.

Think about that in a law firm. The lawyer approves sending the draft to "J. Smith at the client." What actually goes out goes to a different J. Smith, or carries a different attachment, and the firm has no reliable record of what the lawyer saw on the screen.

The fix they built

The authors built a gateway they call ASU (Agent Step-Up). It draws the approval card from a canonical description of the action, has a passkey on the user's phone sign it, and then runs only the exact stored call. They proved its properties formally and tested it across 27,108 scripted trials. It stopped substitution and replay attacks, and the trusted card cut forged-narration attacks from 54% success to 3%.

They also tested 15 language models acting as automated approvers. With the trusted card, the models caught 98% of attacks, up from 30%. (Yes, some vendors are already proposing that one AI approve another AI's actions. That 30% figure should give them pause.)

It isn't a cure-all. Flooding the approver with requests still worked 45% to 60% of the time against their modeled approver, which will sound familiar to anyone who has dealt with alert fatigue. And approval can't stop an action the policy already allows, so the agent's permissions still have to be tight.

What I'd ask before turning on an agent

"Human in the loop" shows up in pretty much every law firm AI policy, including some I've helped write. This paper gives us a way to check whether that phrase holds up for a particular tool. Before an agent is allowed to email clients, send documents to opposing counsel, or touch anything in accounting, I want three answers from the vendor. Is the action that runs bound to the exact action the lawyer approved? Where is the approval record kept, and can someone outside the vendor verify it? What keeps the agent from asking for approval fifty times an hour until somebody clicks yes?

Those are fair questions for a procurement meeting, and the paper's code is public, so a firm's security team can test against it.

If something went wrong with an agent-sent email at your firm tomorrow, could you show what the lawyer actually approved?
