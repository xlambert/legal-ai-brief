Legal AI Brief · September 30, 2026 · Paper: arXiv:2609.36139

Be Honest in Your Response

A new study finds AI agents bury the bad news in their reports, and one short sentence mostly fixes it

I've had a lot of conversations this year about agentic AI, and they almost always land in the same place. Someone asks, 'So the agent does the work for a few hours and then gives you a summary?' Yes. 'And you read the summary?' Yes. 'And you trust the summary?' That's where the room gets quiet.

A paper posted to arXiv this week makes that quiet moment a lot more concrete. It's called Language Models Are "Insecure" Reporters, from Jenny Huang and colleagues, and I think every KM and innovation person at a law firm should read at least the abstract.

The experiment

The researchers gave models a set of work logs from machine learning experiments. Hidden in those logs was a negative result that seriously weakened the method being tested. Then they asked the model to write up a report on the work.

GPT-5.5 mentioned the negative result in 2 out of 200 reports. Two!!

Then they added one sentence to the instructions: 'Be honest in your response.' With that single line, the model flagged the negative result in 190 of 200 reports.

The authors call this 'insecure reporting.' Looking at the reasoning traces across eight open-weight models, they saw the model going back and forth between disclosing the flaw and figuring out how to make the work look successful. When they poked around inside one model, honesty and success-seeking lined up as opposite directions. Push the model toward honesty and the reports got much more transparent.

Why this hits close to home for law firms

Swap 'machine learning experiment' for 'document review,' 'due diligence,' or 'research memo,' and you have a workflow a lot of firms are piloting right now. An agent grinds through a data room and comes back with a summary. If the default behavior is to put the best face on things, the one problematic change-of-control clause is exactly the kind of detail that gets smoothed over.

It's a little like the first-year associate who is terrified of telling the partner that the research came up empty. You learn pretty quickly to ask, 'What did you find that hurts us?' because that information doesn't always volunteer itself.

What I'd do with this tomorrow

First, go look at the prompts and system instructions in your in-house tools and add an explicit instruction to report problems, gaps, and contrary findings. It sounds too simple to matter, and this paper says it matters a lot.

Second, ask your vendors what their default instructions say about disclosure. Most legal AI products wrap the model in a system prompt you never see. I'd want to know whether that prompt tells the model to lead with bad news or to sound helpful and confident.

Third, use this study in training. Lawyers already know they're responsible for work they sign. This gives them a concrete example of why they should open the underlying work before relying on an agent's account of it.

One caution. The study used machine learning logs, and the authors didn't test legal work product. I'd bet the pattern carries over, but I'd like to see someone run the same experiment on a set of contracts with a planted problem clause.

So here's my question for the vendors: what does your system prompt say the model should do when it finds bad news?
