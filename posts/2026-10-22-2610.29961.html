What Does Your Legal AI Do When the Right Case Isn't There?

A new CaseHOLD experiment shows most models would rather guess than say "none of the above."

Back when I was running a law library, the best research attorneys I worked with had one habit in common. When they couldn't find the case, they said so. They'd walk into my office and tell me, "I've looked everywhere, and I don't think there's anything on point." That sentence saved a lot of partners from a lot of bad briefs.

So I was very interested this week when a paper by M. Mikail Demir showed up in my arXiv feed. It's called Commit or Abstain? System One Decision Models, LLMs, and Missing Legal Authority, and it tests exactly that habit in legal AI tools.

The experiment

If you haven't run into it, CaseHOLD is a well-known legal AI benchmark. You get a passage from a court opinion with a citation, and you pick the right holding out of five choices. Most current models do pretty well on it.

Demir did something sneaky. In 1,670 of the 5,314 questions, he pulled the correct holding out entirely. Every remaining answer is wrong. The right move is to abstain.

The six generative LLMs he tested still picked an answer between 38 and 86 percent of the time. They committed to a wrong holding instead of saying nothing fits. The most cautious of the "System One" decision models (these return a probability for each option rather than writing out a response) still committed 30 percent of the time. Better, but nobody gets a gold star.

On the regular benchmark, with the correct answer present, the best decision model tied the best LLM, 88.1 percent to 88.2 percent. It did that at about 1/55th of the cost.

The part I'd steal tomorrow

Here's the finding that I think matters most for those of us buying and building these tools. When Demir accepted the LLM's answer only if the decision model agreed with it, wrong citations dropped by half.

That's a second opinion. We do this with people all the time. An associate drafts, a senior associate checks, a partner signs. Nobody thinks the second reader is a waste of money when a bad cite in a brief can end up in a sanctions order and in the local paper.

We have been running something similar in our own shop, using a small decision model to check work alongside a bigger generative model, so I'll admit I read this paper with a bit of a confirmation bias. Still, the numbers are hard to ignore.

Accuracy is the wrong scoreboard

Demir closes with the argument that legal AI should be judged by its wrong citations, and I agree with him. Vendor demos love accuracy numbers. "Our tool gets 90 percent on the benchmark!" Great. What does it do on the other 10 percent? Does it say "I don't know," or does it hand your associate the closest-looking case and let them run with it?

Every lawyer I know who has been burned by an AI tool was burned in that 10 percent, usually by a tool that gave no sign it was out of its depth.

The other thing that jumped out at me is how much the generative models varied. A range of 38 to 86 percent is huge. That means the model your vendor picked, and the way they prompt it, makes a real difference in how often you'll get a confident wrong answer. That's information you should be asking for in procurement, and most of us aren't.

Questions for your next vendor meeting

If I were sitting across from a legal research vendor this week, I'd want to know three things. What happens when the controlling authority isn't in your retrieval results? Do you test for that case specifically? Is there a second model, or a second step, that checks the first one before the answer reaches my lawyer?

I'd also want to see the test. Demir built his by removing the right answer from an existing benchmark, which is cheap and clever. There is no reason a vendor couldn't do the same thing with their own question sets and show us the results.

Our lawyers already know how to say "I couldn't find anything." Shouldn't we expect our tools to learn it too?
