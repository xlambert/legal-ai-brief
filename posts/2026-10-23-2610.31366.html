What Happens When You Ask an AI Agent to Forget?

A new benchmark shows deleted memories can come back, and that matters for client confidentiality

Every legal AI platform I've looked at in the last year has added some kind of memory. Projects, workspaces, matter folders, an assistant that remembers your preferences. It's useful. I like not having to explain the same thing to a tool five times.

I've also sat in enough records management meetings to know what question comes next. What happens when we need it gone?

A matter closes and the retention schedule kicks in. A client asks for its data back. A lateral partner arrives and an ethical wall goes up around three matters. In each case, someone is going to ask the vendor to delete something, and the vendor is going to say it's done.

A paper on arXiv this week suggests we should ask a follow-up question.

The experiment

In AfterDeleteBench: Auditing Post-Deletion Information Persistence in LLM Agents, Ehsan Saleh, Roozbeh Nahavandi, Sama Salarian, Raef Bassily, and Andrew Perrault looked at agents that keep an external memory and build derived records from it, like summaries.

They planted a synthetic secret in an agent's memory, then deleted the original record. Then they compared that agent with a matched one that had never seen the secret at all. That comparison is the clever part, because it lets them pin any recovery on the original exposure.

In their main setup, a single question pulled the deleted secret back out of Qwen2.5-7B about two thirds of the time. The never-exposed agents recovered it zero times.

It gets worse. Even when no complete copy of the secret survived anywhere, the models could rebuild it from pieces stored separately, 62% to 99% of the time across Qwen, Mistral, and Phi-3. Think of shredding the original contract but leaving the deal summary, the closing checklist, and three emails that together tell you everything in it.

What worked

The authors tested two fixes. A filter that blocks the exact secret from being written back into memory stopped it from being re-saved, but it couldn't do anything about information already spread into other records. What they call provenance-aware closure, which tracks what was derived from what and deletes the descendants along with the source, eliminated recovery in the settings they tested.

For us, the takeaway is practical: deletion has to follow the information into everything built from it.

Questions for the vendor

I'll be fair here. These were small open models in a controlled test. Nobody tested Harvey, CoCounsel, Copilot, or anything else a firm is likely running, and the commercial platforms may handle this well. The trouble is that we don't know, and right now most of us aren't asking.

So here's what I'd add to the procurement checklist and the security questionnaire. When we delete a document or a conversation, what happens to the summaries, notes, embeddings, and memory entries that were created from it? Do you track that lineage? Can you show us a test where deleted content can't be recovered by asking the assistant about it?

This belongs in the AI policy conversation with records and risk, too. Our retention schedules and ethical screens were written with document management systems in mind, where deleting a file pretty much takes care of it. AI memory adds new places for information to hide, and Rule 1.6 applies to all of them.

When a client asks you to delete their data from your AI platform, how would you prove it actually forgot?
