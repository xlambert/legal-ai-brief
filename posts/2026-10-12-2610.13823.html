Run It Again... and Watch the Answer Change

A study of 1.2 million model calls shows temperature 0 won't promise you the same answer twice

Here's how most legal AI bake-offs go. We pick 20 or 30 questions, run them through two or three tools, have a couple of attorneys grade the answers, and pick a winner. I've run plenty of these myself. One pass per question, maybe two if something looks odd.

A new paper makes me think we've been kidding ourselves a bit.

Same Query, Different Answer: Run-to-Run Variance in Semantic Query Processing, by Oktie Hassanzadeh and Dharmashankar Subramanian, looks at semantic query engines. Those are systems that ask an LLM to judge or extract something from each row of a table or each document in a set. If you've used a tool that reviews 5,000 leases and fills in a column for the assignment clause, you've used something like this.

What they measured

The authors re-ran three published benchmarks (SemBench, LRO-Bench, and UDA-Bench) through their own harnesses and logged every one of 1.2 million model calls. Everything ran at temperature 0, the setting most of us were told makes the model deterministic.

It doesn't quite work that way. Depending on the model, between 0.07% and 10.2% of rows changed their output across five executions. On one task it hit 24%.

The bigger problem shows up when those rows roll up into an answer. On UDA-Bench, only a few percent of extracted values changed, yet about two thirds of the final answers changed. Small wobbles at the row level turn into big wobbles once you aggregate. In SemBench's own published results, 21% of text results changed quality between executions. And 16 of 28 tests of LRO-Bench's implementation recommendations couldn't be settled by the runs the benchmark actually did.

Voting across multiple runs helped, and the authors are clear that some variance remained even then.

Why I care

Law firms are about to lean hard on exactly this kind of tool. Contract abstraction. Due diligence review. Privilege screens. Deposition digests. Every one of them is a row-by-row judgment rolled up into something a partner signs off on.

Picture a diligence team that runs a change-of-control review across a target's contracts on Monday and gets 112 flagged agreements. Somebody re-runs it Thursday after a minor tweak and gets 104. Which number goes in the memo to the client? If a court or the other side ever asks us to reproduce the result, what exactly do we hand over?

The paper does offer something practical for people trying to manage this. The rows that flip tend to be the ones where different models disagree with each other, and a single run's decision margin or reasoning length predicts which rows are likely to change. That gives us a way to flag the shaky rows and send them to a human.

What I'm changing

For our own evaluations, one pass per question isn't going to cut it anymore. I want every test question run at least three to five times, with the spread reported alongside the average. If a tool gets a question right four times out of five, that's useful information. If it splits right and wrong down the middle, that's useful too.

I'd also ask vendors directly what they log, whether they can reproduce a prior run, and whether they surface confidence for each row. The ones who've thought about this will have answers ready.

I still like these tools. A first-pass review that's right 95% of the time and tells you which 5% to check is a great tool. We just need to stop assuming the second run will match the first.

How many of the AI evaluations your firm ran this year would come out the same if you ran them again tomorrow?
