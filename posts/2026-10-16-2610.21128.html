When the AI Agrees With Itself and Is Still Wrong

Why "run it three times" is a weak check for legal AI output

I've lost count of how many times I've heard this advice at an AI training session. If you're not sure about an answer, ask the tool again. Rephrase the question, run it three times, and if it keeps giving you the same answer, you can feel better about it. I'll admit I've said a version of it myself.

A new paper from Xiang Zhang, Lovedeep Gondara, and Jekaterina Novikova, When Judges Agree and Are Wrong: Consistency-Accuracy Dissociation as a Blind Spot for Label-Free Evaluation, put that advice to a real test, and it didn't hold up the way I'd hoped.

What they did

The authors looked at a whole family of "label-free" evaluation methods: self-consistency filtering, majority voting, and reliability scores built on agreement. These are the checks people use when there's no answer key, which describes most legal work. The quiet assumption behind all of them is that a consistent answer is probably a correct one.

They tested that assumption on four financial question-answering datasets, using two frontier model families and ten different ways of perturbing the question. Then they looked at the answers one at a time.

Sixty percent of the wrong answers were highly consistent, scoring 0.8 or above on their consistency measure. And 77.7% of those were what the authors call complete answer collapse. The model gave the identical wrong answer every single time, no matter how the question was reworded.

That pattern held at every threshold they tried, from 0.5 all the way to 1.0, and it showed up in both model families.

Why that's a problem for us

Think about how a careful associate uses an AI research or review tool today. They get an answer that looks off, so they ask again in a different way. Same answer. They try a third time. Same answer. At that point most people relax, and this paper says that's exactly the group of answers where the errors are hiding.

Consistency checks are good at catching the unstable answer that wobbles from run to run. A mistake the model makes the same way every time sails right through, and from the user's chair it looks like the most reliable answer on the screen.

This goes beyond individual users. Some vendors and some internal teams use agreement between models, or between runs, as their quality signal when they don't have labeled data. If your firm's validation of a tool leans on "the models agreed," you have a gap.

What I'd change

I'd still teach people to rerun a question when something looks wrong. It's a fine habit. What I'd drop from our training is the idea that a matching answer is a reason to trust it, and I'd say so plainly.

For validation, we need a set of questions where we already know the answer. Law firms have more of those than we think: past research memos, closed diligence projects, briefs where the court already ruled. Building a small answer key from our own work is tedious, and it's the only way I know to catch the confident, repeatable mistake.

The financial datasets in this paper are a close cousin of the work our tax and corporate lawyers do every day, so I don't think we can wave this away as someone else's problem.

How many of the AI answers your lawyers trusted this month passed nothing more than the "ask it again" test?
