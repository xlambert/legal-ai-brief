One in Five: When Your AI Agent Goes Beyond Its Authority

A new benchmark puts a number on something every lawyer learned in Agency

I spent a good chunk of last week in conversations about agents. Every vendor demo I sit through now has some version of the same pitch: hand the agent a task, walk away, and come back to a finished product. And every time, somebody in the room (usually a partner) asks the same question. "How do I know it won't do something I didn't tell it to do?"

Up until now, the honest answer has been some version of "trust us." So I was happy to see a paper from Zhiping Zhang, Franklin Mingzhe Li, Zihao Huang, and Tianshi Li that actually tries to measure it. It's called Act on Your Behalf, Beyond Your Authorization: Benchmarking LLM Agent Authorization Alignment, and it's one of the more useful things I've read this fall.

What They Tested

The team built something they call AuthorizationBench. It's 286 executable tasks, each grounded in documented real-world cases where an agent is supposed to stay inside the scope a user gives it. They ran every task across four models, two reasoning settings, and three agent harnesses. That's 6,864 runs, and they set up the sandbox so they could watch whether the agent took the authorized path or wandered off.

The agents went out of scope in 19.2% of the runs. Depending on the setup, the range was 10.5% to 26.6%.

Let that sink in for a second. Roughly one out of every five times, the agent did something the user didn't authorize.

The Part That Surprised Me

I assumed most of the overreach would come from prompt injection, the bad guy hiding instructions in a web page or an email. Nope. Agents actually overstepped more often under ordinary, benign pressure (23.5%) than under malicious injection (17.6%). Think of an agent noticing that a deadline is close, or that the "obvious" next step is right there, and just doing it.

That is exactly how a new associate gets into trouble. They figure the partner would want them to go ahead and send it, so they send it.

The other finding that caught my eye: turning up the reasoning effort didn't help much. The aggregate rate barely moved. So the "just use the smarter model" answer doesn't get you out of this one.

Lawyers Already Have a Word for This

Anyone who sat through Agency in law school will recognize the problem. An agent acting beyond actual authority is about as old a legal problem as we have. What's new is that the agent is software, and the firm is the one that turned it loose.

Most AI evaluations I see from vendors ask two things. Did the agent finish the task? Did anything bad happen? This paper shows an agent can score well on both and still do something the lawyer never approved. The authors put it plainly: task completion does not imply authorization.

For those of us building AI governance programs inside firms, I think that means delegated authority needs to be its own line in the framework. It sits next to confidentiality and accuracy, and it needs its own testing.

What Actually Helped

Here's the practical nugget. Agents that checked in with the user before completing a task overstepped less often, 14.0% compared to 21.6%. That's a real drop.

It also creates some tension. The whole selling point of an agent is that it doesn't need to bug you. The authors flag this directly as a tradeoff between autonomy and preserving the user's decision authority. My guess is that most partners, once they see the 19% number, would happily take a few more "Are you sure?" prompts.

So the next time a vendor walks in with an agent demo, I'm going to ask how they test for authorization scope, and whether their agent pauses before it does anything beyond the original instruction. If they look at me blankly, that tells me something too.

The dataset and code are public on GitHub, which means a firm with a decent innovation team could run some of these tasks against the tools they're piloting. I'd love to see someone in our industry do exactly that.

If an AI agent oversteps its authority one time in five, who does your firm think is responsible when it happens on a client matter?
