Capitalized Terms Mean Something... Unless You're a Language Model

A new paper shows models misread defined terms in real contracts, and handing them the definition fixes only about half the errors

Paper: arXiv:2611.15597 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15597

I spent a good chunk of my early career as a law librarian answering a question that sounds silly until you've been burned by it: "What does this word mean in this document?" Every lawyer learns early that a contract is its own little dictionary. "Affiliate," "Business Day," and "Material Adverse Effect" all mean exactly what Section 1.01 says they mean, and your everyday sense of the word is beside the point.

So when I saw a new paper from Zengqing Wu and Chuan Xiao titled When Local Definitions Are Missing: Stable Errors in Language Models, I went straight to the contract results.

What They Tested

The setup is simple. The researchers wrote multiple-choice questions where the right answer depends on a definition that lives somewhere in the source. Some questions came from a made-up vocabulary, some from Reddit community glossaries, some from the documentation of a crypto exchange, and some from real commercial contracts. They asked three models each question with and without the definition in front of them.

Here's what caught my eye. The words that caused the most trouble were familiar words that the source redefines. That describes every defined term in every agreement your firm has ever drafted. The models also tended to repeat the same wrong answer every time they were asked, and their stated confidence gave no warning. If you were hoping to catch these errors by running the question three times and watching for wobble, the paper says not to count on it.

The part that should bother anyone running contract review tools is the second finding. On the synthetic questions, giving the model the source definition fixed most of the errors. On real contracts, it fixed about half. Half!! Even with the definition sitting right there in the context window, the model's general sense of the word kept winning a good share of the time.

(I'll admit my first reaction was "well, that's what retrieval is for." It turns out retrieval helps a lot and still leaves plenty on the table.)

What It Looks Like at a Firm

Picture the associate who asks a contract AI tool, "Can the buyer terminate if the seller misses a Business Day deadline?" The tool produces a confident, clean answer built on what "business day" means to most people, and the agreement happens to define it to exclude a list of bank holidays in two countries. The answer reads fine and nobody flags it. That's the failure this paper describes, and it slips past a quick partner review because nothing about it looks wrong.

There is a hopeful note. The authors found that a small model, given the answer key, could rank which questions the larger models were likely to miss. That suggests vendors could build tests that target the risky definitions in a document and send human review time there. I'd love to see contract review vendors publish results like that.

What I'd Do Now

When we test a contract tool during procurement, I want test questions that turn on defined terms with a meaning different from plain English. When we train associates on these tools, I want them to know defined terms are a known weak spot, so they go back to the definitions section before relying on the answer. And when a vendor tells me their tool "understands" the contract, I'm going to ask to see their numbers on exactly this problem.

The researchers close by calling for tests that check answers against the full source, including its local definitions. Lawyers have been doing that by hand for as long as there have been defined terms, and it looks like our AI tools need the same habit.

So here's my question for vendors and for the firms buying from them: how many of your contract AI test questions turn on a defined term that means something other than what it means in plain English?
