Is a Burrito a Sandwich? Ask Your Contract AI

A new paper finds language models misread words a contract defines for itself, and handing them the definition fixes only about half the errors

Paper: arXiv:2611.15597 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15597

… restaurant reasonably expected to have annual sales of sandwiches greater than ten percent (10%) of its total sales.

From the exclusivity clause, Section 4.07, of the Panera Bread lease at the White City Shopping Center, Shrewsbury, Massachusetts, as quoted in White City Shopping Center, LP v. PR Restaurants, LLC

The lease never defined “sandwiches,” and that missing definition ended up in front of a judge.

Shrewsbury, Massachusetts, 2006

PR Restaurants operated 22 Panera Bread restaurants in New England, including this one on Route 9. When the landlord rented space in the same shopping center to a Qdoba franchisee, Panera's operator argued that Qdoba's Mexican-style food counted as sandwiches under the clause.

The judge, Jeffrey Locke, noted that the lease “contained no definition of ‘sandwiches.’” So he went to the dictionary, which described two thin slices of bread with a layer of filling between them, and held that the term “is not commonly understood to include burritos, tacos, and quesadillas.” Panera's side had drafted the clause. Because it “failed to use more specific language or definitions,” it was stuck with the ordinary meaning, and the court refused to block the Qdoba.

Fort Wayne, Indiana, 2024

A zoning commitment on a Fort Wayne property barred restaurants except a “sandwich bar-style restaurant” selling “made-to-order” or “subway-style” sandwiches. The developer, Martin Quintana, wanted to bring in Famous Taco.

Judge Craig Bobay applied the same ordinary-meaning rule the Massachusetts court used and came out the other way. “The Court agrees with Quintana that tacos and burritos are Mexican-style sandwiches,” he wrote. Ilya Somin, writing at the Volokh Conspiracy, pointed out that most Americans wouldn't call them that.

So two judges applied one rule to one word and reached opposite answers. That's the case for a definitions section in one story, and it's why the agreements your firm drafts open with pages of them.

November 2026

In When Local Definitions Are Missing: Stable Errors in Language Models, Zengqing Wu and Chuan Xiao tested whether language models actually use those definitions. They wrote multiple-choice questions whose right answer depends on a definition in the source, drawing on real commercial contracts plus some non-legal sources, such as Reddit community glossaries and a made-up vocabulary.

Familiar words that the source redefines caused the most errors, which describes most of a definitions section. The models also tended to repeat the same wrong answer, and their stated confidence gave no warning. Running a question several times and watching for wobble won't catch it.

Handing the models the source's own definition fixed most errors on the made-up vocabulary. In real contracts it fixed only about half.

There's one hopeful finding. A small model, given the answer key, could predict which questions the bigger models were likely to miss, which suggests vendors could point human review at the risky terms.

Back at the firm

Picture an associate asking a contract tool whether the buyer can terminate after a missed deadline, in an agreement that defines “Business Day” to exclude bank holidays in two countries. The tool answers from what “business day” means to most people, and the answer reads fine.

I'd build questions like that into every contract AI acceptance test and ask vendors to show their results on them. I'd also tell associates that when an answer turns on a defined term, they go back to the definitions section before relying on it.

If Panera's lawyers had defined “sandwiches,” would your contract AI have read the definition?
