What a Colombian Law Benchmark Tells U.S. Firms About AI Answers

Fifteen models, 1,042 expert-reviewed questions, and a lot of confident citations to rules that don't exist

Back in my law library days, foreign law questions were the ones that made the reference desk sweat. Somebody walks up needing the relevant provisions of another country's commercial code by Friday, and you start working out which treatise, which database, and which outside expert you can reach before then.

So I was curious when Do Large Language Models Know Colombian Law? A Reliability Benchmark for the Colombian Legal System showed up on arXiv this week. The authors, Rubén Manrique, Michelle Castellanos, and colleagues, built an expert-validated benchmark to test how well large language models handle Colombian law. The short answer is not very well, and the details matter for U.S. firms too.

The setup

The benchmark has 1,042 items across ten areas of law in three formats: multiple choice, semi-open questions, and open-ended IRAC answers. They tested 15 current proprietary and open-weight models, and the questions went through several rounds of expert review.

What they found

On multiple choice, the models looked respectable. Accuracy ranged from 0.905 for Gemini 3.1 Pro down to 0.577.

On free-text legal answers, factual correctness never got above 0.45 for any model. That's on a scale of zero to one.

It gets worse. Answer relevancy and correctness were negatively correlated (rho of -0.46). In other words, the answers that sounded most on point were often the ones that were wrong. The authors flag this as a particular concern for non-expert users, and I'd add junior lawyers to that list.

And then there's the citation problem. A rubric-based LLM judge found that only about half the legal norms the models cited were correct. The rest were wrong or didn't exist at all. Blind human experts reproduced the same ranking of models, so this isn't an artifact of the grading.

Why a U.S. firm should care

The obvious audience is anyone doing cross-border or Latin America work. If your team is using a general model to get oriented on Colombian law, this paper says you need a local expert checking the output.

The broader lesson is about how we read benchmark numbers. Multiple-choice rankings tracked free-text rankings closely (rho of 0.94). So the cheap test is a decent way to rank models. The absolute numbers are where it misleads: a model that scores 0.9 on multiple choice still topped out below 0.45 on open answers, and open answers are what a lawyer drops into a memo.

That's worth remembering the next time a vendor leads with a bar exam score.

There's also a cite-checking lesson. After the fake case citations in Mata v. Avianca, most firms built habits around verifying case law. This study found the models making up statutory and regulatory authority. Our verification policies should cover codes, statutes, and regulations with the same care.

What I'd do with it

When we pilot a tool for a practice area, include open-ended questions that our own lawyers grade by hand. It's slower and more expensive than a multiple-choice quiz, and it's the version of the test that looks like real work. The authors released their benchmark construction pipeline, so a firm with a strong international practice could adapt it.

When was the last time your firm tested an AI tool with questions your own lawyers had to grade?
