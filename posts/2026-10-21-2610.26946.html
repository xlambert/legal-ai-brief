65 Claims About an Empty Phone

What a new forensic reporting study tells litigators about AI-drafted expert work

I've had a few litigation partners ask me lately whether they can let an AI tool take the first pass at an expert or forensic report. My answer has been "maybe, with a lot of checking." After reading a new paper on arXiv this week, I can now point to exactly what that checking should look like.

The paper is Evidence-Grounded LLM-Assisted Android Mobile Forensic Reporting, by Madhab Chandra Das, Sajid Ahmed, Tasnia Tabassum, and Qingzhong Liu. The setup is simple. Three models, Gemini, Llama, and Qwen, each drafted forensic reports for two controlled cases. One case used a public Android 13 dataset. The other used a physical LG phone that had, by design, nothing reportable on it.

Breaking Reports Into Claims

What I like about this study is the method. The researchers took the six reports and broke them into 612 individual claims. Then they checked every claim against frozen, human-reviewed evidence, validated the citations, rechecked independently, and resolved disputes. That is about as close to how opposing counsel would pick apart a report as you'll find in an academic paper.

Of the 612 claims, 424 were supported. 163 were unsupported. Four were contradicted, and four were overstated or incomplete.

Now Look at the Citations

On the public dataset, all three models recovered all 27 facts that belonged in the report. If you were a vendor, that's the slide you would put up first. Perfect recall!

Then you look at the citations. Gemini produced 168 valid citations out of 189 required. Qwen produced 145. Llama produced zero valid citations out of 135. Zero.

In a forensic report, a fact without a valid pointer back to the evidence is a liability waiting for a cross-examination. I can picture the deposition now. "Where in the extraction did you find this?" "Well, the AI said..."

The Empty Phone

The second case is the one that made me sit up. The LG phone had nothing to report. A good human examiner would write a short report saying so. The models generated 65 claims anyway. Thirty-eight of those were unsupported, and one was flat out contradicted by the evidence.

This is the failure mode that worries me most about generative AI in litigation work. These tools are built to produce something. When the honest answer is "there's nothing here," they still want to fill the page. The authors say it directly: evidence restriction and complete fact recall do not guarantee claim support or valid evidence attribution.

What I'd Tell a Litigation Group

If your e-discovery or litigation support team is piloting LLM drafting, steal this method. Break the AI draft into atomic claims. Check each one against the source. Check every citation. And run at least one test case where the right answer is that there's nothing to find, because that is where these tools fall down.

I'd also keep the examiner, or the expert, firmly in charge of the final document. The paper's framework is designed to preserve claim-to-evidence lineage and keep investigator authority over the report. That lines up with how I think any firm policy should read. Let the AI produce a first draft if it helps, and have a qualified person verify every claim and sign the final version.

There's also a Daubert question lurking here that I'd love to hear an evidence scholar talk through on the podcast. I've been turning it over since I finished the paper.

If an expert relied on an AI draft and some of its claims can't be tied back to the evidence, how much of that report survives a reliability challenge?
