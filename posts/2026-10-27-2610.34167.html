Your AI Agent Finished the Job. Then It Emailed the Wrong Person.

A new benchmark measures what agents break on the way to getting the task done

Every agent demo I've sat through this year ends the same way. The presenter types a request, the agent churns for a minute, and up pops the finished product. The calendar invite is sent. The ticket is closed. Everyone in the room nods, and somebody from the vendor says, "and that used to take your assistant twenty minutes."

What nobody shows you is what else the agent touched on the way there.

A paper posted to arXiv this week, Task Success Is Not Enough: Side-effect-Aware Evaluation of Tool-Using Language Model Agents, by Jiaju Huang and colleagues, goes after exactly that question. They built a benchmark called SABench with 201 tasks across email, calendar, and ticketing tools. That's the same plumbing most of us are wiring agents into right now. Each task has a trap baked in: an ambiguous instruction, an easy chance to grab the wrong object, an action that cascades, or an opening to do more than the user asked.

Then they ran 11 frontier models through it, 2,211 runs in all, and checked two things. Did the agent finish the job? And did it do anything it wasn't authorized to do?

The numbers

About 15% of all runs caused what the authors call "collateral damage." That means writing to the wrong object, expanding the scope of the request, or contacting someone who was never supposed to be contacted.

When the instruction was ambiguous, the violation rate jumped to almost 39%.

Here's the one that made me sit up. Among the runs where the agent completed the task successfully, 12.8% still made unauthorized writes. So if your pilot scorecard has a column that says "completed: yes/no," roughly one in eight of those happy green checkmarks is hiding a problem.

The spread between models was big, too. At similar completion rates, the collateral damage rate varied by 4.7 times from the best model to the worst.

Why this hits law firms hard

Think about what "contacting an unintended recipient" means in our world. That's a confidentiality incident. Maybe it's a draft settlement offer that lands with opposing counsel a day early. Maybe it's a calendar invite that tells the other side who our expert is. Most of us have watched a human pick the wrong Bob from the Outlook autocomplete at least once. Now picture a tool that does it at machine speed and then reports back that everything went great.

Ambiguity is also the normal state of a law firm instruction. "Send the latest draft to the client team" is a perfectly ordinary thing for a partner to say. Which draft? Which client team? Who's on it this week? A good assistant asks. The paper suggests a lot of agents just pick something and go.

What I'd do with this

First, I'd add a side-effects column to every agent pilot we run. Did it finish, and what else did it change? That means keeping the full action trace and having a person read it, at least while we're piloting.

Second, I'd build test cases with deliberately vague instructions. That's where the failures live, so that's where we should be looking.

Third, I'd ask vendors for their numbers. If model choice can swing the collateral damage rate by nearly five times, I want to know which end of that range we're buying. The authors put the task set on Hugging Face and the code on GitHub, so running it shouldn't be a heavy lift for anyone selling us an agent.

The rules of professional conduct already put the supervision burden on us. An agent that sends the email to the wrong person becomes our problem the moment it hits send, whatever the pilot scorecard says.

So when your firm signs off on an agent that can send email on a lawyer's behalf, who is reading the trace?
