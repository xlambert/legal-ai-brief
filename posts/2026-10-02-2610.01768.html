The Courier Doesn't Know What's in the Envelope

A new paper shows how an AI assistant's web-fetch tool can carry confidential data out of the building

Think about your firm's mailroom for a second. The people who run it are trustworthy, and they move hundreds of envelopes a day. They also have no idea what's inside any of them. If somebody slipped a client file into an outgoing envelope with a legitimate-looking address, it would go out the door with everything else.

I kept coming back to that picture while reading The Innocent Courier: Covert Exfiltration Through Legitimate LLM Web Fetching by Alessandro Pegoraro, Daryan Merx, Phillip Rieger, and Ahmad-Reza Sadeghi. Their paper shows that your AI assistant can play the part of that mailroom.

How the attack works

Picture malicious software that has made it onto a laptop but can't talk to the internet directly. Maybe the firewall blocks it, or network libraries are locked down. It can still talk to the AI assistant. So it builds a URL with a secret tucked inside, like a password or a snippet of a document, and presents that URL as a page the assistant needs to read to finish a normal task, such as migrating a software library.

The assistant does what it was built to do. It fetches the page. The attacker's web or DNS server sees the request, and the secret rides along in the address. Nobody asked the AI to send anything, and as far as it knew, it was just looking something up.

Across eleven open models, the attack worked 79.7% of the time. The authors also ran a case study against real-world chatbots and showed it working there too.

Why this one matters for law firms

Most of the AI security conversations I've been in focus on two things: prompt injection, and what the vendor does with our data. Those are the right questions, and this paper adds a third. What happens when the assistant itself becomes the way data leaves?

Firms are turning on browsing and agent features fast. Lawyers love being able to say, go read this site and summarize it for me. Every one of those fetches is an outbound request, and in this attack the request is built partly from content sitting on the lawyer's machine. If something on that machine is compromised, the assistant becomes a very polite accomplice.

Confidentiality is the whole ballgame for us. Model Rule 1.6 and the outside counsel guidelines our clients send us don't care which tool carried the data out. Whoever writes the incident report will have to explain it either way.

What I'd ask this week

If I were sitting down with our security team and our AI vendors, I'd start with some practical asks. I'd want a log of every URL the assistant fetched on a user's behalf. I'd want to know whether URLs built from local content get inspected before they go out. I'd want the option to turn browsing off for matters with heightened confidentiality requirements. And I'd ask each vendor whether its own red-team testing covers this kind of covert channel.

None of that requires panic. The attack needs malware already on the device, so our existing endpoint defenses still matter a great deal. It just means the AI assistant belongs on the list of things those defenses have to watch.

I'd also put this paper in front of the AI governance committee. Most firm AI policies I've seen spend their time on what lawyers can put into a tool. Very few say anything about what the tool is allowed to reach out and touch. That's a gap worth closing before a client asks about it.

So who at your firm is watching where the AI assistant goes when it browses on a lawyer's behalf?
