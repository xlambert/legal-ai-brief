Press the Button Twice

A new paper measures how much frontier AI models change their answers when you run the same job again, and why legal teams should care

Here's a test I wish every legal AI pilot included. Take the exact same set of documents, run the exact same task, and then run it again. Same prompt, same model, same everything. Then lay the two outputs side by side and see what changed.

Most of us skip that step. We run the task once, the output looks reasonable, a lawyer spot-checks a few items, and we call it a good result. A new paper on arXiv suggests that habit may be hiding a real problem.

What the researchers did

In Same Feedback, Different Answer: Measuring Run-to-Run Instability in Frontier-Model Customer Feedback Analysis, Viraj Bagal, Raviraja Ganta, and Prabhath Chellingi ran recurring customer-feedback analysis tasks over and over across eight frontier models, with corpora ranging from 100 to 5,000 records. They tracked two things: theme churn (do the categories the model returns change between runs?) and volume disagreement (do the counts change for categories that stick around?).

The answer was yes on both counts. Each individual run looked plausible. Put the runs next to each other, though, and themes came and went, and the numbers moved.

The fix they tested was a taxonomy-grounded agent. It keeps a persistent set of themes and subthemes and makes a prediction for each record against that fixed list. With Claude Opus 4.8 and a 1,000-record corpus, that approach cut theme churn by 86 to 88 percent compared with raw generation, and counts on matched themes stopped disagreeing entirely.

Why I think this matters for law firms

The paper is about customer feedback. The authors say plainly that the framework is meant for repeated synthesis of legal documents, financial reports, and incident records too. That's our world.

Think about the work we're handing to AI tools right now. Summarizing themes across 40 deposition transcripts. Sorting a data room into clause categories. A first pass at privilege calls. Pulling the recurring complaints out of a stack of employee hotline reports for an internal investigation.

If I run that deposition summary on Monday and get seven themes, and an associate runs it again on Tuesday and gets five different ones, which version goes to the partner? Which one goes to the client? And when opposing counsel asks how the categories were chosen, what's the answer?

Lawyers are used to two humans disagreeing, and we have processes for that. I don't think most firms have a process yet for one tool disagreeing with itself.

What I'd do with this

First, I'm adding a repeat-run test to how we evaluate tools. It costs almost nothing. Run the job twice (or three times if you're feeling thorough), compare the categories, and compare the counts. If a vendor's tool moves a lot between runs, that's a conversation worth having before anyone signs anything.

Second, ask vendors how their tool decides on categories. Does it work from a fixed taxonomy that you can see and edit, or does it come up with the buckets fresh each time? The paper's results suggest that one design choice does most of the work.

Third, this is a knowledge management opportunity hiding in plain sight. Firms have been building taxonomies for decades. Practice area codes, clause libraries, matter types, issue codes from old litigation support databases. Those things might finally have a second career as the fixed scaffolding that keeps AI output steady. The KM folks who maintained them all these years might want to remind everyone of that.

I'll admit the paper has limits for our purposes. It tested customer feedback, which is messier and lower stakes than most legal work. Legal documents may behave better or worse. Somebody should run the same experiment on a legal corpus, and I'd love to see the results.

So here's my question for the folks running AI pilots right now: has anyone on your team actually pressed the button twice and compared what came back?
