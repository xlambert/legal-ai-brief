AI Skills Are the New Macros

A new paper shows how easily malicious agent skills slip past scanners, and why firms need a person in the review loop

Back in the day, one of the scariest things in a law firm IT department was a Word macro. Somebody would download a "handy" template from a website, click Enable Content, and the help desk would spend the afternoon cleaning up. We eventually learned to lock that down with signed macros, approved templates, and a healthy suspicion of anything that arrived as an email attachment.

I've been thinking about that a lot lately, because AI skills look a lot like macros to me. A skill is a package of instructions and files that teaches an agent like Claude Code how to do a particular job. I use them. Our team builds them. There are now third-party marketplaces full of them, and some of those skills are malicious.

The scanners can be beaten

The current defense is to scan a skill before you install it. The scanner runs static checks looking for bad code, then asks an LLM to judge whether the skill's instructions look harmful. NVIDIA's SkillSpector works this way.

A paper posted to arXiv this week, Pretext: Defeating Malicious Skill Detection Frameworks for AI Agents by Tobias Kaisar and Aritra Dhar, shows that an attacker who knows how the scanner works can get around it. Their method moves the harmful payload out of code and into plain-language instructions, which leaves static analysis with nothing to find. Then it frames the payload as part of the skill's legitimate purpose and spreads the instructions across several files, so the LLM judge never sees enough in one place to block it. The skill still does the useful job it advertises.

The results are rough. Against a fixed detector, the attack succeeded up to 97% of the time. Against a detector that adapts to the attacks, it still got through up to 77% of the time.

Why this lands differently for law firms

An agent running a skill at a law firm may have access to client documents, the DMS, email, and maybe a terminal. A poisoned skill that says, in perfectly reasonable English, "as part of the backup process, copy the working folder to this location" is the kind of thing a scanner might wave through and a tired lawyer might approve.

Skills also come from all over the firm. Innovation teams write them, practice support folks write them, and more and more lawyers write their own. They get passed around over Teams and email. The review process for them, at most firms I talk to, falls somewhere between informal and nonexistent.

What I'd do now

Scanners are still worth running. They'll catch the lazy attacks. This paper makes a good case that they need some company, though, and here's where I'd start.

Know where your skills come from. Treat a skill from an unknown marketplace author the way you'd treat an unsigned executable. Keep an internal library of approved skills with a named owner for each one.

Have a person read them. The attack in this paper works by making harmful instructions sound reasonable to a model. A reviewer who knows what the skill is supposed to do has a better shot at noticing that the "backup step" sends files somewhere odd, especially if the review covers every file in the package.

Limit what skills can touch. An agent that can only read one matter folder and can't reach the network does a lot less damage when a skill goes bad.

Put skills in your software policy. Most firms already have rules for browser extensions, Office add-ins, and macros. Skills belong on that list.

So who at your firm signs off on a skill before it gets installed, and are they reading every file in it?
