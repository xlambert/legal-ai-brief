How Much of That AI Memo Did You Actually Check?

A new paper puts numbers on why spot-checking gets weaker as AI documents get longer

If I had a dollar for every law firm AI policy that includes the sentence "lawyers must verify all AI-generated output," I could pay for a lot of Westlaw searches. The trouble is that almost none of those policies say how much verifying counts as verifying.

A new paper by Feliks Bańka and Jarosław A. Chudziak, More Claims, Less Evidence: Bounded Verification of AI-Generated Digital Knowledge Artifacts, takes a run at that question with actual math. It's written for digital libraries and repositories, but the problem maps straight onto law firm work.

The spot-check problem

Here's the situation. AI tools can produce a 30-page research memo, a deposition summary, or a diligence report in minutes. The reviewer has the same hour they always had. So they check some of the claims, find them correct, and sign off.

The authors built a Bayesian model around what they call the Predictive Value of Pass. If a document passes a partial check, how confident should you be that the whole thing is reliable? Their model predicts three things. Confidence drops as the document gets longer and the checking budget stays fixed. It goes up when you check more. And it is especially fragile when errors are rare, which describes good AI tools that are right most of the time.

The padding result

The finding I keep coming back to came out of their experiments on the FEVER and FEVEROUS fact-checking datasets. Take a document with a fixed number of false claims and surround them with more true claims, and the document becomes more likely to pass a spot check. The pass also tells you less about whether the document is any good.

Picture a long AI-drafted memo with two bad case holdings buried among forty accurate statements of law. A reviewer who checks five things will probably pass it. The longer and more thorough-looking the memo gets, the better its odds. Nobody has to act in bad faith for that to happen. It falls out of the arithmetic.

What firms can do with it

The paper also shows how to calculate the minimum verification budget needed to keep confidence at a target level. I'm not expecting partners to run Bayesian calculations before reviewing a brief. The general shape of the answer is still useful for policy.

First, review effort should scale with length. A policy that treats a two-page email summary and a forty-page memo the same way is setting people up to miss things.

Second, it should scale with the cost of being wrong. A background summary for internal use can get a lighter check than anything headed to a client or a court.

Third, aim the checks where errors hurt. The holdings, the quotes, the numbers, and the citations deserve attention before the background narrative does.

This also gives innovation and KM teams a better way to talk with practice leaders about AI productivity claims. If a tool saves four hours of drafting and the right level of review takes three, that is still a win, though a smaller one than the vendor slide promised. Putting a realistic review budget into the numbers makes the ROI conversation more credible.

How many minutes of review does your firm's AI policy expect for a forty-page AI-drafted memo, and has anyone written that number down?
