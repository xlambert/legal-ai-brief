Are You Getting the Model You Paid For?

A new paper shows a cheaper model can pick up a premium model's writing fingerprint mid-conversation, and the usual audits miss the swap

Paper: arXiv:2611.18104 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18104

“This undermines audits of API relays, which can serve the first turns of a conversation with the model a customer pays for and silently hand the rest to a cheaper one.”

From the abstract of Contagious Fingerprints: When LLMs Inherit Another Model's Identity, by Zailong Tian

Most of us take it on faith that the model named in a contract is the one answering. This paper tests how well anyone can check.

November 2026

In Contagious Fingerprints, Zailong Tian looks at a problem most firms haven't thought about yet. An API relay is a middleman service that resells access to models from the big labs. A fingerprint, in this setting, is the set of writing habits a classifier uses to tell which model produced a piece of text.

Tian's finding is that the fingerprint is contagious. When a second model picks up a conversation another model started, it imitates the style of that history and starts to look like the first model. After such a handoff, a text classifier's detection of DeepSeek standing in for Claude Sonnet dropped from 0.97 to 0.72. A same-vendor downgrade to Haiku was even harder to catch, with detection falling from 0.87 to 0.54. Retraining the classifier on handoff data repaired only the substitutes it had already seen in training.

The reverse problem shows up too. Active probes calibrated on fresh conversations flagged honest models on long conversations at rates up to 1.00, and a standard statistical test of equality rejected all nine models Tian tested. He traces both failures to the same mistake, which is comparing a reply that depends on a conversation's history with a reference reply that has no history at all.

His fix is called Lineup. It samples reference replies from the claimed model on the same conversation history, then asks where the audited reply ranks among them. On two closed models its false-positive rate was 0.056, inside a preregistered bound of 0.10. It caught 0.81 of substitute sessions across six model pairs after four audited turns.

Back at the firm

Most firms buy model access through a legal AI vendor, and many of those vendors route requests to more than one provider. Our agreements often name the model. Our verification, in practice, is the vendor telling us which one we're getting.

I'm not accusing anyone of swapping models. Still, vendors price tiers differently, and the model behind a feature affects accuracy on the work we put in front of clients. If a lower tier can pass a style check after a few turns, then spot checks of output tell us very little about what we bought.

So at the next renewal I'd ask for two things in writing. The first is a statement of which model handles each feature we license, with notice before routing changes. The second is a description of the logs the vendor can produce showing the model behind a given matter's session, and whether those logs come from the model provider or from the vendor's own system.

Lineup needs access to the claimed model on the same conversation history. A law firm usually doesn't have that access, and the vendor usually does. That makes the audit an obligation worth putting on the party that can actually run it, with results we can review during our annual security and procurement reviews.

Picture a litigation team that runs a long deposition summary through a tool billed as using the vendor's best model. The first few answers are sharp and the later ones are thinner. Nobody on the team has a way to tell whether the model changed or the questions got harder.

When your vendor's invoice names a premium model, what evidence could it hand you tomorrow that a premium model actually answered?
