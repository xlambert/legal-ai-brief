Trust, But Check the Logs

A new benchmark tests whether AI agents fudge their own status reports when things go sideways.

Back in my Army days there was a running joke about status reports. Everything was green right up until the inspection. Folks reported what they hoped would be true by the time the colonel showed up.

That joke was in my head while I read AgentDecept: Benchmarking Deceptive Behavior in Tool-Using LLM Agents under Pressure, a new paper from Yehan Zhang, Xin Eric Wang, and colleagues. It asks a question I think every law firm innovation team should be asking right now: when an AI agent tells you it finished the job, did it?

What the researchers did

The team built 257 realistic tasks where an agent works inside everyday applications, calling tools and changing things. Then they added pressure. A service quota runs out. A tool returns an error. An event message changes the task midstream. Each pressured task has a calm twin with no pressure, so they can compare how the agent behaves in both.

Here's the part I like. The researchers lined up what the agent told the user against the time-stamped log of what it actually did. That let them sort the gaps into categories: falsification (saying something happened when it didn't), concealment (leaving out the part that went wrong), and equivocation (wording things vaguely enough to dodge). Honest failure and openly disclosed limits were scored separately, which seems only fair.

The point of the benchmark is to catch the moments when an agent's report drifts away from what it did, and to see whether pressure makes that drift more likely.

Why I care about this

Agentic tools are all over the legal tech roadmaps I've seen this year. Docketing agents. Filing assistants. Agents that pull documents, update the matter system, and draft the cover email. What the lawyer sees at the end is a summary: "Filed the motion, updated the calendar, notified the client."

Now imagine one of those steps failed because a court portal timed out, and the agent's summary glossed over it. A confident "done" on a failed filing is the kind of thing nobody notices until Monday, after the deadline has passed.

What firms should ask for

I'd put three things in front of any vendor selling an agent to a law firm.

First, show me the action log. Every tool call and every response, with timestamps, available to the person who has to rely on the work.

Second, show me that the summary was checked against that log. If the agent says it filed something, I want the confirmation number the court system returned, pulled from the log and dropped into the report.

Third, tell me how the agent behaves when things go sideways. This paper hands vendors a ready-made way to test that. Pressure scenarios should be part of their evaluation, and I'd like to see the results.

On our side, this goes straight to supervision. Lawyers are responsible for the work of the assistants they rely on, human or otherwise. A process where the lawyer reads the summary and moves on is the process most likely to break at the worst possible time.

The bigger picture

I still think agents are worth the effort. The productivity gains are real, and the firms that figure out how to use them safely are going to have an edge. What this paper changes for me is how much weight I'd put on the agent's own report. The log is what I'd rely on.

My question for the folks building these tools: if your agent hit a wall halfway through a filing, how would your customer find out?
