Who's Really Being Sycophantic Here?

A small study on confident AI and the people who believe it, and what it means for "human in the loop"

We spend a lot of time worrying that AI tools tell us what we want to hear. There's a whole research area on sycophancy, and I've sat through more than one vendor demo where somebody pushed back on the model and it folded on the spot.

A paper posted to arXiv this week flips that around. In Who Is Sycophantic to Whom? Confident LLMs, Human Deference, and the Collapse of Trust Calibration, Deepthika S, Pawni Yadav, and B. Sankar ask what happens when the AI is confident, consistent, and wrong. Do the people fold?

The short answer is yes. A lot.

The setup

They had 32 participants learn up to five science topics by chatting with five assistants. All five ran on the same model, GPT-5-mini. The trick was that each assistant had been briefed with a different share of planted falsehoods: 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, or 100% of its facts swapped for plausible wrong ones. Every assistant spoke with full confidence. After each topic came a surprise quiz.

Scores dropped from 7.4 out of 10 to 2.9 as the falsehoods went up. When the assistant stated a falsehood, people adopted it about 65% of the time, and that rate held at every level. They didn't hold those wrong answers loosely, either. People rated their confidence in absorbed falsehoods at 8.4 out of 10.

Here's the part that got me. Normally people are more confident in their right answers than their wrong ones. That gap disappeared. Their confidence stopped telling them anything about whether they were right. And their trust ratings for the assistants barely moved.

Checking with the same source

One more finding keeps nagging at me. About 30% of the chat turns involved people pasting quiz questions back into the same assistant to check their answers. That's like asking a witness to corroborate his own testimony. The people who went back to the chat more often absorbed more falsehoods.

The assistant, by the way, almost never caved when pushed, which is the reverse of what most sycophancy research worries about.

Why lawyers should care

Every AI policy I've read, including the ones I've helped write, leans on some version of "the lawyer must review and verify the output." ABA Formal Opinion 512 says it too. That's the right rule. But it quietly assumes the lawyer will notice when something is off.

This study suggests that when the tool sounds sure of itself, most people won't notice, and they'll feel just as good about the wrong answer as the right one. This is how people work, lawyers included.

I'll be the first to say this is a small study: 32 people, science topics, a controlled setting. Lawyers checking a memo in their own practice area may do better. I'd like to think so. I've also watched smart people take a confident answer from a tool and run with it, and I've done it myself.

What I'd change

A few practical things come out of this for me.

First, verification has to mean checking against something independent: the statute, the case, the contract, the docket. Re-asking the tool, or running another prompt in the same chat, shouldn't count, and our policies should say that out loud.

Second, the interface matters. The authors argue that tools should make reliability visible claim by claim. In legal tools that means a pinpoint link to the source for each statement, so the reviewer can check each claim against where it came from.

Third, training. I'd love to run a version of this experiment at a firm lunch-and-learn. Give a group of associates a confident assistant with some planted errors in their own practice area and see what happens. I suspect it would land harder than any slide about hallucinations.

The authors call trustworthiness a property of the human-AI pair. I think that's right, and it puts the question back on us. If our people trust a wrong answer as much as a right one, what does "human in the loop" actually buy us?
