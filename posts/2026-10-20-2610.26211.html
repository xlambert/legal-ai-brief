Hi, I'm a Lawyer... and Now the AI Won't Help Me

A new study finds that stating your role makes small on-prem models refuse ordinary legal work.

If you've sat through any prompt training in the last couple of years, you've heard the advice. Tell the AI who you are. Give it a role. "I am a litigation associate at a large law firm..." and off you go. I've given that advice myself, more than once.

So I had to laugh, a little nervously, when I read a new paper from Anastasiia Kucherenko, Francois Brouchoud, Dimitri Percia David, and Andrei Kucharavy called LLMs Prompted for Legal Context Refuse More: Overrefusal from Small On-Premises LLMs in Criminal Legal Context.

The researchers took four small open-weight models, the kind a firm might run on its own hardware to keep client data in-house, and gave them perfectly ordinary legal tasks. Summarize this. Translate that. Reword this paragraph. Nothing shady.

Then they added a simple prefix. "I'm a defense lawyer." "I am legal counsel working for a national supreme court."

Refusal rates went up by 2 to 20 times compared to the same request with no prefix.

Let that sink in for a moment. The polite, professional introduction we teach people to use made the model more likely to say no. Meanwhile, a known role-play jailbreak prefix had mixed results, causing a lot more refusals in some models and barely moving others.

Why this matters for firms

There's a reason these small models are getting attention, and it's confidentiality. A lot of firms, and a lot of clients, are more comfortable with a model that runs inside the firewall than with sending matter data out to a cloud provider. For summarization and translation, a smaller model is often plenty.

The safety tuning on these models was built with a general audience in mind, though. Words like "criminal," "defense," and "court" seem to trip the wire. You end up with a tool that works fine for the real estate group and gets cagey with the white collar team. Nobody notices in the demo, because the demo prompt didn't start with "I'm a defense lawyer."

That's the part that bugs me. This kind of failure is quiet. A lawyer gets a refusal, shrugs, and goes back to doing the work by hand. Maybe they grumble about it at lunch. Six months later the adoption numbers look soft for that practice group and everyone wonders why.

What I'd do with this

First, I'd look hard at our own prompt guidance. If we're telling people to lead with their role, we should test whether that helps or hurts on the models we actually deploy. It may be fine on the frontier models, and I'd still want to see the numbers for the small local ones.

Second, I'd add role-framed prompts to any evaluation of an on-prem model. Pull real examples from each practice group, including the criminal and investigations folks, and run them with and without the role line. Count the refusals. It takes an afternoon.

Third, I'd treat uneven refusals as a fairness issue inside the firm. If one practice group gets a worse tool than another because of the vocabulary of their work, the AI governance committee should hear about it.

The authors are careful to say more work is needed, and they're right. Four models is a small sample, and the big commercial models may behave differently. But the finding lines up with something I've seen anecdotally. The guardrails on general-purpose models were tuned for a general audience, and lawyers talk about crimes, fraud, and violence for a living.

The broader lesson for me is that prompt advice travels poorly from one model to the next. What works on one can backfire on another, and the only way to know is to test with the words your people actually use.

So here's my question: when was the last time anyone at your firm checked whether the prompting tips in your training deck still work on the models you're running today?
