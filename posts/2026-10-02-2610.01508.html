Building an Ethical Wall for an AI Agent That Keeps Reaching

Every model tested in a new benchmark pulled more private data than the request needed

Anyone who has helped manage an ethical wall at a law firm knows the call. Somebody on the screened team opened a document they shouldn't have. Usually it was an accident. A search returned too much, a folder was mislabeled, or they clicked the wrong matter. The intent didn't matter much. We still had to document it, and somebody still had to explain it to the general counsel.

I thought about those calls while reading OverAct: Measuring and Mitigating Proactive Over-Authorization in LLM Tool-Calling Agents by Taolin Zhang, Jiuheng Wan, Hanyu Wang, Tingyuan Hu, and Chengyu Wang. The authors studied what happens when AI agents with tool access decide for themselves how much data to pull.

Agents grab more than they need

The authors call the behavior proactive over-authorization. An agent asked to do a narrow task reaches for more private data than the request requires. Think of asking for one client's billing address and getting back the whole billing history.

They built a benchmark across eight privacy-sensitive domains, with scoring that doesn't depend on another LLM acting as judge. Every one of the seven models they tested, from four different model families, went beyond the authorized scope. The vaguer the request, the worse it got. Adding more tools to the agent's toolbox increased the overreach, though more slowly than you might expect, and turning down the temperature setting barely mattered.

Their explanation is a cost imbalance. The models act as if missing something is the expensive mistake and grabbing extra is cheap. That makes sense from the agent's side, since extra data protects it from coming up short. For a law firm, that habit is exactly the problem.

Why the vague request part worries me

Lawyers write vague requests. I say that with love. "Pull together what we have on the Henderson matter" is a perfectly normal thing to type, and according to this paper it's the kind of request most likely to send an agent wandering.

Now connect that agent to the document management system, email, and the time-entry system, which is where a lot of firms are headed. The ethical wall rules still apply when a piece of software does the reaching. If the agent touches a screened matter while helping a lawyer on the wrong side of the wall, we have the same problem we had when a person did it, with a much less obvious audit trail.

A fix that's simple enough to ask for

The good news is the authors tested a straightforward mitigation they call SelfAudit. Before the agent makes a data call, it writes a short justification tied to the user's actual request, and calls it can't justify get filtered out. No special training is needed. That cut the privacy-oriented excess by 43%.

A 43% cut is a meaningful improvement, and it still leaves plenty of overreach in place. I'd treat it as one layer. The access controls in the underlying systems still have to do the heavy lifting, and walls need to be enforced at the repository level so the agent can't see anything the lawyer can't see.

When I talk to vendors, I'd ask whether their agents run any check like this before each tool call, and whether those justifications get logged. That log would be worth a lot the next time someone asks how a screened document ended up in an agent's context.

I'd also bring this into lawyer training. Specific requests produce tighter behavior. Telling lawyers to name the matter, the document type, and the date range is good prompting, and according to this paper it's good data hygiene too.

So when your firm connects an agent to the DMS, who decides how far it's allowed to reach?
