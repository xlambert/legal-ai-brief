Is That Video Real? Courts Are Still Deciding Who Has to Ask

A new paper offers the International Criminal Court a test for objections that evidence is AI-made, while the federal rules committee keeps its own deepfake rule on hold

Paper: arXiv:2611.21455 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21455

“Of the 931 judges who responded, only 15 reported having encountered a deepfake challenge.”

From the minutes of the May 7, 2026 meeting of the Advisory Committee on Evidence Rules, reprinted in its October 2026 agenda book

The federal judiciary has spent more than a year drafting a deepfake rule that few of its judges have needed yet. A new paper argues the International Criminal Court needs a clearer test of its own.

Washington, D.C., May 2026

The Advisory Committee on Evidence Rules met on May 7, 2026, at the Thurgood Marshall Federal Judiciary Building. On its agenda was a draft Rule 901(c), titled “Potentially Fabricated Evidence Created By Artificial Intelligence.”

The draft worked in two steps. A party claiming fakery “must present evidence sufficient to support a finding of such fabrication to warrant an inquiry by the court.” If it cleared that bar, the item would come in only if “the proponent demonstrates to the court that it is more likely than not authentic.”

The committee had already paused once. In its December 2025 report, the committee, chaired by Judge Jesse M. Furman, said the proposal was “being held in abeyance because the Committee is not yet convinced” that deepfakes were showing up often in federal court. It asked the Federal Judicial Center to survey judges.

The survey came back with 15 deepfake challenges among 931 responding judges. According to the minutes, 82 percent of the judges without that experience said they would want some initial showing before entertaining a fabrication claim, the same gate the draft would build. The committee held the rule again and planned a mini-conference for its fall meeting.

November 2026

In Synthetic Doubt and the Integrity of Digital Proof before the International Criminal Court, Seyed Mohammad Sadegh Dashti takes up the same problem in an international criminal setting. Generative AI makes it easier to fabricate audiovisual material, and it makes authentic digital evidence easier to dismiss as fake. He calls the second problem synthetic doubt: an objection alleging that a digital item may be AI-generated or AI-manipulated.

His question is when that objection should trigger closer reliability review under the Rome Statute without displacing the prosecution's burden under article 66 or compromising fair-trial rights under article 67. Provenance here means the record of where an item came from and how it was handled.

Dashti proposes a staged test. The party offering the item makes a baseline showing of authenticity and provenance, and the objector then has to meet a case-specific articulation threshold before the Chamber conducts proportionate reliability review under articles 64 and 69(4). He treats provenance data and the output of deepfake detectors as context, and neither one settles the question by itself.

Back at the firm

Both bodies landed near the same place. The person raising a fakery claim has to point at something specific before the court spends time on it. I think that's right, and litigation teams should get ready for both sides of it.

Picture a commercial case where our key exhibit is a phone video of a warehouse fire. Opposing counsel says it might be AI-generated and offers nothing more. Under either approach that bare objection shouldn't get far, but I'd still want a provenance file ready on day one showing who shot the video and how it reached us.

When we're the ones challenging an exhibit, a detector score alone won't carry the objection. Our litigation support team needs a routine for building a specific, articulable reason to doubt an item.

If opposing counsel called your best video exhibit a deepfake tomorrow, how long would it take your team to prove where it came from?
