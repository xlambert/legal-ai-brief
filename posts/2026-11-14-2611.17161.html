Cited but Unused: The Footnote Problem, Round Three

A new paper finds AI citations that pass every check lawyers have learned since Mata v. Avianca and still weren't where the answer came from

Paper: arXiv:2611.17161 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17161

Retrieval-augmented generation systems attach citations to attribute claims to their sources. These citations frequently misstate provenance, and standard verification cannot detect this.

From the abstract of Tanzeel Abbas, Tehseen Zia and David Windridge, “Cited but Unused: Parametric Provenance in Retrieval-Augmented Generation”, arXiv, November 12, 2026

Legal has been arguing about AI footnotes since 2023, and each round has left us with a longer checklist. This paper opens the next one.

Manhattan, spring 2023

Roberto Mata had sued the airline Avianca, and on March 1, 2023 his lawyers filed a brief citing cases that didn't exist. Steven Schwartz had found them with ChatGPT. As Bob Ambrogi reported at LawSites, Schwartz had even asked the chatbot, “Is varghese a real case,” and it told him the case “does indeed exist and can be found on legal research databases such as Westlaw and LexisNexis.”

On June 22, Judge P. Kevin Castel fined Schwartz, his colleague Peter LoDuca and their firm $5,000.

The lesson everyone took from it was easy to teach: check that the case is real.

Stanford, May 2024

The research vendors had an answer to Mata. Their tools pulled real cases from their own databases before the model wrote anything. LexisNexis advertised “100% hallucination-free linked legal citations.”

A Stanford team, with Varun Magesh and Faiz Surani as lead authors, tested those tools anyway. Along with plain wrong answers, they counted a real citation attached to a claim it doesn't support, which they called misgrounded. Lexis+ AI gave misleading or false answers on more than one query in six, and Westlaw's AI-Assisted Research did it on about a third. The checklist got a second line: read the case and make sure it says what the tool says it says.

November 2026

Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, is the design behind those tools. The software pulls the documents first, then has the model write an answer that cites them. In this new paper, the authors looked at RAG claims whose citations do support them, the ones that pass both lines of our checklist, and asked where those claims actually came from.

Their test is clever. They swapped a fact in the retrieved passage for a false one and asked again. If the model stuck with the original fact, it had been answering from what it learned in training, and the passage hadn't mattered. Depending on the model, that was true of between 1.2% and 15.7% of citable claims. Bigger models did it more.

Natural language inference, the standard automated way to check whether a passage supports a sentence, did no better than chance at catching these. The reason is plain once you see it: the model writes the answer it already knows and then picks a citation that agrees with it, so the citation checks out. The authors tried ten ways of detecting this, and even the best one isn't accurate enough to act on. They call separating what a model knew from what it used “the open problem.”

Back at the firm

A model's memory is most likely to be wrong on unusual points of law, and those are often the questions a client is paying us to get right, like a Texas rule that breaks from the majority or a client agreement that departs from market terms. On a question like that, the tool can write the textbook answer and attach a passage that sounds close enough, and an associate working the checklist sees support and moves on.

So I'd add a question to every research tool evaluation: how do you test whether your answers come from the documents you retrieved? If a vendor has run a substitution test like this one, I want to see the numbers. I'd also tell associates that when an answer on an unusual point sounds exactly like the general rule, it deserves a second look.

When your associates cite-check an AI answer, does anyone ask whether the answer came from the case at all?
