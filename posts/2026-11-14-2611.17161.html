Cited but Unused: When the Footnote Isn't Where the Answer Came From

New research finds some cited claims in RAG systems come from the model's memory, and the standard citation check can't tell

Paper: arXiv:2611.17161 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17161

Every time I demo a legal AI research tool for a group of partners, there's a moment where someone points at the screen and says, "Okay, but it gave me the citation, so I can check it." That's the comfort blanket of retrieval-augmented generation. The tool pulls documents, writes an answer, and attaches a footnote to each claim. If the footnote is there, we feel like we can trust the sentence.

A new paper from Tanzeel Abbas, Tehseen Zia, and David Windridge, Cited but Unused: Parametric Provenance in Retrieval-Augmented Generation, pokes a hole in that blanket.

The Test

The researchers wanted to know whether a cited claim actually came from the cited passage, or whether the model already "knew" the answer from training and picked a passage that agreed with it. Their test is clever. They take a fact in the retrieved passage, swap it for a false version, and ask the model again. If the model sticks with the original fact, it was answering from memory.

Depending on the model and the setup, between 1.2% and 15.7% of citable claims came from the model's memory. The rate went up as models got bigger within the same family, which makes sense when you think about it, since bigger models simply know more.

Here's the part I think matters most for us. The standard automated way to check citations, natural language inference, which asks "does this passage support this sentence?", performed no better than chance at catching these. The cause explains why. The model answers from memory and then chooses a citation that agrees with its answer, so the citation does support the sentence even though the sentence didn't come from it.

Why a Supporting Citation Isn't Enough

For a lot of everyday questions, maybe that's harmless. But think about the cases where the model's memory is out of date or wrong for your situation. A superseded regulation. A rule that works differently in Texas. A client's own contract that departs from the market standard. In those cases the model may write the familiar answer, find a passage that sounds close enough, and footnote it. A busy associate doing a quick cite-check sees a supporting citation and moves on.

The authors compared ten attribution methods and found that the best signal for separating memory from reading was comparing how the model answers with and without the retrieved context. They're refreshingly honest that it still isn't accurate enough to act on, and they say plainly that telling what the model knew apart from what it used remains an open problem.

What I Take From It

First, I'd stop letting "it has citations" carry so much weight in our evaluations and our training. A citation tells you there's a source consistent with the answer, and this paper shows that's a weaker promise than most users think.

Second, I'd ask vendors a pointed question: how do you test whether your answers come from the documents you retrieved or from the model's general knowledge? If a vendor has run a counterfactual test like the one in this paper, I'd love to see the results.

Third, for the work where it matters most, like a firm's own documents, a client's specific agreement, or a jurisdiction that breaks from the majority rule, train lawyers to read the cited passage against the specific claim and to be extra suspicious when the answer sounds like the textbook version.

I've been saying for years that the footnote is where legal research earns its trust. I still believe that, and this paper is a good reminder to check what the footnote is actually doing.
