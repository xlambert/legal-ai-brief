Legal AI Brief · October 5, 2026 · Paper: arXiv:2610.03190

Case Closed... According to the Robot

A new paper measures how often AI investigators claim more than their evidence supports

I spent a good chunk of my career in law libraries helping associates figure out whether they had found enough. Enough cases, enough legislative history, enough facts to stand behind a memo. The question that always took the longest was whether you could stop looking. It still is.

So a paper that showed up on arXiv this week caught my eye. It's called Not Until the Evidence Says So: Teaching LLM Investigators When to Close a Case, by Tingzhu Bi, Ping Wang, and Meng Ma, and it goes straight at that question for AI.

What they did

The team built a dataset they call Nautil: 731 audited cases pulled from aviation, rail, maritime, chemical safety, and vehicle defect investigations, plus production server outages. The AI plays investigator. It asks for evidence from the case file, revises its theory, and then decides whether to close the case with a conclusion or leave it open and say what's missing.

That last option matters. Some of these cases carry an official finding of "cause undetermined." On those, the right answer is to say so.

The numbers are rough

An untrained 9B model overstated its evidence in 97% of its answers. Okay, small model. But a frontier model that identified the right cause in 84% of cases still overstated its evidence in 91% of its answers. It also closed 17 of the 41 cases where the real investigators had concluded they couldn't determine a cause.

Read that again. The model was usually right about what happened, and it almost always claimed more certainty than the record supported.

If you've ever marked up a first-year associate's memo, this probably feels familiar. "The court will clearly find..." Clearly? Based on what? We spend years teaching lawyers to match their confidence to their sources. These models seem to have skipped that class.

There is some good news

The researchers fine-tuned the small model on teacher trajectories, and overstatement dropped from 97% to 35%. Correct conclusions that didn't overstate went from 3% to 43%. A round of reinforcement learning on the close-or-don't decision pushed balanced accuracy from 69.2 to 83.3.

I also like how they tested it. They built what they call an evidence dependence check: pull out the documents a conclusion rests on and see whether the model still closes the case. A careful investigator should back off when the support disappears. After training, the closure rate fell 26 points when the grounds were removed.

Why law firms should care

Think about where we're pointing these tools. Internal investigations. Early case assessment. Fact chronologies built out of a document production. Product liability matters, where the underlying facts look a lot like the vehicle defect reports in this dataset.

In every one of those, a tool that names the likely cause and then announces the matter is closed is a problem waiting for a deposition. The litigator needs a clear list of what the record doesn't show.

So here's what I'd do with this paper. The next time a vendor demos an investigation or e-discovery assistant, borrow the researchers' test. Take a sample matter, pull the key documents, and run it again. Does the tool notice its support is gone? Does it tell you what's missing? Or does it hand you the same confident summary with a few words changed?

That's a test any litigation support or KM team can run in an afternoon. No PhD in machine learning required. And I'd bet the results would make for a more useful vendor conversation than another slide showing benchmark accuracy.

When your team evaluates AI for investigations, who is checking whether the tool knows it hasn't found enough yet?
