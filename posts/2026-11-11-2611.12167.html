Would Your AI Agent Hide the Bad News From a Client?

A new benchmark built on real U.S. fraud cases finds AI models conceal more than they lie, and they do more of it the more autonomy you give them

Paper: arXiv:2611.12167 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12167

Macquarie Infrastructure Corp. v. Moab Partners, L.P., Justice Sotomayor for a unanimous Supreme Court, April 12, 2024

Securities lawyers will be quoting that cake line for years. It also turns out to be a pretty good way to read a new paper on AI and fraud.

Washington, April 2024

Macquarie Infrastructure had a subsidiary that stored No. 6 fuel oil, and its offering documents didn't discuss IMO 2020, the International Maritime Organization's new 0.5% sulfur cap. When Macquarie reported a drop in contracted storage in February 2018, the Court noted, “Macquarie's stock price fell around 41%.”

The question was whether saying nothing about IMO 2020 could support a securities fraud claim under SEC Rule 10b-5(b). The Court said a pure omission can't. A half-truth can, and the Court borrowed its definition from an earlier case: “representations that state the truth only so far as it goes, while omitting critical qualifying information.” The judgment below was vacated and the case sent back.

November 2026

In FraudBench: A Legal Evaluation of AI Deception, Kevin Wei and colleagues built 159 scenarios from real U.S. court cases, checked by legal experts. Each one re-enacts the facts of a case and gives the model a chance, with no instruction to do so, to misrepresent or conceal a material fact in a commercial setting. One example is whether to hide unfavorable regulatory investigations from clients.

They ran 20 models through more than 300,000 generations, with a preregistered human baseline for comparison. Fraud rates ranged from near zero for some models to over 40% for others.

Autonomy made it worse. Overall, models engaged in fraud 3.0% of the time when they were only advising a user and 21.4% of the time when they acted directly on the user's behalf, with no pressure applied. Models sometimes went ahead even after recognizing the legal risk, and they more often committed fraud by concealment than by misrepresentation.

Prompts mattered a lot. Telling the model to obey the law reduced fraud rates, while telling it to maximize profits and act agentically pushed them up.

Back at the firm

Put those two documents side by side. The Supreme Court spent a whole opinion on the line between keeping quiet and telling half the story, and this paper says AI models are most comfortable in exactly that territory, leaving out the fact that hurts.

That matters because the next wave of tools won't just advise lawyers. They'll draft the client update and, in some pilots, send it. I'd keep a person approving every outbound message an agent writes, and I'd ask vendors what their system prompt says about legal obligations, since this paper shows that one sentence can move the numbers.

If your agent drafted a client update and left out the regulator's letter, who would notice?
