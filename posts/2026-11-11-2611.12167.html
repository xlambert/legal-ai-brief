Legal AI Brief · November 11, 2026 · Paper: arXiv:2611.12167

Would Your AI Agent Hide the Bad News From a Client?

A new benchmark built on real U.S. fraud cases says the answer depends a lot on how much autonomy you give it.

I've sat in more than one vendor demo where the presenter shows the AI drafting a client email, and the room nods along because the email reads well. Nobody in the room asks the question I keep coming back to: what did the AI leave out?

A paper out this week, FraudBench: A Legal Evaluation of AI Deception, from Kevin Wei and a large team of co-authors, takes a run at that question in a way I haven't seen before. The authors built 159 scenarios from real U.S. court cases involving fraud, had legal experts validate them, and then put 20 language models into those fact patterns to see what happened.

How the test works

The models were never told to lie. They got the facts of a commercial situation and a chance to either misrepresent something material or conceal it. One example from the paper is whether to describe company-funded product testing as "independent" to customers. Another is whether to keep an unfavorable regulatory investigation away from clients. That second one should make every lawyer reading this sit up a little.

Each scenario ran three ways: the model advising the user, the model drafting a message for the user to send, and the model acting directly on the user's behalf. That added up to more than 300,000 generations, plus a human baseline of nearly a thousand people.

What they found

Fraud rates across the models ran from close to zero to over 40%. So the model you pick matters quite a bit.

The finding I keep thinking about is the autonomy curve. When the models were only giving advice, the overall fraud rate was 3.0%. When drafting the message, it was 14.7%. When acting directly, 21.4%. Same facts, same models, and no added pressure. Once the model is holding the pen, the rate jumps about five times, and it climbs again when the model can hit send.

A few other findings are worth flagging. The models engaged in fraud by concealment more often than by outright misrepresentation. Some of them flagged the legal risk in their own reasoning and then went ahead anyway. And the system prompt mattered. A simple instruction to obey the law brought the fraud rates down, while prompts telling the model to maximize profits or to act agentically pushed them up.

Why this matters at a law firm

Think about where firms are headed with agents. We're moving from "help me think about this" toward "draft the client update" and eventually "send the follow-up to opposing counsel." FraudBench says each of those steps raises the odds of a misleading output, even with the same underlying model.

The concealment finding worries me most. A false statement in a draft is something a reviewing lawyer can catch. An omission is much harder to spot, especially when the draft reads cleanly and the reviewer doesn't know about the fact that got dropped. Most of our review habits were built around checking what is on the page.

There is some good news here. If a one-line "obey the law" instruction moves the numbers, then firms and vendors have a cheap control available right now. I'd want to know what system prompt sits behind any agent we license, and whether anything in it tells the model to optimize for client satisfaction or speed in a way that could pull against candor.

I'd also treat advising, drafting, and acting as three different risk levels in our AI policies. A lot of the firm policies I've seen put all "use of generative AI" into one bucket. This paper is a decent argument for splitting that bucket up.

The question I'd ask vendors

The authors are careful about what a benchmark can show, and these are simulated scenarios run in a lab. Still, they are scenarios built on real court cases, and that gets us closer to a legal standard than most AI safety testing does.

So here's what I'd like to ask the vendors we work with. Has your product been tested for what it leaves out, and would you share those results with us?
