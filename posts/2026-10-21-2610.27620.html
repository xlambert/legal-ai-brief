Every Step Approved, and the Policy Still Got Broken

Why per-action guardrails on AI agents can't enforce an ethical wall by themselves

I was on a call recently with a vendor walking us through the guardrails on their agent platform. Every time the agent wanted to call a tool, a guard model looked at the request and decided whether to allow it. It was a nice demo. I remember thinking it felt a lot like a security guard checking badges at the front door.

A new paper this week explains, with actual math, why a badge check at the door isn't enough. It's called Local Allow, Global Deny: Why Per-Action Authorization Does Not Compose in LLM Agents, by Anmol Pandey, Aditya Jain, Liang Chen, and Carsten Maple. It's a formal paper, and I'll admit some of the logic went over my head on the first read. The core idea, though, is very relevant to law firms.

The Basic Problem

Most agent guardrails today look at one action at a time. Is this tool call allowed? Yes or no. The authors show that a sequence of actions can each pass that check and still add up to a policy violation. They call this a compositional authorization failure.

The authors prove that a per-action monitor that never raises a false alarm will catch none of these relational purpose violations, no matter which model or decision procedure the guard uses. They also surveyed six existing guardrail systems, and none of them do the cross-execution monitoring that would be needed.

The LLM Judges Saw It Once They Had the Full Picture

To test this, they built a solver (no language model involved) that generates the smallest possible examples of these failures. Then they showed those examples to nine different LLM judges.

When the judges looked at each action by itself, they approved the violating cases almost every time, at about the same rate as a harmless control. When the same models were shown both executions together, they flagged the problem.

So the models can recognize the violation. They just have to be given both halves of the story.

Why This Hits Home for Firms

Think about how much of law firm risk management is relational. Ethical walls are about information moving between matters. Privacy law has purpose limitation baked in. Client outside counsel guidelines increasingly say their data can't be used to train or improve anything for anyone else. Every one of those rules is about how actions relate to each other over time.

If we deploy agents across the DMS, email, and matter systems, and the only control is a per-call guard, we have no way to enforce those rules on the agent. Somebody has to log what the agent touched, by matter, and compare across sessions. The authors are blunt about it: a monitor has to compare executions or accept false alarms.

Firms should keep piloting agents, and we should change the questions we ask the vendors. I want to know whether the platform keeps a record of what data each agent session touched and why, and whether anything ever looks across sessions for misuse.

I'd also put this paper in front of our general counsel's office. The firm's AI policy probably says the right things about client data. The harder question is whether any tool we're licensing can actually check it.

When your agent platform approves every individual step, who at your firm is looking at the steps together?
