Your Client's AI Act Checker Says They're Fine. Now What?

The first empirical study of EU AI Act compliance checker tools finds they skip obligations and give guidance you can't act on

Every time a big new regulation comes along, a wave of tools shows up promising to tell you whether you're compliant. I remember the GDPR version of this pretty well. Lots of questionnaires, lots of dashboards with green checkmarks, and a lot of lawyers who ended up redoing the analysis anyway.

The EU AI Act has its own wave now, and this week a team of researchers took the first real look at it. Their paper, An Empirical Study and Assessment of EU AI Act Compliance Checkers, comes from Zhen Tao, Jens Grossklags, and colleagues. They picked 12 mainstream AI Act compliance checkers and put them through their paces.

What they looked at

The authors did three things. They characterized the tools themselves, including how you interact with them and how easy they are to use. They measured legal coverage and alignment, meaning whether the tools actually reflect what the Act requires. And they analyzed the compliance reports the tools spit out for structure, determinacy, and actionability. In plain terms, does the report tell you something definite that you can go do?

What they found

Quality varies a lot from tool to tool. The checkers tend to oversimplify key obligations or leave them out entirely. And the guidance at the end is often too vague to act on. The authors conclude that the current generation is only useful as an early orientation tool, and that leaning on these tools may create 'a false sense of compliance.'

That phrase is the one I'd underline. A tool that tells a client 'you're fine' when they aren't creates a problem that shows up later, usually with a regulator attached to it.

Why law firms should pay attention

The people these tools are aimed at are small and mid-sized businesses and individual developers who don't have in-house AI Act expertise. A lot of those companies are our clients. Some of them are going to show up with a checker report in hand and ask our privacy or regulatory lawyers to confirm it.

This paper gives those lawyers a solid, citable reason to treat that report as a first pass. It also gives us something useful internally. When we do diligence on our own AI vendors and they tell us they're AI Act ready, it's fair to ask how they got there. If the answer is a checker report, we now know where the gaps tend to be.

I also think there's an opportunity here for firms. The authors close with design principles for better compliance-support tools. Somebody is going to build a checker that actually maps obligations to a specific system and produces advice you can act on. Law firms with strong regulatory practices and a decent innovation team are well placed to be part of that, either as a partner to a vendor or by building something for their own clients.

A quick gut check

Using a tool to get organized is fine by me. A structured questionnaire is a decent way to start a conversation with a client about which of their systems might be high risk. The trouble starts when the output of that questionnaire gets treated as the answer.

If your firm is advising on the AI Act, which obligation would you check by hand first when a client says the tool already cleared them?
