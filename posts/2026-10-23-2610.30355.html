Finding the Fake Cite Is the Easy Part

A new arXiv paper on hallucinated citations asks the question our cite-check tools keep skipping

I've lost count of the sanctions orders. Every few weeks another one crosses my feed: a brief with a case that doesn't exist, a judge who is not amused, and a lawyer explaining that the AI tool sounded so sure. By now most firms have some kind of policy, and plenty of vendors are selling some kind of hallucinated-citation checker.

So I perked up when I saw a paper on arXiv this week called CiteAnchor: Source-Aware Grounding for Hallucinated Citation Detection and Resolution, by Chenyi Tong, Ping Nie, and Yu Zhang. The paper deals with scientific citations, so there's no case law in it. Stick with me anyway, because the way they framed the problem is the way we should be framing it too.

Two questions hiding in one

The authors split citation checking into two separate jobs. First, is this citation valid? Second, if it's broken, can you figure out which real source the model was reaching for?

That second question is the one I care about. When an associate runs a cite-check and the tool says a case doesn't exist, the work isn't done. Somebody still has to figure out whether the model mangled a real case name, merged two cases together, or invented the whole thing. If it was reaching for a real holding, you want that case. If it invented the proposition, you have a bigger problem with the brief than one bad cite.

To test both jobs, they built a benchmark called CiteResolve with 423 real hallucinated citations, each annotated for whether the true source can be recovered. These were collected from actual model output, which matters. Synthetic errors tend to be easier to catch than the weird ones models really produce.

The numbers

Their framework checks citations field by field (author, title, venue, year) and changes its retrieval strategy depending on the type of source. On an existing detection benchmark it hit 0.841 F1 using a frontier model and 0.784 with an open-weight model. That beat the same-model baselines by 7.9 and 36.8 points.

On the recovery task, it got the job done end to end 74.7% of the time with the frontier model and 70.7% with the open-weight one. The web search baselines trailed by 16.3 and 65.3 points.

Seventy-five percent is good. It also means one in four broken citations still lands on a person's desk with no answer attached.

What I'd do with this

If I'm sitting across the table from a vendor selling a citation checker for briefs, I now have a better question to ask. Detection rate is the first thing they'll show me. I want to know how often the tool can tell my associate what the right authority was. And I want to know how they tested it, because a benchmark built from real hallucinations is a much harder test than one built from typos.

I also like the field-level approach. Legal citations have structure too: reporter, volume, page, court, year, pin cite. A model can get the case name right and the reporter wrong, or nail the citation and attribute a holding the court never wrote. A checker that looks at each piece separately is going to tell you more than one that runs a search and calls it close enough.

I want to be fair about the limits. Scientific bibliographic data is messy but mostly open. Case law has its own quirks, like unpublished opinions, parallel cites, and the fact that the best databases sit behind Westlaw and Lexis subscriptions. Somebody needs to build the legal version of CiteResolve, and I'd love to see a law school clinic or a legal tech vendor publish one with real numbers.

The policy angle

Most firm AI policies I've seen say something like "verify all citations before filing." That's right, and this paper is a decent argument for treating an automated checker as a first pass, with a person finishing the job. Even a strong tool leaves a meaningful pile for a human to resolve.

That human time is where the real cost shows up. If a tool flags the fake but leaves the associate to hunt down the real case, how much time is it actually saving?
