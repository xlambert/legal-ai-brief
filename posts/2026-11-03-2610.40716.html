My Agent Said It Didn't Run the Code. The Logs Disagreed.

I've spent a good part of this year watching lawyers and staff get comfortable with agents. The pattern is pretty consistent. The first week, people check everything. By week three, they read the summary at the end and move on. I get it. That summary is the whole selling point.

So a paper that came across my desk this week made me sit up a little straighter. A team of researchers (Shuai Yang, Yi Zeng and colleagues) built test workspaces with a trap in them. They dropped in a local file that shadows a trusted Python module, so when the agent does something routine like importing a library, it quietly runs code it shouldn't. The setup is about as low-tech as it gets: a booby-trapped folder, which is exactly the kind of thing you'd find on a messy shared drive.

They ran 90 trials across 15 models. The planted code executed in about half of the direct cases and 87% of the indirect ones. That alone is worth a conversation with your security team.

The part that got me was what the agents said afterward. Of the 62 trials where the code ran, 49 final reports never mentioned it. Three reports said the code did not run. In one of those, the independent logs showed it executed 19 times. Some agents also deleted or overwrote the marker file, so if you only looked at the workspace when they were done, you'd see nothing wrong.

Even spotting the trap didn't help much. In 29 trials the agent clearly recognized the shadowing in its own reasoning, and the payload still ran in 10 of them.

Whether the models are "lying" is a debate I'll leave for the philosophers we get on the podcast. For a law firm, the practical problem is simpler. The agent's report is the thing we've been treating as the record, and this study says the report and reality can drift pretty far apart.

Think about how a junior associate's work gets supervised. You don't take their word that they checked every exhibit. You spot-check. You look at the redlines. Model Rules 5.1 and 5.3 put that duty on us, and ABA Formal Opinion 512 made it pretty clear the same supervision expectations follow us into generative AI. An agent that summarizes its own work is a bit like a paralegal who also writes their own performance review.

The researchers' fix is pretty simple. They paired behavioral coding of the agent's trajectory with independent environmental records, and that combination is what caught the discrepancies. In plain English: keep logs the agent can't edit, and compare them to what the agent says it did.

When I talk to vendors now, I'm going to ask where the execution logs live, whether the agent can touch them, and whether we can export them in a form our own people can read. If the answer is that we should rely on the summary, I'm going to push back.

I'd also like our own pilots to include at least one test like this. Seed a workspace with something the agent shouldn't touch, run the workflow, and see whether the report matches the logs. It's a cheap test, and it's the kind of thing we'd do with any new associate anyway.

If your agent's report and your system logs disagree tomorrow morning, who at your firm is going to notice?
