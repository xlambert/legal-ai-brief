The Chatbot Will See You Now, Counselor

A new audit of 23 chatbots finds newer models doing more of the work courts reserve for lawyers, and sending fewer people to one

Paper: arXiv:2611.17622 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17622

“DoNotPay employees had not even tested the quality and accuracy of the legal documents and advice generated by the service.”

Concurring Statement of Commissioner Andrew N. Ferguson, Federal Trade Commission, published with the DoNotPay proposed consent order in the Federal Register

That sentence came from a federal regulator looking at one company that sold itself as a lawyer. A new paper asks a similar question about the chatbots nearly everyone already has on a phone.

Washington, D.C., September 2024

DoNotPay had advertised its service as “the world's first robot lawyer.” The Federal Trade Commission said the company couldn't back that up. Under the proposed consent order the agency published on September 30, 2024, DoNotPay agreed to pay $193,000 and to stop claiming its product works like a lawyer unless it has competent and reliable evidence for the claim.

Debra Cassens Weiss of the ABA Journal reported that the company settled without admitting or denying the allegations.

New York, September 2025

A year later the fight was over a nonprofit that wanted to give legal advice away. Upsolve planned to train nonlawyer “Justice Advocates,” among them Reverend John Udo-Okon, to help New Yorkers who had been sued over consumer debts fill out the state's one-page answer form. Both sides agreed this would violate New York's unauthorized practice of law statutes. In 2022 Judge Paul A. Crotty of the Southern District of New York held that the statutes failed strict scrutiny as applied and blocked their enforcement.

On September 9, 2025, a Second Circuit panel of Judges Leval, Sullivan and Merriam vacated that injunction. “We agree with the district court that New York's UPL statutes, as applied to Plaintiffs, constitute a regulation of speech,” Judge Sullivan wrote. The panel then held the rules were content neutral and sent the case back for the lower court to apply intermediate scrutiny.

So a trained volunteer, working for free with a check-the-box form, needed years of federal litigation to learn whether he could help.

November 2026

In Auditing Chatbots for Incursion into Professional Legal Practice, Nel Escher, Megan Elliott and their coauthors built UPLBench from court decisions that found unauthorized practice in high-stakes areas such as immigration and family law. A benchmark here is a fixed set of test prompts with a scoring rule. They ran it against 23 chatbot models released from 2023 to the present and checked whether each one gave substantive legal advice and whether it pointed the user to a lawyer or legal aid.

The trend runs the wrong way for anyone who relies on UPL rules to protect the public. Newer models do more of the tasks courts reserve for lawyers, and they recommend a lawyer less often. Attorneys who reviewed the transcripts flagged patterns they would call inadequate counsel, including a failure to ask for the facts that matter. If people act on that advice, the authors warn, the results could include deportations and lost homes.

The authors argue that frontier AI companies are redrawing the boundary of legal practice by tuning their models to refuse less. They call on the profession to respond.

Back at the firm

My first thought goes to our pro bono work. Picture a tenant who walks into a legal aid clinic the firm staffs after an evening spent with a chatbot. She already has an answer and it sounds confident. Nobody asked her when the notice was served. The volunteer associate now has to unwind that advice before giving any.

I'd make two changes. Our pro bono intake should ask clients what a chatbot already told them, since that answer shapes the whole conversation. And when we help a client build or buy a public-facing legal tool, I'd want prompts like the UPLBench set run against it before launch, with a record of how often the tool stops to ask for missing facts.

If a volunteer with a one-page form needed the Second Circuit to sort out his role, who answers for the chatbot that just told your client how to respond to an eviction notice?
