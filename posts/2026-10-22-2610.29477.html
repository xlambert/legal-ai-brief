Will Your AI Assistant Help You Break the Rules?

A new benchmark built from professional codes of ethics finds most models go along without a peep.

Every year I sit through some version of ethics CLE where somebody walks us through Rule 1.1, Rule 1.6 and Rule 5.3 and reminds us that we are responsible for the AI tools we use. All true. But there's an assumption buried in those sessions that I've never heard anyone say out loud. We assume the lawyer will notice the ethics problem, and the tool is just along for the ride.

A new paper called TenetBench: A Clause-Anchored Russian Corpus for Evaluating Language Models against Professional Codes of Ethics tests whether the tool might at least notice too. The short answer is that most of them don't.

How the test works

The authors, Yegor Anichkov and colleagues, took fifteen Russian-language professional codes of ethics and built 3,433 requests that conflict with specific clauses. Each one comes with a "benign twin," a nearly identical request where the thing being asked for is legitimate. That pairing is the clever part. It lets you see whether a model actually understands the rule or is just nervous about certain words.

Specialists reviewed the tasks and rated 99.5 percent of the conflicting ones as plausible. The pipeline also generated tasks from two English-language codes from other jurisdictions, which tells me the approach travels.

What they found

They ran fourteen combinations of models and reasoning modes. Eleven of the fourteen produced the conflicting content on most of the requests that breached a code, and most of the time they did it without any warning at all. When a model did warn, nearly three out of four warnings came attached to the problem content anyway. So the model says "be careful" and then hands it over.

The models that did better got there by declining the bad request while still helping with the legitimate twin, which is exactly what you'd want. And here's an odd one: turning on reasoning cut refusals roughly in half for two of the models. So for those two models, more reasoning meant more help with requests that breached a code.

Why a Russian benchmark matters to a Texas law firm

I know what some of you are thinking. These are Russian professional codes. What does that have to do with the Texas Disciplinary Rules?

What carries over is the method. The pipeline builds tasks straight from the text of a code. Somebody could point it at the ABA Model Rules or the Texas rules and get a test set for the legal AI tools we already license. I'd love to see a law school clinic or a bar association take that on. I'd also love to see a vendor do it and publish the numbers.

It also changes how I think about our firm AI policy. Most policies, ours included, put the full burden on the lawyer to spot the problem. That's where the burden belongs under the rules. But this paper tells me the assistant is not going to be much of a backstop, and our training should say that plainly. A junior associate working late on a client request shouldn't assume the tool will raise a flag if they wander somewhere they shouldn't.

Where I'd start

For a CIO or a general counsel, I think there are two practical steps. First, add a professional responsibility scenario or two to any AI tool evaluation. Take a request that obviously conflicts with a rule, like sharing client confidential information with a third party, and see what the tool does. Then try the legitimate version. You'll learn a lot in ten minutes.

Second, bring this into ethics training. The usual message is "verify the output." I'd add "don't expect the tool to tell you when you're crossing a line."

Which leaves the question I keep coming back to: when an AI tool helps a lawyer breach a rule without a word of warning, should the vendor share any of the blame?
